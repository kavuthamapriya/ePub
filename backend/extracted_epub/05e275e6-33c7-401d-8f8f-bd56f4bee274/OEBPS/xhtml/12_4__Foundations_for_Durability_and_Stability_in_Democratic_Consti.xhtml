MARXIST APPROACH ON CONSTITUTION
AND LAW

Marx distinguished between the economic structure of society (the ‘base’ or ‘infrastructure’) and the ‘superstructure’ which rose upon this ‘real foundation’.1 The term ‘superstructure’ is used in three senses: (i) legal and political forms which express existing real relations of production; (ii) forms of consciousness which express a particular class view of the world; and (iii) a process in which individuals become aware of fundamental economic conflicts and fight those out.2

Examining the relationship between Capitalism and Marxism concerning law and constitutional interpretation reveals significant divergence in their perspectives. Marxist ideology interprets law through three fundamental principles: dialectical materialism, the law of production and historical materialism. In contrast, capitalists view the constitution and law as inherently self-sustaining entities, treating concepts like the rule of law, capitalist or bourgeois societal structures and the separation of powers as immutable truths.

Nevertheless, Marxists view law as a tool wielded by the ruling class to oppress the governed. In a class-based society, law serves as a weapon used by the dominant class to subjugate the subordinate class. It does not exist in isolation but functions as a superstructure built upon the economic and social foundations of society, shaped by economic forces. For Marx, while philosophers have interpreted the world, the ultimate duty lies over turning it for a better one. Thus, involving society and understanding law as dynamic are essential corner stones for a successful legal system.

According to Marxist theory, all ideological constructs, including religion, moral philosophy, art, as well as political and legal institutions, are not autonomous entities but rather grounded in the economic and political framework of society. These constructs do not derive from any inherent or eternal notions of law and justice. In Marxist thought, law and the state are inseparable; a state devoid of law is inconceivable. In the earliest stages of societal formation, such as in primitive communist societies, there was an absence of formal legal systems, a characteristic that is anticipated to persist in future communist societies. With the emergence of the concept of personal property, societal classes began to form, leading to the progression through various socioeconomic stages—including slave societies, feudal societies, capitalist societies and socialist societies—all culminating in the eventual establishment of a communist society. In this future communist society, economic contradictions are expected to be resolved, thereby ending the exploitation of individuals by others and rendering the law, as a tool of exploitation, unnecessary.

During the transitional phase from capitalism to socialism and ultimately to communism, following a proletarian-led revolution, the role of law persists as a mechanism for facilitating the transition. Throughout this transitional period, the law serves multiple functions, including the facilitation of the collective ownership of means of production, in doctrination of individuals in communist ideals and the suppression of reactionary movements seeking to revert to previous socioeconomic structures. Therefore, during this transition, the law is viewed as a two-edged tool that is employed to educate the public and quell counterrevolutionary elements. However, as society moves closer to communism, the need for laws as tools of oppression decreases, eventually causing them to become outdated and wither away.

The following three laws of materialist dialectic are: the law of unity and struggle of opposites, law of quantitative to qualitative change and law of negation of negation. The unity and struggle between opposites constitute the fundamental law of materialist dialectic. According to this principle, every object embodies two conflicting aspects, which engage in perpetual conflict, compelled to intermingle for their own sustenance. Both aspects are dynamic and vibrant, often transforming into each other under certain circumstances. For instance, capitalists and the proletariat represent mutually contradictory facets of society. While their interests diverge, they are intricately intertwined internally, forming a unity between opposing elements. The capitalists have to buy labour power for production and the workers have to sell labour power to satisfy their needs, indicating a unity between opposite forces. However, class struggle between them is still fierce as capitalists try to make as much profit as possible while workers want to ensure higher wages. This central conflict clearly shows that these two concepts are often at odds with each other.

When conflicting forces struggle for dominance, a victor may emerge, or a new element may develop, signifiying a change. These changes can be categorized into two types: quantitative and qualitative.

A gradual progression within an object without altering its form or quality constitutes a quantitative change. Conversely, a qualitative change involves a radical transformation in both form and quality, often occurring suddenly. For example, the gradual heating of water illustrates a quantitative change as the water retains its form and properties throughout the process. However, once the temperature reaches 100O Celsius, the water undergoes a qualitative change, boiling and transforming into steam and exhibiting distinct properties and form.

The third principle, the law of the negation of negation, refers to the replacement of one entity by another. While this process may seem to replicate the original entity, it ultimately leads to the emergence of a qualitatively distinct object. This principle applies to societal development as well. The emergence of class society negated primitive communist societies, while future communist societies will negate class societies. Similarly, the evolution from spontaneous materialism to mechanical materialism and subsequently to dialectical materialism, exemplifies the negation of negation. However, in the envisioned classless society of the future, where conflicting classes are absent, this process of negation will cease. It is believed that in such a society, where economic and social structures will be separated, exploitation and class differentiation will disappear, eliminating both rulers and the ruled, as well as exploiters and the exploited.

CONSTITUTION AS A POLITICO-LEGAL
DOCUMENT

Advocates of capitalist democracy regard the law as relatively autonomous but they consider the constitution as a politico-legal document. The constitution encompasses principles and practices that establish the fundamental framework of governance for an organized political community. It directs politics by defining the distribution and utilization of state power. The constitution is both a political and a legal document, as it ensures the freedoms and rights of citizens, legally regulates the government and can be enforced by the courts.

Marxists or socialists perceive the constitution crafted under their leadership as a revolutionary manifesto. Constitutions emerging after socialist revolutions aim to comprehensively overhaul established civil societies based on the principles and achievements of the original social revolution, reflecting the ideologies and impacts inherent in it. Such constitutions are designed to dismantle existing structures entirely and redefine their components. They delineate the rights, roles and responsibilities within the state system, excluding political participation from specific classes (counter-revolutionary classes). In such contexts, ultimate authority over the state resides in the hands of well-organized revolutionary leaders.3

In contrast, when a state system arises from compromise, the constitution is typically formulated through collaboration among stakeholders or a conference of stakeholders. State power is distributed among various entities through the participation of stakeholders under the principles of federalism. In class-based political systems, where two distinct classes coexist, one class views itself as the sole architect of the nation’s fate, assuming the privilege and responsibility of drafting the constitution, which is subsequently conferred by the higher authorities. The role of the other class primarily involves adhering to the provisions of the constitution, where consent is characterized by faith or allegiance to authority. Examples of top-down constitutions given by ruling classes include those proclaimed by monarchs during the medieval period and constitutions prior to the Second People’s Movement.

FOUNDATIONS OF DURABILITY AND STABILITY
OF THE CONSTITUTION

Advocates of capitalist democracy consider the constitution as a sacred document based on democratic constitutionalism and generally do not accept its rewriting. It is due to the circumstances surrounding the capitalist revolution, which recently liberated itself from British imperialism. Thomas Jefferson, one of the architects of the American Constitution, remarked to James Madison in discussion that the Constitution should be rewritten by every generation,4 emphasizing that the deceased should not dictate governance to the living. The US Constitution of 1789 stands as the oldest surviving constitution. Historian Steven Ming writes that the United States has the oldest written national framework of government in the world.5 By the end of the twentieth century, he writes, 159 countries had written constitutions and 101 of those had been in practice since 1970.6 However, America is the only nation that has been governed by the same constitution for two centuries.

On the other hand, in France, around the same time—namely, after the French Revolution in 1789—fifteen constitutions were enacted following the Constitution of the First Republic, issued in 1791. Consequently, among the contemporaneous constitutions, the American constitution has endured for approximately 230 years with only twenty-seven amendments. Through this constitution, the nation, recently liberated from imperialism, was able to evolve into the world’s most powerful country, despite enduring two civil wars and two world wars. On the other hand, France enacted 16 constitutions during the same period of upheaval and considering the average lifespan of these constitutions, it appears that a new constitution was drafted approximately every twenty years. More surprisingly, the French Additional Act of 1815 was enforced for only twenty-one days.

Before discussing the reasons for this, it is relevant to discuss the background of the constitution. The constitution lays down the basic principles governing the state’s operation. As a framework of government, the constitution describes the basic structure of the government, institutions and political process. Additionally, as a protector of rights, it declares certain rights as fundamental or basic rights and outlines measures for their protection within society. Constitutions designed post-revolution or after reform movements incorporate agendas raised during the revolution and the aim to address the people’s expectations during the implementation of the constitution. The success of a constitution is largely determined by its ability to meet the expectations placed upon it.

The durability of the Constitution is not an end in itself. Jefferson contends that the Constitution serves as a remedial measure. Professor Joshua Saylor of Sandra Day O’Connor College of Law argues that the framers of the American Constitution aimed to foster democratic discourse, safeguard against governmental fragmentation and abuse and equitably distribute power among the three branches of government. Post-revolution, they established a government independent from the legislature to embody the desire for a sufficiently robust central authority, thus ensuring the Constitution’s durability. Some scholars attribute the enduring nature of the US Constitution to the wisdom of its framers, who crafted a document featuring a separation and balance of powers, concise language and a judicial activism. Particularly, the judiciary’s role in interpreting the Constitution according to contemporary needs has facilitated its adaptation without formal amendments. The trust and reliance of citizens and other governmental branches on the judiciary play a pivotal role in upholding the longevity of the US Constitution. Martin H. Redish distinguishes the American Constitution as a consensual social agreement, contrasting it with the French Constitution, which is viewed as a subject of contention. As a result, the American Constitution endured, whereas the French Constitution has been subject to frequent changes.

Even in our context, the people’s expectations at the time of drafting the constitution played a pivotal role. Since the people’s movement in 2007 BS was aimed to transfer the authority from the Rana to the King, the public was not particularly focused about the constitution. Indeed, in the royal declaration made by King Tribhuvan on 7 Falgun 2007 BS he proclaimed that ‘. . . today it is our wish and decision that the government of our people should be in accordance with the republican constitution drafted by a Constituency Assembly elected by them. . .’. However, when King Mahendra came to power, he adopted the policy of dividing and ruling the political parties. Until the year 2015 BS, there was no constitution, no elections and the breakdown of democratic power, as the King manipulated the political parties. However, the political leadership at that time believed that everything could be put back on track after the enactment of a parliamentary constitution and the election of the government.

Unfortunately, the elected government did not gain momentum after the King took power. Consequently, the political parties had to struggle for the restoration of the parliamentary system for three decades.

In bourgeois democracy, there is a strong emphasis on political and civil rights and freedoms. This principle was evident in the 2046 BS people’s movement, where both local and foreign bourgeois democratic forces shared the common goal of leading the movement and ensuring it remained free from the influence of leftist ideologies. As a result, the people’s movement was characterized with limited political freedom.

After the 2046 BS people’s movement, the primary ally of the movement, the Samyukta Janamorcha, not only faced complete disregard but was also prohibited. About the constitution, which initially prioritized political and civil freedoms, along with the poor governance of elected government, the Chairman of the Maoists’ affiliate organization, Samyukta Janamorcha, Baburam Bhattarai, handed over a 40-point petition to then Prime Minister Sher Bahadur Deuba. The petition outlined various points, including the abolition of unequal treaties such as the Nepal-India Treaty of 1950 (Point No. 1); ending the dominance of monopoly capital in Nepal’s economy and establishing customs policies to foster a self-sufficient national economy (Point Nos. 6 and 7); drafting a new constitution by elected representatives to establish democracy and end royal privileges (Point Nos. 10 and 11); declaring Nepal a secular state (Point No. 18); ensuring equal rights for daughters to ancestral property (Point No. 19); ending all forms of caste-based exploitation and oppression and establishing ethnicity-based self-governance in areas where a particular ethnic community constitutes a majority (Point No. 20); eradicating discrimination against Dalits should be and completely abolishing the practice of untouchability (Point No. 21); providing equal opportunities and facilities for all language speakers, with arrangements for education in the mother tongue up to the higher secondary level (Point No. 22); strengthening and empowering local bodies (Point No. 26); and providing free and scientific health and education services for all (Point No. 35).7 The neglect of these demands by the government led to a decade-long civil war, known as the People’s War, which ultimately brought an end to the 240-year-old feudal monarchy and paved the way for a federal, republican, inclusive and secular state, leading to the drafting of a new constitution.

Although the Nepali Civil War concluded through an agreement prompted by national and international circumstances, numerous political agendas highlighted the conflict, along with certain economic, social and cultural rights, enshrined as fundamental rights in the constitution. A constitution created in the aftermath of a revolution must reflect the spirit of the movement and lay the groundwork for fulfilling its expectations. Mere words within the constitution cannot automatically meet the people’s aspirations; it is the state’s institutions and their actors that must bring them to life. The most crucial aspect is building trust among citizens that the constitution and its associated institutions are addressing their expectations. Achieving this, fosters hope among the public, although this alone does not guarantee the longevity of the constitution.

PEOPLE-ORIENTED PROVISIONS UNDER THE
NEPALI PREVAILING CONSTITUTION

Nepal underwent seven decades of both large- and small-scale armed conflicts, as well as peaceful protests and demonstrations, to achieve a comprehensive democratic constitution. Following the 2062-3 BS movement, when the Constituent Assembly enacted the new constitution in 2072 BS, it was declared that the era of political struggle had ended and the focus shifted to economic revolution. This indicated that all major political issues had been addressed through the constitution. In essence, the nation transitioned into a secular, federal and inclusive republic, where individuals from all walks of life can aspire to the highest positions in the country. The introduction of federalism has paved the way for equitable development, while the assurance of economic and social rights signifies that further political upheaval is unnecessary.

The current constitution, drafted by the Constituent Assembly, emphasizes its commitment to socialism directly in its preamble. It seeks to eradicate all forms of discrimination and oppression departing from the feudal, despotic, centralized and unitary state system, thereby ensuring social justice. Article 3 of the Constitution defines the nation with multi-ethnic, multilingual, multi-religious, multicultural characteristics, while Article 4 characterizes the state as a secular, inclusive, socialism-oriented and federal democratic republican. These foundational principles hold immense significance and pose a challenge to feudal powers.

In addition to these core provisions, the fundamental right enshrined in Part Three of the constitution—especially the newly introduced rights—highlight the socialist and inclusive character of the constitution. Article 16 affirms that every person has the right to live with dignity, implying that Nepali citizens are entitled to lead a dignified life, secured by basic rights and their effective enforcement. The responsibility of ensuring this lies with the state. Similarly, Article 24 of the Constitution guarantees the right against untouchability and discrimination as a fundamental right. This article stipulates that no person shall be subjected to any form of untouchability or discrimination in any private or public places on grounds of his or her origin, caste, tribe, community, profession, occupation or physical condition. Additionally, there should be no discrimination in the production or distribution of goods, services or facilities. It is universally recognized as a fundamental human right that individuals should not face discrimination based on factors such as gender, ethnic origin, colour, political beliefs, religious affiliations or sexual orientation.

Likewise, Article 32 provides for the right to language and culture, Article 33 guarantees the right to employment, Article 34 enshrines the right to labour, Article 35 ensures the right to health, Article 36 establishes the right to food sovereignty, Article 37 secures the right to shelter and Article 38 safeguards the rights of women. These rights represent the collective entitlements of the people. It seems that many of the demands outlined in the petition presented by the Samyukta Janamorcha prior to the enactment of the constitution have been incorporated into it.

Article 42 of the constitution outlines the right to social justice, ensuring the inclusion of socially backward classes, including women, in state bodies based on inclusive principles. Additionally, Article 42(2) mandates that indigent citizens and citizens and communities on the verge of extinction shall have the right to get special opportunities and benefits in education, health, housing, employment, food and social security for their protection, upliftment, empowerment and development.

Moreover, the guiding principles of the state in Part Four of the Constitution also reflects its socialist orientation. Due to space constraints, a detailed discussion of these principles is not feasible here.

EXAMINING CONSTITUTIONAL DURABILITY
AND STABILITY: KEY INQUIRIES

Concerns about the Constitution’s durability and stability have occasionally arisen in the seven years following its enactment. While dissatisfaction was widespread at the time of its adoption, the intensity of criticism has since diminished. Initially, certain political factions denounced the constitution as flawed, but with time, many of these groups have come to accept it in practice. In addition to participating in electoral processes under the constitution, these factions have also engaged in government formation, thereby tacitly acknowledging its provisions. What began as demands for a complete rewrite of the constitution has gradually transitioned into calls for constitutional amendments. This shift signifies a growing acceptance and adoption of the constitution. It is essential to view such demands for amendment positively, recognizing that no constitution can be immutable. To ensure its relevance and efficacy, periodic amendments and developments through formal procedures, judicial interpretations and practical applications are imperative.

In recent times, concerns regarding the stability of the constitution have emerged. These concerns do not reflect the rejection of the constitution itself but rather echoes the concern over the individuals entrusted with the responsibility of governing the state within its framework and those who have taken solemn oaths to safeguard its integrity. Notably, frequent dissolution of the House of Representatives through mechanisms not sanctioned by the constitution, reluctance to form a government despite enjoying majority support from parliamentarians and reluctance to endorse bills passed by both houses, particularly when additional questions not included in the original text of bill are raised, have reignited doubts about the constitution’s stability.

The first condition for the constitution to endure is for everyone to embrace its provisions, which entails internal acceptance. In practice, the effectiveness of the constitution is demonstrated by the exercise of rights and adherence to duties outlined within it. Distinguished jurist H.L.A. Hart suggests that for a constitution or any law to be effective, it must achieve a minimum level of effectiveness, especially in a developed society where it should be embraced and adhered to by state officials as a rule governing their formal conduct. This is also referred to as constitutional culture, which entails understanding and interpreting the constitution’s provisions. Unfortunately, we have yet to cultivate a strong constitutional culture. There is a tendency to interpret the constitution’s provisions subjectively, which poses challenges to its implementation and sustainability. This factor also contributed to the failure of the 2047 BS constitution.

It can be observed that even political factions which initially expressed extreme dissatisfaction when the constitution was promulgated, have subsequently participated in elections conducted in accordance with its provisions and have agreed to amend the constitution without revising it entirely. This suggests that discontent at the civil level is gradually dissipating. However, in such a scenario, it is gravely concerning that the responsible stakeholders of the state, who are entrusted with the guardianship of the constitution, has exceeded its constitutional limits. Given that the constitution itself grants the Supreme Court the authority to determine the constitutionality of bills and invalidate them if found to be unconstitutional, attempts made to nullify this right to interpret the constitution will be questioned for a long time.

The second vital aspect for ensuring the longevity of the constitution is fostering a sense of ownership among the populace. It is imperative for everyone to feel connected to the constitution. This sense of ownership can be nurtured through active participation in its creation and by addressing grievances through amendments. To ensure the widespread acceptance of the constitution, it is crucial to promptly address emerging issues since its promulgation. This serves as a safety mechanism for the constitution. The 2047 constitution retained certain provisions as basic principles while allowing for amendments on other matters. However, the failure to utilize this safety mechanism to address immediate grievances through amendments led to the downfall of the constitution. During discussions about amending the constitution, there were objections from the establishment side against altering trivial matters. Nonetheless, the constitution was repealed without any attempt at amendment.

The development of the constitution takes place through its application, court interpretation and through formal amendments. The things that are not in the constitution or the provisions that are not clear can be developed through good practice, which can also be referred to as organic development. To alleviate dissatisfaction with the current constitution, an acceptable amendment draft can be prepared through thorough discussion and consultation, addressing concerns through constitutional amendment. This could be a significant step toward ensuring the stability of the constitution.

In our context, the third thing necessary for the stability of the constitution is the actual projection of rights. Although the constitution has adopted a federal model, the trend of centralization of rights has not improved. It is necessary for everyone to understand that all three levels of government are under the rule of the constitution and are independent in their areas and should function accordingly. Our political leadership has not internalized the function of provincial government and local bodies as that of government.

The fourth condition for the longevity constitution is the democratization of institutions. Democracy demands participation, discussion and transparency in policymaking. The more the state institutions are democratic, the people’s trust in democracy increases. This helps in the longevity of the democratic constitution.

The fifth important condition is good governance and development. People want to improve their standard of living. Moreover, democracy is for advanced and prosperous life. The constitution is not an end but a means to achieve a high and prosperous life through the democratic process. No matter how good the constitution is, if prosperity is not achieved, the people will not be satisfied. Therefore, we should be able to make the people optimistic that this constitution is ours, the government is for us and the government works for our welfare.

The sixth condition is the actual participation of the people. The current elections are only formal and syndicate based. Elections are expensive. There have been instances where some good politicians have hesitated to contest the election. It is necessary to improve the electoral system and make it less expensive. As a result, people’s participation will increase, people’s democracy will be established and the people themselves will protect and own the democratic constitution.

The seventh condition is the democratization of political parties. Political parties are the cornerstone of democracy. Apparently, there is lack of internal democracy in political parties. The political parties operate with a bureaucratic approach that is subject to the whims of certain leaders. To increase people’s faith in democracy, parties themselves should adopt democratic practices. Enhanced internal democratic practices strengthen democracy, foster the growth of democratic culture and consequently heighten public appeal.

The eighth thing is to develop a sense of responsibility. Democracy is a system of public accountability. If the people feel that the representatives sent by them are sensitive and responsible towards them, they will believe in political parties, democracy and the constitution.

The ninth thing is to implement the inclusiveness adopted by the constitution in practice. At present, inclusivity is not given attention in state agencies, political parties or election candidates. The people’s faith in the constitution cannot be maintained when the expectations that led to participation in the democratic movement are not fulfilled.

The tenth and last condition is the practical implementation of various economic, social and cultural rights guaranteed by the constitution. The practical implementation of important economic, social and cultural rights such as the right to live in a clean environment, the right to education, the right to language and religion, the right to food, the right to housing, the right to social security, the right to health and the right to consumers is essential for the perpetuation of the constitution.

However, the situation is different in the twentieth century. Constitutions enacted after the revolutions largely resembles the revolutionary manifestos, often structured upon the ideals and accomplishments of original social revolutions, aiming to comprehensively reshape established civil societies. These therefore aim at completely dismantling previous structures and redefining them with a view of integrating previously excluded groups into the country’s governance. Even though they are considered as instruments of change, sometimes they show a society-state connection that is far from reality hence calling for reforms whenever there is a wide difference between fiction and truth. As such, amendments or changes have been sought to build a new political order through constitutionality. However, if the power structure of the state cannot be fully reshaped then it becomes difficult to implement such constitutions practically. The effective implementation of constitutional provisions necessitates cooperation from all institutions within the state structure, including the courts, military, police and other constitutional and administrative bodies. Without such cooperation, the realization of constitutional provisions becomes unattainable. Currently, we are experiencing a similar situation.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the constitution stands as the cornerstone of the political landscape, which is rightly recognized as a political-legal document. Our prevailing constitution reflects a compromise between established and emerging powers, akin to a contractual agreement where rights and responsibilities are distributed among all stakeholders. Upholding the longevity of our democratic constitution requires firm commitment from all the stakeholders to adhere to its principles. This emphasizes the importance of fostering a democratic culture among political actors, guiding their actions to align with constitutional provisions. Furthermore, embracing judicial interpretations is crucial for ensuring the resilience of our democratic system in the long run.
