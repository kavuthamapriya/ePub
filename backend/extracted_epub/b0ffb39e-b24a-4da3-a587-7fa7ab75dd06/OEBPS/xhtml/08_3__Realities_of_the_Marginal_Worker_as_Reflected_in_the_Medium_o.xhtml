CHAPTER 3

Realities of the Marginal Worker as
Reflected in the Medium of Cinema

M.M. REHMAN AND KANTA REHMAN

INTRODUCTION

Cinema has become a very powerful medium to study, examine and reflect our realities of existence. Ever since its appearance in India, the medium has been used to entertain, educate and disseminate information in a dramatic form and in a most effective manner. Dadasaheb Phalke who learned the craft in England, made the first film in 1913, titled Raja Harishchandra. And since then, the medium has been endlessly essaying in dramatic form many aspects of society in the country. In India, the social realities are very complex. The complexity has become very complicated over the centuries/ millennia. The apparent social structure could be examined from two terms of reference among others, rather simplistically. The terms are ‘the advantaged’ and ‘the disadvantaged groups’. Here in this essay, an attempt has been made to study and examine how the literature of cinema has taken the issues regarding the disadvantaged sections of the society in India. Here, the disadvantaged sections of Indian society are termed as marginals, to examine realities as reflected in some motion pictures produced over the decades.

Initially, cinema was a medium of entertainment. But gradually it became a powerful analytical tool for examining and reflecting the conditions of the marginal of the society. The marginal are identified as the ‘Dalits’, the sex workers, the disadvantaged workers like the agricultural labourers, the distressed marginal farmers, the slum dwellers, the street vendors, small artists and many others. The Indian cinematic literature has been studying and examining the conditions of these sections over the decades and the medium is trying to also send messages that the emerging society looks forward to. In this chapter, an attempt is made to see this aspect.

This chapter provides an overview of the cinema industry and examines how some films reflect and narrate the realities of the existence of some marginalized groups in Indian society.

HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE

It was 132 years ago, i.e., on 7 July 1892, the first film show was arranged by the agents of two French brothers, Louis and Auguste Lumière, at Watson Hotel in Bombay.1 The show was hailed as the marvel of the century. Another landmark was the construction of the first cinema hall in India in Calcutta in 1907. The hall was known as Elphinstone Picture Palace built by J.F. Madan. Cinema was born abroad i.e., in England. The technique of film production was not known to the Indians initially. It was Dadasaheb Phalke who was the first foreign returned cine-technician. Dadasaheb learned the craft of filmmaking from Cecil Hepworth who was the prominent producer at Walton in England, in 1912. However, the turning event took place in 1912. It was in this year that the first film titled Raja Harishchandra was produced by Dadasaheb Phalke. This 3,700 feet length film was released on 3 May 1913, at the coronation cinematography in Bombay (now Mumbai). From then on, Bombay (henceforth Mumbai) nurtured and maintained by a veteran and pioneer like Dadasaheb Phalke, has occupied an important place in the industry. It is the first city where the film was produced and has remained as the dreamland of show business.

Beginning with a handful of people, today the ‘industry’ employs several lakh people. The industry is now spread over several states such as Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, Karnataka, Gujarat, Odisha, Kerala, Assam, Manipur, etc.

EARLIER MILIEU

Filmmaking began in India when the country was under the colonial regime. It was also a period when the freedom movement was beginning to peak. The country was witnessing an upsurge of expectations for freedom from the British Empire. The situation was, therefore, quite volatile. The empire was very cautious about allowing people to express their thoughts either through print or audiovisual mediums. Censorship was quite strict. The situation was aptly described by V. Shantaram. He writes:

In the old days, the Censors were more concerned with politics than morality. In 1930, I had to change the title of a film from Swarajya to Uday Kal to satisfy the foreign censors. With the advent of freedom, there was a natural shift towards puritanism. In their zeal to preserve our ancient traditions and culture, the censors became increasingly puritanical and moralistic in their outlook. However, one expected that, with time, the zeal would die away, just as the prejudices against the British have died away’ (Shantaram: 1963).

Shantaram, however, expressed his dismay, that even after the independence, the situation had not improved. He said,

But censors have not been able to keep pace with the changing social conditions and manners. I, for one, would not plead for a license to show anything obscene or vulgar: but, at the same time, I would call upon the censors to be more rational and uniform in judging films (ibid., p. 110).

This indicates that there were and still are obstacles to filming subjects that may not be palatable to the existing power structure, and the reasons may be many. Therefore, subjects to be picturized have to adapt to the rules of the rulers. This is because ideas never die. In a segregated and differentiated socio-economic and political system, the call for justice and equality might be disconcerting to the advantaged groups and their power instrument. This is one side of the medium cinema and its expression from the supply angle. There is another side, i.e., the demand side. On the demand side is the audience. The audience decides which film should be viewed and which one not. And the members of the audience are free in their choice. No one has any control over them. It is their money that helps the film industry to survive. As a result, filmmaking is a risky business; and investment in filmmaking is very huge even for a small film. Hence, the producers and the directors have to keep the choices of the audience in view. This way the suppliers are constrained. They will have to create a situation: you win, I win. However, it is not an easy choice. Mehboob Khan says very clearly while raising many problems of the industry:

Public taste influences the quality of films in as much as the producers, while making a film, is guided by the thought of making a commercially successful film—a film which will appeal to the majority of picturegoers. Most of the patrons of Indian films consist of lower-middle-class people and the labour masses who want song and dance and glamour and thrills and laughter and tears and melodrama, all in one film. And this line of entertainment is an ancient tradition with Indian masses… (Mehboob, 1963).

These are the pulls and pressures from both supply and demand sides that determine the type of stories to be turned into cinemas, because filmmaking is both a risky and costly business. Scores of people, like artists, technicians, directors, and a host of others like dress designers, and hairdressers are involved in making a film. Thus, the failure of a film means a heavy loss to the producers. There also have been moral concerns, i.e., cinema’s influence on the ethos and ethics of society. Cinema has a powerful influence on people. Hence, the topics and storylines are expected, to a great extent, to have ‘special approval’. This has been very clearly expressed by one of India’s former Presidents, Dr Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan. He says:

It is essential for all those connected with film production, whether they are directors, producers or actors and actresses, that they should resolve that they will not use their knowledge, their skill, their imaginative power, their artistic ability for any ends other than justice, freedom, peace and dignity; and they will not merely cater to the ruthless intent of certain individuals, who, in their anxiety to make profits, tend to corrupt human nature, degrade human beings and lower their tastes. That is a danger that we should like to avoid (Radhakrishnan: 1963).

All this has influenced filmmaking—the risks, profits, social situations, political structure and finally people’s expectations. This is brilliantly illustrated by Ingmar Bergman: ‘The creator of the film involved as a means of expression which concerns not only himself but also millions of other people, and more than not he feels the same desire as other artists: ‘I want to succeed today. I want to be a celebrity now. I want to please, to “delight, to move people immediately”’.

Midway between this desire and its realization is found the public, who demands but one thing of the film: ‘I’ve paid, I want to be distracted, swept off my feet, involved; I want to forget my troubles, my family, my work, I want to get away from myself. Here I am, seated in the darkness, and like a woman about to give birth, I want deliverance.’

The filmmaker who is aware of these demands and who lives on the money of the public is placed in a situation which is difficult, and which creates obligations for him (1998) (quoted in Chanana: 1998).

The limitations of expressing through cinema are much more complex than any other medium. The writer of books, articles or stories need not invest so much money in the process of expressing her/his ideas. But cinema producers cannot take unlimited liberty because of money, profit and social and political ‘permission’ both ‘covert and overt’.

Films are a form of literature. It is audiovisual literature that depicts any phenomenon in the most visually accurate manner. It can depict and describe any subject—physical and emotional. As we have noted in the beginning, in India the major focus of filmmaking was stories and ideas available in our written literature. And it was initiated with Raja Harishchandra, by Dadasaheb Phalke, the father of Indian Cinema.

However, after a long time, since the beginning of filmmaking in India, the issues and problems of workers, their workplace relations, organization and struggle began to get attention from some filmmakers both in the mainstream Hindi Cinema and in regional cinemas.

STUDY OF SELECTED HINDI CINEMA

Here an attempt has been made to examine some films produced in the medium of Hindi. The purpose is to see, to what extent, the films narrated the existing conditions and what message they attempted to deliver by the directors and producers and to the audience.

Damul

One of the most exploited segments of workers in India is constituted by the bonded labourers. They existed in the far and long past; they still exist in thousands and they will continue to exist in future as well. In 1968, an act was passed to abolish the bonded labour system. But it has been so pernicious and resilient, that incidences of their exploitation is quite common. To highlight their plight, filmmaker Prakash Jha made the film. It was released in 1985. Helmed by talented actors like Deepti Naval, Annu Kapoor, Manohar Singh and others, the film narrated precarious and exploitative situations in which the bonded system is perpetuated. The important points which emerge from the narrative are that a sectarian social system, skewed distribution of land and uneven participation as owner and owner are responsible for the existence of the system of bonded labour. The bonded labourers, being the most marginalized, do not have much access to any institution for the redressal of their problems. They are unorganized and unregulated. The film narrates the plight and suffering of the bonded labourers in a very touching manner along with unravelling the complexity of socio-economic system. However, the film does not provide any way to escape the system. Intervention by some worker’s organizations would have indicated that an attempt could be made to change the situation. The film describes a situation as it is along with many issues that contribute to the existence of bonded labour in India today.

Paar

The film Paar (Crossing) was made by Gautam Ghosh which depicted the cruel existence of a migrant couple in Calcutta (Kolkata). Helmed by Shabana Azmi and Nasiruddin Shah, the film narrates the difficulties faced by migrant workers who belong to the socio-economically disadvantaged groups in society. And being highly disadvantaged, often, they accept the most difficult, dirty and dangerous/works/jobs (Shabana: 2012). This is amply illustrated in the film. Once again, the film does not show us any sign of redemption. A flicker of hope should have a great idea to be thrown into the narrative.

Coolie

One of the most unprotective groups of workers is constituted by the coolies (porter). They are engaged in carrting luggage of travelers. More often they are treated very badly by people who use their services. The film coolie depicts the realities of the lives of coolies who could be found in many large railway stations in India. The director narrates the living and working conditions of coolies and shows their struggles and achievements. The film finally tries to bring the socio-cultural milieu into perspective. The film starred Amitabh Bachchan, Kader Khan and others. It was directed by Manmohan Desai.

Kaala Patthar

This film was produced and directed by Yash Chopra and released in 1979. The film story is based on the tragic incident of an accident that took place in Chasnala in Dhanbad. Yash Chopra has tried to highlight the sufferings of the workers, engaged in coal mines and makes a point about how management is indifferent to workers’ sufferings. This film starred Amitabh Bachchan and Rakhee.

Naxal

The film was directed and produced by K. Abbas and starred Mithun Chakraborty, Smita Patil and others. Abbas has tried to depict the problems and issues that led to the birth of the Naxalite movement. He highlighted the dynamics of a socio-economic system that was exploitative which led to rebellion. However, the film does not provide us with any trajectory which can help to change the socio-economic system.

Mirch Masala

The film was helmed by Naseeruddin Shah, Smita Patil and others. It was directed by Ketan Mehta. The film picturizes the oppression of poor women and their efforts to organize themselves to protect their honour and dignity. This film gives an idea of women’s organizations and the technique for thwarting harassment by privileged people.

Importance of the medium, films unlike books could be read by anybody, literate and illiterate alike. It is a powerful means to disseminate information, either for simple knowledge or for entertainment and education which includes skills and ideas. Its impacts are immediate, and get internalized by many. It may encourage, may disappoint, may incite, negatively and positively. Thus, a filmmaker is constrained by factors like who are producing a film, either for profit only or for sending a social message. The power of films has been aptly noted by India’s first President Dr Rajendra Prasad: ‘As a medium of education, films have come to be looked upon as a vital factor the world over. In the more advanced countries of the West, films were being used for spreading education. I am sure we too, in India will be making the fullest use of films in educating our people’ (Adarsh: 1963).

During the last several decades, we have witnessed several controversies and conflicts around several films, mostly related to films with historical backgrounds or films with a religious perspective. The controversy around the film Padmavat and earlier around the Naxalite are an example of constraints that the filmmakers have to take into consideration. People’s choices and dignity as social and economic groups have to be respectfully protected.

Workers, especially the marginal segments, are exploited, oppressed and unorganized and sometimes disorganized. To articulate and express their situations through film narrative scientifically, one requires proper terms of reference and contexts. However, this is not an easy task. The most powerful organization in human society is the government/state. It perpetuates more often the dominant power and to maintain its power, it perpetuates the exploitative socio-economic and political systems. This conflict is inherent and unavoidable in the explanation and presentation of an exploitative situation. The state power and its beneficiaries more often than not do like the truth of inequality and injustice to be narrated in films because films may tilt their boats.

Because of these constraints, not many feature films are produced in the country depicting the process of exploitation. Nevertheless, many attempts have been made by documentary filmmakers to narrate the plights and problems of marginal workers in the world in general and India in particular. As we have noted, cinema is powerful literature. Human and environmental situations are continuously narrated in a more visually realistic manner which influences human minds very effectively.

CONCLUSION

From the beginning of the making of films, the subjects of marginal workers were not in focus of the film literature. The initial filmmaking in India revolved around history and mythology. In the second wave, the filmmaking was more focused on social and personal relations and their complexities. Of course, the plot, often, highlighted the romantic nature of human lives and also narrated socio-economic conflicts in a socio-political situation. For example, the film Naya Daur illustrated the conflict between the use of automation and non-automated sources of livelihood. Many mainstream Hindi films also depicted the realities of farmers, agricultural labourers, migrant workers, vendors and others. However, little attempt has been made to picture the process of struggles of the marginal workers, the development of their organization and eventually and their salvation from their poverty. The films dealing with marginal workers portrayed them, i.e., the marginal workers as the victims, victimized by others. But the extent of victimization by their ignorance and efforts of their potential have not been adequately narrated in our films. Maybe this is an area in which films can make their foray.
