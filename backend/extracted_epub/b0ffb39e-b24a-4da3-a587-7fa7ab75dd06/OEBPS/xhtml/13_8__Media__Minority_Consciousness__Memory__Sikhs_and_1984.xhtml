CHAPTER 8

Media, Minority Consciousness, Memory
Sikhs and 1984*

SHRUTI DEVGAN

Before the process of alienation was set in motion Sikhs never considered themselves a minority. They took for granted that they were equal citizens in a pluralistic society, with equal rights and equal responsibilities. The fact that they are a small numerical minority, only two per cent of the population, has, literally, been pushed down their throats. If the fact of being a numerical minority automatically carries the corollary of second-class citizenship, can any minority feel it has an equal stake in the country? And once the stake is in doubt, can the commitment to the nation be unconditional and complete? (Harji Malik: 1985, 61-2).

This excerpt represents and encapsulates the widespread Sikh sentiment in the aftermath of the anti-Sikh violence of 1984. On 4 June, the most sacred of all Sikh shrines, the Golden Temple or Darbar Sahib, was seized by the Indian army under state auspices, killing the controversial Sikh leader Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale and his followers along with thousands of innocent Sikh pilgrims. This attack on the Darbar Sahib was followed by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s assassination on 31 October by her two Sikh bodyguards. This became the pretext for ruthless massacre of Sikhs in the Indian capital city, New Delhi, and other parts of north India. This violence continued unabated and with state sanction until 4 November 1984 even though in popular imagination it is remembered as a sudden outbreak or ‘riots’. Since then, ‘1984’ has become a certain shorthand, symbolizing the tensions between the community and state as well as state-inflicted civilian atrocities and state abuse in Punjab.

In the excerpt above Harji Malik, a Sikh columnist conveys the collective angst of the community post-1984. Despite strains between the central government and Sikh leadership before the 1980s, the community at large had assumed their taken-for-granted status in India. After the events of 1984, however, and the distorted imagery and language manufactured and disseminated by the mass media all Sikhs became stigmatized as the ‘other’ and suspected ‘terrorists’. The state and state-sponsored media not only created and facilitated conditions for a process of misrepresenting the Sikh community but also neglected the role of the central government in brewing tensions that would prove lethal for Sikhs. As Malik points out, there was no natural association between Sikhs’ numerical minority status and minority consciousness before the events of 1984. After the events of 1984, however, the Sikh community was made to feel they occupied a precarious and unstable place in what had so far been perceived as a relatively secure context.

The consequence of these state-created representations is that Sikh memories of 1984 and the community’s interpretations of 1984 events have not found much space in the Indian ‘public sphere’1 (Habermas: 1989; Calhoun: 1992). The Indian state and mass media either continue to reproduce and perpetuate impoverished and one-sided images of Sikhs that first came into circulation in the 1980s or suppress and neglect these memories. An emergence of minority consciousness (Gupta: 1985) that started unfolding in 1984 continues today, albeit veiled in ‘normalcy’ in popular imagination (see e.g. Kaur: 2013). Official memories of 1984 are deliberately frozen and static and these in turn inform popular memory. Dominant memory in India still considers 1984 as an aberration in which the Sikh community at large was responsible. The invasion of the Darbar Sahib in 1984 is justified as necessary and benevolent for upholding national interest and the systematic massacre of October-November are dismissed as ‘riots’ or spontaneous and ‘serendipitous’ (Ghassem-Fachandi: 2012, 9) outbursts. The complexity of the situation in Punjab and labels created by the state and media have not received critical attention with a few scholarly exceptions (see e.g., Major: 1987; Gupta: 1996). Militant counternarratives gained ground in the immediate aftermath of 1984. The state had vested interests in exaggerating these voices and representing them as purely destructive despite the necessity of understanding the complexity of the situation and motivations behind this movement (see Mahmood: 1996). While dominant memories of 1984 are constructed and monopolized by the Indian state, there is an emerging set of diasporic voices counteracting these hegemonic accounts. A section of diaspora Sikhs are documenting and recording experiences of marginalization that they encountered in 1984 and that continue to inform Sikh identity today. The segment of Sikhs engaged in an active process of recreating memories in public spaces informed by the present is doing ‘memory work’ (Haug: 1987; Kuhn: 1995; Sa’adi and Abu-Lughod: 2007). Diasporic memory work is intergenerational. In addition to first generation Sikh immigrants who are direct migrants from India, their children or second generation with ‘postmemories’ or ‘delayed, indirect, secondary’ (Hirsch: 1997, 13) memories of 1984 are actively engaged in representing and reinterpreting 1984 and Sikh identity. Sikhs are using both their diasporic locations as well as availability of digital media to practice memory work.

I will examine media representations of Sikhs in the 1980s and their impact on Sikh identity. I focus mostly on media depictions of events leading up to 1984 and specifically the attack on the Darbar Sahib and Sikh massacres following Indira Gandhi’s assassination. There is a severe paucity of media accounts from this period, because of the large-scale media censorship that was in effect (Singh: 1984; Major: 1987). It is important to remember that state assaults and media manufactured distortions about civilian Sikhs carried beyond that year, even though an analysis of these texts lies outside the scope of this article (see Kumar: 2008 for an examination of media misreporting of human rights abuses in the aftermath of 1984). While mainstream or legacy media such as print, television and radio were strongly influenced by the state in their depiction of events and imagery about the community, there is a shift underway because of transformations in media of communication. As I noted above the Sikh diaspora is playing a key role in initiating these shifts and using digital media as a tool towards this end.

This chapter will address the following questions: What were some representations that Indian mass media constructed and popularized? How did these texts create a process of Sikh marginalization? Why are these representations being contested most emphatically in the diaspora and what are some components of new narratives that diasporic Sikhs are creating via digital technology? I draw on a combination of methods including content analysis of print media,2 radio and television reports, commentaries from journalists and scholars, narrative analysis of 1984 websites as well as personal interviews with Sikhs in North America, United States and Canada. I am glossing over some minute details in this chapter to provide a critical appraisal of media imagery while highlighting the hitherto neglected Sikh point of view.

Legacy media institutionalized feelings of being a minority among Sikhs by producing hegemonic and unchanging representations and memories of 1984. As an antidote to these dominant texts and memories, digital media with its horizontal and diffuse structure is ushering in a shift in imagery. Even though all representations, memories and narratives are inherently selective and incomplete, digital media is making available tools and spaces to piece together imagery and texts that have been suppressed so far. Memory work in and through digital media is proving important in fracturing dominant memories and representations. Diasporic memory work is laying bare the process by which the Sikh community became subordinate by managing and presenting their own narratives, and consequently restoring ‘symmetry of the communication process’ (Goffman: 1959, 8). In the process of creating counter memories to dominant narratives of 1984, a section of diasporic Sikhs engaged in memory work are both drawing on community values and rejuvenating community identity.

MEDIA, REPRESENTATIONS AND MEMORY

Media is not merely a reflection of reality, but it also creates reality (Tuchman: 1978; Schudson: 2011). Despite the veneer of objectivity and value-neutrality, news reports are ‘narratives’ or ‘stories’ that are socially constructed and distilled through a ‘process of selecting, highlighting, framing, shading, and shaping’ (Schudson: 2011, xiv). Mediated images must therefore be understood not as pristine and verbatim accounts of the social world but as ‘representations’. Representation in turn means the process through which people assign meanings to ‘objects, people, events, in the world’ (Hall: 1997, 61). Representation as a process of meaning making is contingent on questions of power (Foucault: 1980). Who assigns meanings to certain groups and events? Why are only some meanings selected to the neglect of others? These questions are important to understand representations, popular texts and imagery. These questions are also critical to understand memory because ‘journalism … is also the first draft of memory’ (Garde-Hansen: 2011, 5). McLuhan ([1964] 1994) advanced that media are extensions of memory. The need to remember and share is profound and keen but human faculties are limited. These limitations were the impetus to create various media forms and practices. However, memories must also be understood as narratives or partial and incomplete interpretations of past based on present time and place.

According to sociologist Maurice Halbwachs, collective memory is a project necessarily situated in the present. Our memories are a product of our belonging to social groups in certain spatial locations based on the present moment. These memories are in turn a significant component of our shared identity. In a similar vein, Hirsch and Smith define cultural memory as ‘an act in the present by which individuals and groups constitute their identities by recalling a shared past on the basis of common, and therefore often contested norms, conventions, and practices’ (2002, 5).

Media is an important vehicle for the production and dissemination of memory. Even within the broad canopy of media, there is a distinction between mainstream or legacy media and digital media. Legacy media produces news stories vertically by relying on ‘journalistic authority’ (Zelizer: 1992). Legacy media also follow certain routines and norms necessary for ‘professional, legacy news making’ (Ostertag and Ortiz: 2013, 5). On the other hand, digital media is free of reliance on journalistic authority. Digital communication and information generation is more horizontal and diffuse. This is because digital platforms such as online discussion forums, columns and blogs are sites for both production as well as consumption of narratives and ‘prosumption’ (Recuber: 2012). The process by which memories are produced and shared are then qualitatively different in these different kinds of media. While legacy media often create memories top-down, digital media are bringing about a democratization of memory as increasing number of people gain access to media through which to communicate, share and store their memories publicly (Garde-Hansen et al.: 2009, 8-19). This is not to say that digital media is free of bias and immune to issues of surveillance and scrutiny,3 but in comparison to mainstream media, digital media are proving indispensable in doing memory work or a deliberate and public reconstruction of pasts based on present contexts.

1984: An Overview

The attack on the Darbar Sahib and the massacre of Sikhs following Indira Gandhi’s assassination was the culmination of at least a decade of strained politics between the state and Sikhs. Until the partition of the Indian subcontinent in 1947 into Hindu-majority India and Muslim predominant Pakistan, despite some simmering discontentment among Sikhs, they formed an alliance with Hindus against Muslim Pakistan (Gupta: 1996). At the time of the partition, Punjab was split into Indian and Pakistani sides, but Sikhs who had continued to stay in India did not receive separate territories because of their small numbers.

Many of the problems that came to headway in 1984 started with peaceful protests led by the Sikh political party the Akali Dal and centred on the document known as the Anandpur Sahib Resolution. The principal demands in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution included: first, the demand for Chandigarh as the capital of Punjab alone rather than both Punjab and Haryana; second, the demand that the sharing of river water between the adjoining states of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan be adjudicated by the Supreme Court; and finally, territorial disputes between Punjab and its adjoining province of Haryana be settled by an independent tribunal. These demands along with the lack of Congress (I) leadership in Punjab made the state uncomfortabl.4 This led to an increase of Congress (I) led state interference in the region’s workings.

The lack of success of non-violent protests for regional autonomy yielded the rise of a charismatic Sikh leader, Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale, of small farmer background and who had acquired great popularity as a preacher. His role in interpreting the document and wreaking violence in Punjab is disputed within the community as well as between Sikh scholars and commentators (see e.g., Nayar and Singh: 1984; Sandhu: n.d.). As tensions kept unfolding tensions between the state and Bhindranwale, the latter shifted to the Darbar Sahib complex in 1983. The state under the leadership of Indira Gandhi repressed Sikh voices carried in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution by declaring President’s Rule in Punjab in the same year, dismissing the elected Punjab government and replacing it with her own staff.

On 4 June 1984, the Indian army occupied the Darbar Sahib under the pretext of ‘flushing out terrorists’ and ‘recovering arms’ under a code name, Operation Bluestar (White Paper on the Punjab Agitation 1984). The army occupation killed at least 493 innocent Sikh pilgrims and Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale and his followers. This is only according to official accounts contained in the Indian state issued White Paper. Civil organizations like Movement against State Repression have estimated the number of dead to be over 10,000. The attack on the Darbar Sahib and other gurudwaras or Sikh temples was followed by targeted violence against Sikhs from the evening of 31 October through 4 November 1984. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s two Sikh bodyguards assassinated her on 31 October 1984. In the immediate aftermath of Indira Gandhi’s assassination, violence broke out against Sikhs killing more than 3,000 Sikhs in Delhi and several hundred outside Delhi. This was a systematic targeting of Sikhs and included organized ‘slaughter and rape that accompanied looting, arson and burning’ (Kishwar: 1984, 10). Voters’ lists were used to mark Sikh houses and businesses. Poor Sikhs suffered more than middle- or upper-class Sikhs even though all Sikhs were made targets. The famous and controversial writer Khushwant Singh noted at the time that he felt like a Jew in Nazi Germany (1992, 93). He did get refuge in the Swedish embassy, however, unthinkable for Sikhs who faced the brunt of violence. Public buses and trains were used to transfer paid mobs. Mobs were given money and liquor as remuneration to set Sikhs on fire. Sikh men were dragged out of their houses often by their hair or kes5 and burnt and women were raped (Who are the Guilty? [1984] 2003). In official accounts of the time and in memories continuing today, these events were/are dismissed as ‘riots’ or spontaneous and accidental emotional outbursts of Hindus against Sikhs.

Representations, Minority Consciousness and 1984
Pre-1984

Strains between the Indian state and Sikh community were portrayed and refracted selectively by the Indian mass media including national newspapers like The Times of India, The Hindustan Times, Doordarshan and All India Radio. Media was owned and controlled by the state (see e.g., Ohm: 1999) precluding even an attempt at an accurate depiction of events. Even before the invasion of the Darbar Sahib in June, and Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s assassination in October 1984, the state started manufacturing feelings of alienation among Sikhs at large.

The central government had started creating an antagonistic climate for Sikhs by using several tactics especially between the years 1980-4. These measures included: (a) denying the Sikh political party, the Akali Dal’s demand for greater regional autonomy contained in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution; (b) portraying the Anandpur Sahib Resolution as a secessionist document; and (c) using the state-controlled mass media as a mouthpiece to circulate their one-sided representations that held Sikhs accountable for the events of 1984.

The Akali Dal and its negotiations with the central government were continually cast in a negative and disruptive light by the state and media. Media reports focused on the threat that Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale’s violent measures posed but did not cover the manipulations of the Congress (I) and its political leadership (see e.g., Nayar and Singh: 1985). The Indian state used the document put forward by the Akali leadership, the Anandpur Sahib Resolution as a tool to further their political ambitions. The three main demands contained in the document (the demand for Chandigarh; river water distribution and river water rights; and deciding territorial disputes by an independent tribunal) were ‘straightforward regional and secular issues’. The very transparency of Akali Dal’s demands and the religious composition of the Akali regional party gave the Congress (I) a reason to ‘communalize’ demands contained in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution and created fear of Sikh separatism among Hindus (Gupta: 1985, 218-9).

The ‘non-reading of the Anandpur Sahib Resolution’ (Gupta: 1985, 221) was deliberate and willfully manipulated for electoral gains. Mass media provided emphatic endorsement and became a carrier for a systematic, sustained and vociferous campaign of ’government misinformation and disinformation on Punjab’ (Gupta: 1985, 221). Harji Malik points out that the overwhelming importance to the separate Sikh state of Khalistan was an invention of the media rather than being representative of majority Sikh sentiment (1985, 39). The anti-Sikh rhetoric contained the message that ‘Sikhs had to be taught a lesson. That “they” had it coming!’ (Malik: 1985, 50). For example, in an article appearing in The Indian Express before the events of 1984, Arun Shourie, who later wrote in support of the Sikh view of the October-November massacre targeted and portrayed the entire Sikh community as greedy, manipulative and anti-national:

A demand born out of whipped-up phobias as the panth6-in-danger, is inherently insatiable. The moment you satisfy a part of the demand, or even all of it, a new and more extreme one is put forth…. Do not lull yourself in the belief that Khalistan is just the brainwave of a Jagjit Singh Chauhan.7 It has an ancestry, and in the eyes of many a respectable one (The Politics of Pandering, 1982).

June 1984

The pattern of partial or non-communication continued during Operation Bluestar. There were huge gaps in the media’s reports of this sinister episode. There was a media blackout during Operation Bluestar and two weeks after the army invasion of the Darbar Sahib, newspapers were not published or were unavailable (Singh: 1984, 1569). The state-controlled television channel, Doordarshan, used language such as ‘fortifications’ inside the Golden Temple and ‘caches of arms’ seized by the army. These images were absorbed passively and unconditionally. Much of this information that media reports on the All India Radio and Doordarshan were conveying was false and exaggerated. Sikhs were successfully labelled destructive ‘terrorists’ while the Indian state and army were applauded for ‘saving’ the country (Malik: 1985, 44). As Pritam Singh writing on the media coverage of Operation Bluestar and what followed notes:

It was obvious that such news manipulation was bound to hurt Sikh sentiments. But consistent with the objectives of the media programmes, what mattered was not the Sikhs susceptibilities but the acceptance of such propaganda material by the wider Hindu population. Consequently, Hindu-Sikh relations were further embittered and communal polarization was sharpened (Singh: 1984, 1571).

Reporter Brahma Chellaney who reported the goings-on inside the Darbar Sahib was harassed and charged with sedition by the government (Ghazali: 1985).

After the invasion of the Darbar Sahib, All India Radio and Doordarshan were assertive in their reporting that ‘no damage has been done to Harmandir Sahib’8 during the army action (Singh: 1984, 1571). This was another glaring omission. Sikhs who visited the complex after the army action and saw the damage firsthand, write of the Harmandir Sahib pockmarked and damaged by bullet marks (Nayar and Singh: 1984). Far from playing an objective role in its coverage of the army invasion, media representations of the events as they unfolded were either absent or heavily partial and one-sided. The media created a climate to fuel feelings of hostility and communalism. The Sikh perspective was left out of mass media reporting systematically and deliberately. A journalist writing in Mainstream magazine legitimized and approved of state actions with complete disregard to the effects of this discourse on the Sikh community:

Could the military action in the Golden Temple have been avoided? If what was happening before the military action had continued for another two or three months, there would have been a massive Hindu backlash. There would have been massacre of Sikhs outside Punjab. The possibility of some sort of coup and the army marching into the Golden Temple…could also not have been ruled out (Kewal Varma, ‘Genesis of Sikh Alienation’, 1984).

Expressing his anger, Patwant Singh, a prominent Sikh writer noted, ‘What to others is just a building is to the Sikhs the supreme symbol of their honor and inspiration’ (1985, 21). State-backed mass media failed deplorably in capturing these community reactions. In an evaluation of media reporting during 1984 and the prolonged period of crisis in which Sikhs were embroiled, independent observers and scholars have noted that leading English national dailies like The Times of India and The Hindustan Times, far from diffusing tensions, caused damage and destruction by inciting communal hatred (Das: 2009).

October-November 1984

The same pattern of partial reporting and/or non-reporting continued in October and November when her Sikh bodyguards assassinated Prime Minister Indira Gandhi. The entire Sikh community was targeted and stigmatized and mass media became the main instrument for forming and communicating these images. The mass media neglected and made invisible the organized massacre of Sikhs and instead fixed attention on the death and funeral of Indira Gandhi (Mitta and Phoolka: 2007). As Assem Shrivastava a witness writes:

Such seemed to have been the depth of state endorsement of the violence. Not one image of the looting and arsoning, let alone of the killing, was ever shown on TV. It would have appeared that the city of 9 or 10 million people was in solemn sorrow and mourning at the death of the great departed leader (‘The Winter in Delhi, 1984’, 2005).

The media designed a narrative of communal ‘rioting’, that was a consequence of Hindus’ inflamed sensibilities. State-controlled radio and television depicted the violence as spontaneous ‘madness’ carried out in ‘grief and anger’ (Who are the Guilty? [1984] 2003, 1). Organized massacre of Sikhs was underplayed as ‘riots’ by the media and this language continues to be used to frame the violence today. The term ‘riots’ evokes spontaneity and suddenness when the attacks on the community were intentional and well planned (Who are the Guilty? [1984] 2003). Doordarshan broadcast images of crowds shouting ‘khoon ka badla khoon say’ (blood for blood). As novelist Jaspreet Singh writes it was later found out that this soundtrack had been created by cabinet ministers and members of parliament (Singh: 2012, 160). The only mention of the brutal mayhem that was unfolding on the streets of Delhi and other parts of North India was depicted in an inadequate and impoverished media summary: ‘There have been some disturbances’. The state controlled All India Radio announced that leaving aside a few episodes ‘the situation was under control’. While international media (Singh: 2012, 160) and therefore diaspora Sikhs were watching qualitatively different images of what was unfolding on the streets of Delhi, Indian viewers saw only Mrs. Gandhi’s body lying in state (Malik: 1985, 50).

The slain Prime Minister’s son, Rajiv Gandhi who succeeded her, justified the violence against Sikhs. Rajiv Gandhi issued a statement on 19 November in which he considered the mass massacre of Sikhs, an emotional reaction or a manifestation of people’s ‘krodh’ or anger and said, jab bada péd girta hai, to dharti hilti hai (when a big tree falls, the earth shakes). The state clearly and neatly abdicated all responsibility.

State narratives made it seem like Sikhs had called on Operation Bluestar and now if a ‘handful’ of Sikhs were killed, it was nothing more than collateral damage. Even though Sikhs in Punjab continue to suffer in the aftermath of state atrocities and media narratives (see Kumar: 2008), mainstream media have been only too eager to integrate ‘former terrorists…within the national imagination as harmless, cheerful tricksters in a slew of Bollywood blockbusters…’ (Kaur: 2013).

COUNTER NARRATIVES

One of the first and few Indian initiated counter narratives to the state came from independent human rights organizations. Writing about the June attacks, the organization Citizens for Democracy included a critique of the official response to the demands in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution. The report entitled ‘Report to the Nation: Oppression in Punjab’ was banned by the Indian government. The report states,

Now in 1984 after the Operation Bluestar suddenly the Government clamped down on Punjab an undeclared emergency. Punjab was cut off from the rest of the country for days, censorship was declared, all Pressmen—both foreign and Indian—were expelled. There was total black-out of news except what was told by the Government-run All Indian Radio and Doordarshan, again bringing back the unpleasant memories of Emergency days…Without a scrap of evidence we were expected to believe that it was Pakistan which was responsible for the growth of terrorism in Punjab and there was a seizure of highly sophisticated weapons (‘Report to the Nation: Oppression in Punjab’: 1985, 5-6).

The active role of the state and state-backed media in starting and fuelling the systematic November massacre has been recorded by civil rights organizations, specifically the People’s Union for Democratic Rights and People’s Union for Civil Liberties (PUDL-PUCL), in their report entitled, ‘Who are the Guilty? Report of a Joint Inquiry into the Causes and Impact of Riots in Delhi from 31 October to 10 November’. The report notes,

The attacks on members of the Sikh community…far from being a spontaneous expression of ‘madness’ and of popular ‘grief and anger’ at Mrs. Gandhi’s assassination as made out to be by the authorities, were the outcome of a well-organized plan marked by acts of deliberate commissions and omissions by important politicians of the Congress (I) at the top and by authorities in the administration (Who are the Guilty? ([1984] 2003, 1).

However, these counter narratives have not led to any major shifts in how 1984 is remembered in the Indian context.

The ascendance of Sikh militancy as resistance to state narratives and state action was the most important set of counter narratives that gained ground in the aftermath of 1984. Once again, as with the events of 1984, the state and media stigmatized and dismissed these voices instead of understanding the issues at stake. The militant movement must be contextualized and understood as more nuanced and complex than state and media portrayals (see Mahmood: 1996). At the same time, however, violent methods that militants subscribed to as a form of resistance drowned voices of suffering and trauma instead of bringing them to the attention of mainstream Indian population. It has also been to the state’s advantage to allow only these dissenting narratives to emerge. The Indian state used militant counter narratives as an instrument to paint Sikhs without qualification as ‘secessionist’ and ‘fundamentalist’ (see Major: 1987 for a discussion of subtle differences between labels).

DIASPORA, DIGITAL MEDIA AND
COUNTER MEMORIES

In opposition to top-down memories created by the state and mass media, a section of diasporic Sikhs are doing memory work by using new digital media. Despite the selectivity of all narratives, these spaces are critical for the community to tell their own version of what they experienced during and since 1984. This memory work emphasizes the ‘grievability’ of all lives (Butler: 2010, 13), not just the lives of majority community members, economically advantaged, or political leaders like Indira Gandhi. ‘Memory workers’ (Stein: 2009) are also using digital media to bring attention to social and economic issues of drug abuse, farmer suicide and female feticide that are rampant in Punjab. Digital spaces are helping Sikh memory workers represent a more complex and differentiated portrait of events than in circulation at present. Some of the websites I analysed include: sikhchic.com (created 2006); ensaaf.org (created 2003), sikhgenocide.org (created 2004); sikhtoons.com (created 2009); sikhmuseum.com (created 2000).

DIGITAL CULTURAL MOMENT

Digital means of communication are new forms of writing, speaking and listening in the cultural moment at which we are located. Digital media is a far more horizontal and diffuse form of information creation and circulation than mainstream media. This horizontality of the digital is especially helpful for suppressed narratives, such as Sikhs’ experiences of 1984. While the Indian mass media has been filtering and obliterating Sikh points of view, digital media with its ease of recording, sharing information and making it public, is ushering in new and engaged ways of communicating with the media. The digital cultural moment is playing a key part in upsetting the dominant narratives on 1984 in circulation and coming up with alternative narratives composed of Sikh voices.

Sikhs are telling their own experiences of 1984 so they can assert acknowledgement and speak about 1984 over non-acknowledgement and silence within and outside the community. Digital technology makes it possible to converge many times, many places, many experiences, many ways of expression- writing, speaking, art, music all at once and this convergence is playing an important part in giving digital memory work on 1984 a distinctive edge. Websites in 1984 contained many kinds of audiovisual texts in addition to written accounts to viscerally evoke readers of texts.

Diaspora, Transnational Connections and
Second Generation

Most memory workers are Sikh women and men9 in the diaspora with direct experiences of 1984 or children of survivors. 1984 is an important component of Sikh identity for this segment and they are taking the initiative to create their own texts of 1984 and share these with the community at large. In the face of physical distance from ‘home’ or Punjab, diasporic Sikhs maintain transnational connections with Punjab. This emotional connection is about nostalgia and a longing for the culture and heritage left behind. But it is also about the emotional connection with loss and suffering that becomes an unbreakable tie. In India speaking about 1984 continues to carry fear of being persecuted and being apprehended as ‘terrorists’ and ‘separatists’. Also, the very shock of what they had witnessed directly or indirectly left the first generation speechless. Immigrant and online contexts are providing ‘safe social spaces’ (personal interview) for writing, talking, sharing and listening about 1984. Loss is both real, that is directly experienced and vicarious or imagined, empathetic suffering felt without direct hurt. Memory work is intergenerationally transmitted.

The second generation’s role is especially important in understanding vicarious or imagined suffering of 1984. They are playing an integral part in carrying out active and activist memory work in 1984 based on their postmemories or delayed and indirect imaginings. As a generation that are ‘digital natives’ (Prensky: 2011) that is they are born into rather than migrate to digital cultures, they are at the forefront in recreating and reconfiguring existing narratives. Further, this generation is simultaneously rooted in Sikhi or the Sikh religious and cultural heritage along with a distance from the Indian state and media memories. As one of my second-generation interviewees explained to me, he felt a deep sense of attachment and affinity with Sikh history and heritage including stories of the Gurus and values of seva or altruistic service and shahidi or martyrdom. Second generation Sikhs admit that they do not take their identity for granted and go about finding a ‘sense of purpose’ because they are questioned about their identity on an everyday basis. Because of this particular cultural and temporal location, second-generation narratives of 1984 are different from state and media accounts. In a personal interview, another of my respondents explained:

… there is very much a strong narrative that’s been painted that is not favorable for Sikhs … in North America, with the benefit of distance you’ve been able to put together a Sikh narrative which is supported by tools of Internet and technology.

Another important trigger for the second generation gravitating towards memory work are the events of 9/11 in the United States specifically. This led the second generation to reflect on their ‘difference’ from the mainstream population in ways they had not done before and go back to tracing their genealogies or start memory work.

Memory work is a two-way process. While diasporic Sikhs are motivated to do memory work because of their direct memories of 1984 or commitment to Sikh values, memory work also helps them strengthen their ties with the community. To this extent, memory work performs the role of rituals (see e.g., Connerton: 1989). Memory workers rejuvenate and revive their belongingness to their group identity by re-presenting their own experiences and imagery. This is especially relevant in diasporic contexts where immigrants must continually reinvent and reconstruct ‘home’ and its memories (Hall: 1980). The struggle with top-imposed representations is a struggle to redefine the past as much as it is a struggle for diasporic Sikhs to keep their community identity dynamic and interactional rather than static and unchanging.

Given the distorted representations of 1984 where all Sikhs are painted as ‘violent’, ‘terrorist’ and responsible for creating 1984, an important theme on all websites is to educate by including testimonies of direct and indirect Sikh accounts. Testimonies are stories told by witnesses of violent and traumatic events to reveal aspects of tragedies that might begin to bring about social change (see for example Wolf: 2007). Testimonies of 1984 are often connected with other important events in Sikh history such as the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre of 1919 in which the British army officer General Dyer ordered the shooting on several innocent Sikhs who had come together for a peaceful protest.

COLONIAL RULE; THE PARTITION OF 1947
AND THE BUILDUP TO OPERATION BLUESTAR

Emotions of anger and disbelief and criticism of the Indian state are part of online accounts. Digital spaces are important platforms to talk back to the dominant memories of 1984. The following account is typical of the way events are traced. The significance of the day of attack is brought out as well as the language clearly expresses the Sikh perspective on Operation Bluestar:

On 4 June 1984, India’s Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi, ordered the Indian Army to invade the Golden Temple Complex in Amritsar, the Sikhs’ national institution. As it was the anniversary of the martyrdom of the Sikhs’ 5th Guru Arjun Dev, it was full of thousands of pilgrims…. On the pretext of apprehending a ‘handful of militants’ lodged inside the Golden Temple, the Indian Army unleashed a terror unprecedented in post-independence India (PS, ensaaf.org).

In addition to creating an educational critique of what transpired during Operation Bluestar and the time that immediately followed, there are several accounts that evoke the sinister atmosphere of the October-November genocide. An instance of evocative memories is feelings of humiliation at letting go of kes or hair and an assault on the Sikh Khalsa body. This is poignantly expressed in accounts such as these:

…Word had spread that Indira Gandhi had been shot dead by her Sikh bodyguards… My father had discarded his turban and worn a helmet to reach home, an act that left a deep wound in his psyche … the next three days were about death… The streets of India’s political capital were littered with burning vehicles, burning houses, burning gurudwaras, burning men. Death was in naked display; turbaned Sikhs easy prey. … (HB, sikhchic. com)

Memory workers are using digital media to fill gaps in representation but also facilitate a movement where this reconfigured imagery can facilitate rehabilitation for survivors of 1984. They want to excavate pasts both to (1) understand Sikh identity in the light of this trauma that repeats itself because of the magnitude of damage; and (2) address present-day problems such as drug abuse and farmer suicides in Punjab that can be traced back to the 1980s and begin creating alternative futures.

Sikhs are also asserting their identity by expressing their alienation with the Indian state and its functionaries—the government, courts, police—and aligning themselves with their places of settlement, without using the language of state-media created labels of ‘extremism’ or ‘fundamentalism’. As one website contributor writes:

I had become a US citizen in the late ‘60s, but when I look back, in my heart I must have maintained a dual identity, and shared my loyalty with India. These strands were finally divided post-1984 … Yet, I continue to acknowledge and treasure the rich culture that shaped me, and immensely value Sikhi that came from that land (IJS, sikhchic.com).

CONCLUSION

State-backed Indian media constructed impoverished and limiting representations of Sikhs in the 1980s, specifically 1984. These media representations are the source of dominant memories of the events. Sikhs’ numerical smallness did not automatically translate into a minority consciousness. Rather, this minority consciousness was manufactured by state actions and mediated texts. Memories of this period continue to impinge on the Sikh identity today but without public space for sharing experiences in the Indian national context. A section of intergenerational diasporic Sikh memory workers are overcoming state-engendered and media circulated fear to create spaces for free and uninhibited discussion of experiences of the time. Digital media is proving indispensable in these efforts. Digital media’s easy accessibility where information is horizontally produced and circulated rather than vertically consumed is a major impetus behind memory work to talk back to dominant memories of 1984. Memory work both builds on belongingness to the Sikh community but also acts as a ritual to rejuvenate community identity and representations.

Memory work around 1984 is emerging and fledgling. While a section of Sikhs in the diaspora are invested in excavating memories and making efforts at placing their narratives alongside official representations, not everyone in the community is an equal participant. The fear of retribution and the solidity of state and mass media accounts continue to haunt the community. The passage of time, however, and the shift toward public commemoration of 1984 in and through digital media is facilitating subtle changes within the community. How will this digital and diasporic memory work affect representations in the mass media and in the Indian national context? Can frozen and static official memories begin to start responding to this memory work? These are questions that time and future research will reveal. In the meantime, the relationship between media, memories and Sikh consciousness alerts us to the durable impact of media texts on producing marginalization.
