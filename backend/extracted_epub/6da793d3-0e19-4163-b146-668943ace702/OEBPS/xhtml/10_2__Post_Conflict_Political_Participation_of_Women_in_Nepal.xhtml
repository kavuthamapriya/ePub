Post-Conflict Political Participation
of Women in Nepal

SARMILA BAGALE

HISTORICAL OVERVIEW OF NEPALESE WOMEN

The total population of Nepal as of 2021 was 29,164,578, with females comprising 51.13 per cent, which is slightly higher than the number of males.1 The present Constitution of Nepal 2015 AD has guaranteed some specific provisions to offer certain political rights to women. It has acknowledged, ‘Every woman shall have equal lineage right without gender-based discrimination’, Article 38 and Article 38(5) accepts that [Nepalese] women have obtained special privileges in education, health, employment, social security, etc., to empower the women for making positive changes in their lives.2

Historically, Nepal, a Himalayan country, emerged as a unified state, the Kingdom of Gorkha Shah dynasty, which governed the Kathmandu valley. This region has a long history, with over 2,000 years of civilization. From the beginning to the emerging Shah dynasty, there was a very strong influence of Hindu patriarchal mindset in the Nepalese political system that continuously marginalized the women in the political system. Nepal is a culturally diverse country and Hindu religion played a significant role in shaping Nepalese society; and only the promulgation of Interim Constitution in 2006 changed in this provision.3 In a patriarchal society, like Nepal, the relationship between male and female is formed by religious ideologies.4 Hinduism has been a way of life for Nepalese people as majority of people follow this religion. It shapes the socio-cultural values in Nepalese society. R. Stump (2008) asserts that religion is deeply ingrained in peoples’ experiences and behaviours which stimulates the socio-economic and political dynamics of societies.5 In both the modern Western worldview, and in Nepal, religion shapes human perspective and influences the control of societal events.

Different perspectives of religion play a dominant role in shaping social system, e.g. substantive view of religion entails it as a system of culturally experiencing the way of life and its practices lean towards cultural dominion of people. The functional view of religion establishes powerful constant moods and motivations in people’s mind to develop conceptions of a general order of existence. In this way, male domination in Nepalese society—despite the higher female population—is reflected in national policy. B.N. Adams and R.A. Sydie (2009) note that Western religion stems from a patriarchal worldview, resulting in patriarchal domination across all sectors.6 In line with this, A. Sachedina (2003) remarked that women were historically denied fundamental human rights as a result of economic manipulation and exploitation of their position in the family and society.7 Unlike this, the revolt of Yogmaya Neupane—a common Brahmin widowed-woman gave voices to the voiceless women, had married thrice when widow marriage was a deplorable offence in Nepalese society. It was an offence against religious values of Hindu socio-politico-cultural system that inspired Nepalese women to stand for their own future and fight against sexual discrimination. Her movement from traditional social beliefs to modern ways of thinking was an initiation of new threat to patriarchal political power system of Nepal.8

Michael Foucault (1981) states that the theoretical modality of political exclusion of women is a part of political ideas that intends to subordinate the females in social sector. For him, the notion of political exclusion of women leads to marginalization in socio-political sector. The emergence of exclusionary institutions is accompanied by the formation of scientific language that incorporates the idea of exclusion. He discusses exclusion and exclusionary procedures, whereby in every society the use of discourse is simultaneously managed, chosen, arranged and redistributed by a number of procedures whose purpose is to avoid its formidable, ponderous materiality, to gain control over its chance events and to fend off its powers and dangers.9 S. Bem (1994) frameworks three elementary assumptions that shape the cultural understandings of human beings that make a difference between males and females. The hypotheses are ‘andocentric’, ‘gender polarization’ and ‘biological essentialism’.10 The androcentric assumption always places male at the centre and female at the periphery. The gender polarization reinforces the idea that male and female are socially different while biological essentialism is emphasized as the most important aspect of the society. In this context, the situation of women in Nepal is not different to other places and politics is a power device to shape and lead the society and other existing institutions for peace, participation and development. As everywhere, in Nepal women are holding less political power compared to their male counterparts and have occupied less political space to govern the country. One of the major reasons behind it is a patriarchal mindset that has created structural discrimination against women to create socio-economic barrier which is part of the political system for the meaningful sharing of power for the women in politics.

Nepalese society has differently structured the gender relations, in which women are seen to be predominantly cooking, cleaning and rearing children. Nepalese women, as in most of the countries, had very pathetic and terrible life. They were living with double exploitation of class and gender. In addition, child marriage, polygamy and forced marriage were prevalent in Nepalese society. Punam Yadav has analysed the condition of Nepalese women from the perspective of social transformation and views that the social realities and religious beliefs construct the political framework.11 Bourdieu and Nice elucidate how masculine domination influences the structures of the political system. The social world view is always perceived by males as market goods for women.12 As we have argued here religious social structure is based on masculine world view which since long had curbed the political desire of Nepalese women, since the state culture is viewed as a social structure ruled by male principles. To justify it, the fundamental myth creates an opposition between nature and culture, or more specifically, between nature’s sexuality and society’s sexuality. The act of anomie, which is performed next to the fountain, the epitome of the feminine and was instigated by the woman, the perverse seductress, who was naturally knowledgeable about matters of love, contrasts with the act of nomos, which is domesticated and domesticated, performed at the behest of man and adhering to the order of things, fundamental hierarchy of the social order and cosmic order.13 R. Eyben (2010) describes political empowerment as ‘people’s capacity to influence policy, make demands and call to account the state institutions that impact upon their lives. This includes political representation and collective action’.14 In many public spheres and across all geographic regions, women continue to face severe discrimination because of their involvement in public and political life. The under representation of women in positions of authority and decision-making has many intricate and varied causes. In addition to negative stereotypes about women and ingrained gender roles, economic, social and cultural issues also pose significant barriers to women’s involvement in public and political situations. The question of how gender is constituted differently in public and private spaces is central to the conceptualization of gender discrimination in the public domain. Feminist academics have been attempting to eliminate the barrier between public and private space for more than twenty years.15

WOMEN AFTER 1951 TO 1996

Two expected and highly ambitious plans were framed after the downfall of Rana regime that had ruled Nepal for 104 years. Deepak Thapa and Bandita Sijapati (2006) assert that King Tribhuvan had given a historic speech on 18 February 1951 and the monarch had declared his intention and choice to draft a democratic constitution by a constituent assembly.16 This political shift of 1951 was a major political movement in Nepalese political history to protect the rights of the people in different fields. Even after the end of Rana regime, during the first democratic period (1951-61), the socio-political sphere of women in Nepal was not remarkably good and authoritative patriarchal domination still played in their lives. Women’s political participation and inclusion in policy making level even in Panchayat regime (1961-90)17 remained too thin and this non-democratic system could not bring any policy to change in the existing system. It failed to draw the devotion to macro policies of development for women’s participation in politics and economic sectors. The political game of exclusion was practiced again, often in these same places and for the same reason—to make women weaker in politics and social sectors.

The Panchayat system had institutionalized the privileges of Kathmandu-elites that only prioritized the patriarchal system and ultimately it had marginalized the women. In Nepalese political history, the cultural understanding of male and female is different and is constructed by religious ideologies. Religious and cultural factors had placed the males at the centre and as Yadav writes until 2006 Hinduism was a dominant religious ideology in Nepal18 and only the Interim Constitution 2007 had accepted Nepal as a secular country. The political movement of 1990 was an end of Panchayat system that reinstated a multi-party democratic system and brought a little change in political ideology. However, the new Constitution in 1991 failed to address the political problems of Nepalese women and they felt no substantial changes in their political career. It failed to make a good progress on people’s participation in structural politics of Nepal. Catinca Slavu (2012) views that the failure of social inclusion to a large extent was a consequence of insufficient changes in the 1991 constitution and it maintained key exclusionary features reaffirming Nepal as a Hindu state and maintaining highly centralized state structures.19 It had created a gap between the constitution, rhetoric and gender equality within Nepalese politics. The 1991 Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal, called for the political parties to reserve at least 5 per cent candidature for women and made the assurance of 5 per cent women’s representation in parliamentary system. The following subsequent national elections in 1991, 1994 and 1999 resulted in only 5 per cent and 6 per cent and 9 per cent of women’s representation in parliament through election. These figures demonstrate women’s representation in parliament increased on average by only 4 per cent.20

B.R. Upreti (2008) states that ‘with the success of the democratic struggle of 1990, political awareness among Nepalese people had increased tremendously and their expectations with new government were much higher than in the past. However, the government failed to introduce good policies to fulfil the commitments’21 and Yadav adds that the Government of Nepal slackened to bridge the gap between haves and have-nots and eliminating inequality based on caste, ethnicity, gender, religion, region and sex which encouraged the Maoists to lead the new movement for establishing new social system based on their leftist ideology.22 The basic feature of women in democratic system of governance, even after the political changes of 1991, their participation and access to policy-making level was quite negligible. The Fourth World Conference on Women, held in Beijing in 1995 had focused on women’s participation in all the decision-making levels around the world. In Nepal, women’s participation in legislative, judiciary and executive were not felt important. It has thus become difficult for women to break from the vicious circle of poverty and disempowerment. Women were economically and politically disadvantaged due to their exclusion from the public domain and lack of public involvement.

Unlike this, Tulsi Acharya (2017) assumes that, after avoiding the constitution of 1991 by Maoists, Nepalese women’s participation in political movements made them politically, socially and culturally conscious. It acknowledged the changes in their traditional ways of thinking to move ahead in the political history. Their encouraging participation in the political movement persuaded them to challenge the social, cultural and religious practices that had marginalized them for centuries. Once more, the study indicates that women’s access to positions of political and administrative decision-making has been limited because of social expectations regarding their exclusive household responsibilities, limited mobility relative to men and limited access to economic and educational resources. The women’s movement in Nepal after 1990 is shaped by many political forces and ideological perspectives and is ingrained in the country’s political history. The constitution of 1990 had opined that ‘Nepal is a multi-ethnic, multi-lingual, democratic, independent, sovereign, Hindu, constitutional, monarchical kingdom’ and guaranteed fundamental rights to all citizens without discrimination based on ethnicity, caste, religion or sex.23

The political leaders had openly expressed the constitution to be ‘the best constitution in the world’. However, the constitution had offered the king as the central authority and finally the constitution failed to incorporate many demands of common people, women, etc. A unified women’s movement cannot emerge in a society that is driven by other hierarchical distinctions; it can only do so in tandem with the removal of other social bases of subordination aside from gender, despite the need for one being so obvious. This is why the most common catchphrase among Nepalese women activists even today is the lack of a certain political understanding. Secondly, attempts to enhance women’s status via associations and campaigns that are inexorably influenced by other societal divisions could unintentionally or intentionally hinder the development of a cohesive women’s movement.24 Therefore, Nepalese women had realized the importance of power politics and got the ideas of power and its legitimacy in politics that had reshaped their mind to build the ideas of political authority. Without getting political power, social transformation for Nepalese women was not possible.

NEPALESE WOMEN IN CONFLICT

The failure of the constitution of Nepal was the basic point for the Maoists to wage a decade long conflict. They began the protest in 1996 and many women from rural area were involved in the rebellion. The women, then, played the roles in the militia, cultural group, joined to play active role in the Maoists’ army and in the political wing of the party. They got actively involved in politics to get their political rights. Women mostly followed Marxism, Leninism and Maoism (MLM) ideology, which in turn inspired them to be fully free from patriarchal domination. Before 1996, there was a growing recognition of political discrimination against women based on gender, class, and sex. In fact, there was a strong desire for social transformation across the regions of the country to permanently improve the condition of women. The CPN (Maoist) had expressed its firm commitment about ending patriarchal exploitation and gender discrimination and giving the right of granting parental property to daughters.25 Similarly, Sierra Tamang agrees that among the many actors in the women’s movement in Nepal after 1990s, the CPN-M has received the most attention. The majority of writing about the ‘People’s War’ in Nepal by foreign and domestic journalists has included images of young and armed guerrilla women, wearing combat fatigues along with claims that between 30 per cent and 50 per cent of women participated in the conflict.26 Hisila Yami (2007) concedes that the strategy of incorporating women into the people’s army from the beginning has raised socio-political awareness, forcing the political parties of Nepal to recognize the need of addressing women’s political demands which highlights the unique nature of the People’s War.

The socio-political position of Nepalese women was shaped by political structure of the country which was prevalent at a political and socio-cultural level. The fundamental problems which have exploited the women as the second type of citizen was reflected in the context of Nepalese politics before the initiation of Maoist’s movement were stronger than at present time were the exploitation in social sector and marginalization. Nepalese cultural imperialism and violence were of the same patriarchal domination. Before 1996 the women who had ideological influence of MLM had observed much discrimination and involvement in politics to get political freedom. In a short time, as Sharma and Prasain (2004) write that the number of Maoists cadres gradually increased to 30 per cent to 40 per cent, attracting common people. They affirm that women have taken up a variety of roles, including those of nurses, couriers, organizers and even fully-fledged guerrilla combatants.27 The scholars have written that the women involved in Maoist movement were radical and CPN (Maoist) always advocated assuring rights for women, especially the working-class women. Yami (2006) writes Nepalese women became professionals in the revolution by joining People’s Liberation Army, militias and production brigades. Women were instructed to target the feudal state apparatus as a means of combating class and gender oppression following the start of the Maoist struggle. For the first time, they were actively organized in villages against the landlord, patriarchal customs, superstitions and socio-cultural stigmata.

This political movement of 2006 gave women political power to make choices and guaranteed them a crucial role in legislature after the initiation of the peace process. Nepalese women were essential political and cultural activists throughout a ten-year political battle. They had even participated in the military operations of the rebels. Women who were family members of the security forces’ were slained, raped, tortured and imprisoned throughout the fighting. The battle caused immense suffering to the spouses, mothers and daughters of men who were slained, disappeared, or subjected to torture. In addition to providing for their families’ needs, they also had to fulfil social and economic obligations. Some women were involved in combat as well. From a philosophical perspective, the ten-year conflict in Nepal is seen as a catalyst for the socio-political inclusion of women, initiating structural changes within the political strata. This shift has transformed the concept of political realization into an understanding of the historical systems of political guidelines. In this regard, the Maoist movement in Nepal represented a significant political turn for bringing equality of women across social classes while advancing their professional and political prospects. It truly brought major changes in political and economic sectors for women, assisting in their empowerment in Nepal.

The Interim Constitution of Nepal (2007) sufficiently addressed gender and women’s rights and, for the first time in Nepal’s political history, recognized women as equal citizens to men. This legal provision brought significant changes, supporting women’s empowerment and increasing their political participation in Nepalese politics. Regarding ‘Citizenship’, the Interim Constitution of Nepal (2007) approved the following provisions: no sex discrimination; employment and social security rights; women’s rights; right to social justice; right against exploitation; and equality between men and women. The Constitution of 2015 reaffirms this in Article (11)(2)(b) stating that a person whose father or mother was a citizen of Nepal at their birth is entitled to citizenship by descent. This marked the first-time women were empowered in this way. However, as Susan Risal writes in The Himalayan, a major English newspaper, women wounded by conflict are battling both external and internal traumas. To help them mend their wounds and rehabilitate them in society with dignity, the state and the transitional justice systems must put in an effort. These women deserve justice, but they also deserve to live honourable lives. Despite these facts, the Interim Constitution incorporated some specific provisions to provide political support to women. The government set up provisions for women’s political participation and constitutionalized it. Women’s participation in politics through election processes to carry out the Constitution and their growing voice is marked and the ‘yes we can do’, mentality is predicted to contribute to equality and success in the economy as well. Empowerment analysis must consider women’s engagement in public and political life in addition to their access to and mobilization within official and informal political venues. Political empowerment is no longer limited to the official sphere, which includes political parties and elections. It now faces additional obstacles as it must operate in entirely uncharted public and political areas regarding the strength of regional to international alliances, the voices and influence of women and innovative approaches to accountability. This paper sheds light on the ways in which the formal and informal converge, emphasizing these novel perspectives on public and political life and demonstrating how state-society connections might generate avenues for the advancement of gender equality objectives.

WOMEN IN POST-CONFLICT NEPAL

The initiation of peace process and promulgation of Interim Constitution were the consequences of a decade long conflict and the peaceful political movement of 2006. The first historic election of the Constituent Assembly in 2008 brought significant change in the pattern of gender equality. Women’s representation in the Legislature Parliament increased dramatically to 33 per cent placing Nepal sixteenth out of 140 countries in terms of the percentage of women in national parliament, down from sixty-eight in 1999. According to Tamang Sierra, the people’s movement of April 2006 exerted unprecedented pressure on the re-established parliament through the large-scale participation of women from excluded populations from urban to rural areas.28 The most positive part of Nepali peace process that was framed after 2006 had accepted the principle of socio-political transformation of Nepalese people including women. It was against traditional thinking that had aimed to liberate Nepalese women from age long traditional method of production and way of life. In Schlitz’s views a traditional society always accepts traditional values while a modern political world view always accepts the capacity of changing the society, inclusion of women in politics and finally making social transformation for peace and sustainable development. Nepalese Civil War that lasted for a decade was followed by the initiation of peace process and the election for the Constituent Assembly for drafting a constitution for Nepalese people. 32.8 per cent of Nepali women were elected as legislators in the voting system in 2008, which included a proportional system. This represents a significant improvement above the 5.8 per cent of women elected in 1999. According to K. Bhattarai and D. Budd (2008), Nepal became the region’s leader in female representation overnight when one-third of the elected parliamentarians were female.29

The Constitution of Nepal 2015 aims to end ‘all forms of discrimination and oppression created by the feudalistic, autocratic, centralized, unitary system of governance’30 (Preamble) and it accepts the role of the glorious history of historic people’s movements, armed conflict, dedication and sacrifice undertaken by Nepalese people that ensures political participation to ensure economic equality, prosperity and social justice for all people. Sarmila Bagale notes that women’s empowerment is demonstrated by their election-based entry into politics for the purpose of enforcing the constitution and by the ensuing attitude of ‘we can do it’ that permeates contemporary society. It is anticipated that women who were marginalized before the adoption of the constitution 2015 will take a more active part in the growth and prosperity of the nation.31 From this background, we can assume that Nepal has made significant progress on women’s rights and political participation.

The academic research works available mainly in this sector focus on political and socio-economic transformation of Nepalese women. Jeevan Raj Sharma and Antonio Donini (2010) view that there is clear evidence of qualitative changes on the aspects of women’s lives which seem more powerful than the normal process of change. They also believe that the current transition process is bringing about significant changes in important political, economic and sociocultural relationships. While the Maoists have played a major role in this process, other elements also significantly contributed to it.32 Yadav (2016) opines the following remarks on the achievement of present constitution of Nepal as it is also explicitly stated in the 2015 that the women would not face any form of gender-based discrimination. Women now have equal rights over inheritance, protection from gender-based violence and reproductive health under the new constitution.33 The political movement with specific guideline can have some intended results as Nepalese women got from the conflict of Nepal. In fact, the political movement which was followed by a successful peace process, led to structural changes by creating an enabling environment for women to exercise their political power. Women politics in Nepal moved from the hard conditions of civil conflict, women demonstration and transformation. In line with this, the conflict of Nepal had ultimately brought many positives changes especially for women. The representation of women in the first and second constituent assembly was a historic representation.

The present constitution of Nepal has given sufficient space to women in political and decision-making level and in the future, it will increase their capacity to fight for equality and justice. Again, it will by design reduce women’s socio-economic and cultural vulnerability from Nepalese society. The access of Nepalese women in political field through electoral process can increase their representation in practice. The many women in numbers can participate in political level; it becomes easier to implement the constitution of Nepal. The positive attitude of women and their voice ‘yes we can do’ attitude has grown among the women is a good sign for women’s political movement and it will enhance the prosperity and equality in politico-economic sector as well. The wave of a political conflict and the mass political movement heralded in the drafting of a new Constitution that ended a decade long political violent of Nepal. Offering the right to gender equality as a fundamental principle is one obvious tendency in this process of constitutional amendment. Realizing gender equality and allowing women to participate in politics is a standard of contemporary political values that protects each citizen’s human rights. In the twenty-first century, widely accepted view is the inclusion of rights of women in which the women can enjoy democratic legitimacy and they feel they are the product of some process of public participation that reflects the composition of the population.

The 2017 local election was significant for women’s political representation in Nepal. At least 40.4 per cent had to be nominated by the political parties in 753 local units. Of these 35,041 elected representatives, 40.96 per cent women got victory and in the political history of Nepal it was a great achievement.34 In the case of national election held for Federal legislature parliament, in South Asian countries, Nepal tops the index of women in political participation which is the significant result of the political shift of inclusive democracy of Nepal.35 The 2015 Nepalese Constitution recognizes that everyone has the right to live in dignity and guarantees 33 per cent of women equal representation in all political spheres. It also eliminates discrimination based on class, caste, sex, gender and other categories. The constitutional allowance of women to hold positions of political and decision-making authority would undoubtedly lessen the socioeconomic and cultural vulnerabilities that women face in Nepalese society. Women’s participation in politics through election processes to carry out the Constitution and their growing ‘yes we can do’ mentality are predicted to contribute to equality and success in the economy as well.

CONCLUSION

The constitution looks at women’s official political participation, such as their ability to choose political leadership roles, take part in politics and influence the political process after being elected. The advancements in these three areas of political participation of women since 2015 seems positive. It draws attention to national trends and advancements as well as obstacles preventing women from participating in the politics. In doing so, it examines efforts to demonstrate that, while political quotas are prevalent, there is the overwhelming evidence of their successful implementation. The women’s advocacy movement is important because it has the potential to advance gender equality and drive change. When considering practical strategies, it is important to ensure that, once elected, women have a meaningful influence on decision-making and resource allocation.

In addition, political philosophy of every party is the main concern for the participation of women in response to the crisis of the modern republican country Nepal which has subsequently adopted the fundamental principles for the social model in the constitution. In such a case, it provides a philosophical discussion of inclusive democratic system and women can see the practice of political participation in national politics. Then, it creates a congenial environment between the legal and moral obligation of rights and questions the moral justifications offered for the right to inclusive political participation of women. For this, Nepalese Constitution acknowledges that all citizens shall be equal before law and in rights of women (Article 38) and it accepts that every woman shall have equal lineage right without gender-based discrimination and they have right to participate in all bodies of the State based on the principle of proportional inclusion.36 If we observe the bright picture of women that the post-conflict constitution mirrors it seem positive and still there are some challenges in this sector. Due to educational background and political experiences compared to their male counterparts, women have inadequate political power to enjoy equal rights. For this, even at present the political role of all political parties seem to use the Constitutional rights to transform the socio-economic life of the women of Nepal. The status of women and their position in politics highlights some of the social and cultural issues with the country moving toward more women-friendly approaches. The study reflects the progress of women’s status and their increasing political participation that can bring changes in modern society.
