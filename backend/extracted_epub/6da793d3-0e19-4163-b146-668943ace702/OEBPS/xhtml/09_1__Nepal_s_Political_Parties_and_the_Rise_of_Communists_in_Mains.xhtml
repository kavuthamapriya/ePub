CHAPTER 1

Nepal’s Political Parties and the
Rise of Communists in
Mainstream Politics

BHIM NATH REGMI

Nepal—a South Asian country has approximately 26.5 million people occupying a horizontal strip of land between two neighbours India and China. It is an immensely diverse country and the Constitution of Nepal 2015 accepts ‘. . . Nepal is a multicultural, multilingual, multi-religious, multi-ethnic’ by all measures, including geography and caste. Prithvi Narayan Shah appeared ready for battle and determined to bring together the smaller states of Nepal to form a larger nation, is closely linked to the history of modern Nepal. He ruled over all eastern Nepal and a huge part of present Sikkim between that time and his death in 1775. Following him, Rajendra Laxmi and Bahadur established a strong state in the Himalayan region with the support of warriors from Nepal. Nepal is one of the world’s most ethnically diverse nations. The issues of castes, classes, or communities in a nation building process become the part of state restructuring. The characteristics of Nepal, never colonized by an external power, are its diverse geography, ethnicity and religion. After killing innocent but brave Nepalese officials in Kot and Bhandarkhal in 1903 and getting political power from Surendra Bikram Shah, Jung Bahadur Rana initiated a despotic system. He had destroyed all of his main competitors and put his own candidate on the throne and all the key positions and made sure that he was the Prime Minister when it came to making important administrative decisions.1 King Surendra turned to be a rubber stamp of Rana regime whom the king had granted the power of the King of Lamjung and Kaski districts. For 104 years, Rana rulers practiced all executive powers including the appointment and dismissal of officials and the formulation of Nepalese laws to rule the country and it collapsed in only 1951.2

In Nepal, different political events occurred to dethrone Ranas and various political parties were established to institute democratic regime. In 1876, Lakhan Thapa Magar a native from Gorkha district led a systematic political revolt against the Rana rulers and organized local people to revolt against the regime to offer justice to the people. After 55 years of this unsuccessful move, another event of Prachanda Gorkha3 was established in 1931 to end the Rana regime and help weaken the root of despotic system through public awareness. Praja Parishad4 was established in 1936 to oust the Rana autocratic rule and the activists of this movement had appealed to the common people to fight for freedom, justice and human dignity. Deepak Thapa and Bandita Sijapati state, ‘Nepal Praja Parishad advocated reforms and fermented anti-establishment sentiments among the people. The Rana rulers quickly crushed the movement.’5 In 1937 the Nepalese Citizen Rights Committee was established to create awareness through pamphlets and articles. The political activists Sukharaj Shastri, Dharma Bhakta Mathema, Dasaratha Chanda and Ganga Lal Shrestha were killed in 1941.6 Jayatu Sanskritam movement of 1947 was followed by a group of students against the Rana regime. Pramode Shamshere J.B. Rana (1999) states that the protestors called for Jayatu Sanskritam.7 The students of the Tin Dhara Pathshala Sanskrit School in Kathmandu claimed democracy, basic welfare and the insertion of contemporary subjects in their curriculum and it was the first students’ political movement (Gautama, 2005), which supported the political movement of 1951.8

Political parties are imperative to the development of democracy and nation-building. Within the political system, they carry out different tasks. In addition to forming political opinions, they carry out some traditional duties like socialization, recruitment of officials, mobilization and mediation among agencies, the state and society. Political parties are receptive and accountable to society through these actions. As a means of nation-building, they also handle conflicts by providing a forum for social, cultural, political and economic integration. They strive for democracy, justice, equality, inclusion and political empowerment. In practice, political parties play a fundamental role within a well-functioning democracy, representing citizens’ interests and verbalizing policies that could support common people’s concerns. In the past, especially in developing countries like Nepal, political parties seemed disconnected from the policies to unite people. They pretended to have connected citizens’ interests with the political guidelines of the parties. In line with this, the Nepalese political parties, were formed to dismantle the repressive Rana family system and establish a democratic regime in Nepal.9

From the above historical perspective, different political movements initiated in Nepalese political history which was essential for setting up a representative democratic system. Hachhethu (2002) views that Nepalese political parties were organized and established ‘in opposition to the Rana oligarchy in the country,’10 and fought for making the people sovereign in history. NC aimed to establish democracy in Nepal—the party leaders were influenced by Indian political movement. Hachhethu (2002) states that the political base of the NC was the same as other political movements and opines, ‘Since the parties had emerged with a mission they had suffered from the lack of clarity of thought once their mission to bring an end to the Rana regime was fulfilled through the 1950-1-armed revolution’.11

The communist movement officially emerged in Nepal in 1949. Pushpa Lal Shrestha, a member of the NC, had quit the party and joined the communist movement. The party’s slogan, at the initial stage, was to fight for ‘civil liberties for all the classes’ was officially drawn from the guideline of Marxist political theory. The political circumstance of Nepal and structure of Nepali society justified that Nepal was a living reality to heighten the communist movement. Archie Brown (2010) views that Marx secretly thought that people would live more freely than ever under communism and the future society which he predicted as an inevitable stage for human development.12 Similarly, Hachhethu observes that although the political parties did not initially appear to be ideological groups when they were formed, the CPN adopted the readymade ideologies of Marxism and Leninism with the aim of overthrowing the despotic Rana rule and establishing democracy.13

After the end of the Rana regime, Nepalese political parties carried out political democratization and modernization through political education and by articulating public interest. However, in a short time, the political leaders appeared weak to maintain a dynamic leadership for sustaining democracy. Using the constitutional emergency power, King Mahendra seized political power over the State on 15 December 1960, claiming that the NC government, formed in 1959, had failed to uphold law and order, encouraged corruption and prioritized party politics over the interests of the country. The King imposed direct rule, adjourned the constitution, dissolved the elected parliament and the cabinet while imprisoning the then Prime Minister B.P. Koirala as well as his closest allies.14 The parties were forbidden to organize any activities. After the political coup d’état of 1960, he got a political space to introduce the party-less panchayat system in 1961.15 As the government was dissolved, Mahendra took over, imprisoning the opponents and establishing an authoritarian regime. He banned political parties outright in favour of a system without the existence of political parties as he believed that they contained antinational elements. In addition, he disapproved of and protested King Tribhuvan’s choice to recognize a democratic system and hold an election of constituent assembly.16 The political leaders, therefore, failed to consolidate the state, ‘a state in which the whole is greater than the sum of its parts’.17 Thereafter, political conflict intensified due to poverty and income inequality. From the beginning, all parties both the NC and the CPN experienced challenges with implementing intraparty democracy and improving the living standard of people. The main leaders chose to nominate the central working committees according to their ways, which ultimately weakened the ideological struggle within the parties. The undemocratic structure of the leading political parties hindered the equal participation of all strata of society further disadvantaging communities that had already been marginalized before the advent of democracy.

The involvement of marginalized groups in politics by the radical communists was a positive sign, garnering significant support from the common people. The poor oppressed politically and socially excluded individuals, along with women benefitted from the open-space programmes of the communists to articulate their aspirations, which helped expand their political programmes. Even the political movements of 1951 and 1990 failed to address the common desire of people. K.P. Malla (1973) argues that the political change of 1951 occurred within a definite political framework and parties had to outline democratic codes to govern the people. After 1951, in the post-democratic era, Nepali intellectuals explored political guidelines, emphasizing that the post-1950 decade was marked by an unprecedented proliferation of intellectual and cultural opportunities, replacing the traditional constraints with a new found liberation and intellect freedom. The decade from 1950 to 1960 is aptly referred to as an extroverted decade, marked by a plethora of ideas, events and coordinated endeavours.18

To settle the political issues and achieve the socio-economic progress in the country, the political parties are the main stake holders to implement good policies and set the required plans. However, the parties and the leaders failed to play a significant role which offered the space for political conflicts in different times. The NC holds the title of principal proponent of Nepal’s multiparty democracy because of its history of democratic struggle. Despite claiming to support democratic socialism, the NC has historically, supported liberal, centre-right policies. Despite its repeated setbacks by the monarchy in 1960 and 2002, as well as ongoing internal strife, it has played a vital role in Nepal’s political evolution establishing a secular and republican government. Although several NC leaders have voiced doubts about federalism and secularism, the party has shown flexibility since the 2006 transition and has, in theory, supported progressive agendas like positive discrimination for marginalized groups and proportional representation.

PARTY CULTURE AND IDEOLOGY

Based on a particular ideological worldview, the leaders mobilize their activists and common people within the party organization and craft new opportunities to rule the country. Every political party made up of a group of people stands unitedly to promote the national interest within a shared set of ideological principles. The agenda of political party celebrates the fact that the party building is for acknowledging its own socio-political goals to maximize intraparty democracy. The ideology of NC endorsed its guideline as ‘democratic socialism’ from its Birgunj party convention,19 and aimed to ameliorate for the effective development of the country. The slogan of CPN at its initial convention was ‘civil liberties for all classes’, with the idea of armed struggle clearly outlined. Some radical communists followed the same doctrine even after 1990, expressing their commitment to the belief that ‘Nepal should establish a new democracy as in China, if necessary through armed struggle, so as to create a People’s Republic’.20 For a long time, it remained a firm ideological doctrine of the Maoists and they accepted the principle to transform the country into a socialist state as Lenin had coined in Russia. Similarly, the present Constitution of Nepal (2015) affirms its commitment to socialist democratic norms and values.21

Even in 1984, the Royal Palace controlled Nepali politics and the country’s central government was disorganized while lacking a clear strategy. The political division between the hard-liner and liberal panchas,22 represented by Lokendra Bahadur Chanda and Surya Bahadur Thapa, deepened as dissatisfaction with the panchayat system grew.23 If such a political situation develops, the ideology of every political party becomes a governing principle from which the party advances its activities among the people. Ideology and party principles are the central aspects of political parties based on which they train their workers and get the support of the people to achieve their intended political goals. Politically, NC had a strong faith in a liberal economy and since its establishment a huge section of the middle-class people favoured its ideology. In contrast, the CPN brought the voices of the voiceless people, the landless, the oppressed and espoused rural agrarian struggle to fulfil the party mission. Yub Nath Lamsal writes, ‘It is the ideology and values that make a political party distinct and different from others.’24 Nepalese communist parties were established to make the people happier than before, and the first pamphlet of CPN accepted the same feeling. As the principle of Marxism states, ‘Karl Marx sincerely believed that under communism—the future society of his imagination which he saw as an inevitable and ultimate, stage of human development—people would live more freely than ever before.’25

The political identity of every political party is attached to the political guidelines. The credibility of the party remains among the people for a long, if the party’s ideology is shaped by political history, the missions of establishing the party, its objectives and the moral ethics of the leaders. The study of the political culture and ideology of NC and CPN shows that they were established with the same background but look different in their political doctrines. NC adopted democratic socialism whereas CPN’s party line embraced the global views of Marxist theory and its doctrine of class struggle. The available sources states that the political parties in Nepal had once again adopted the same political tactic to restore democracy in 1989-90. The coalition movement of Democratic Party NC, United Left Forum (ULF) and United National People’s Movement (UNPM)—a group of radical Communists succeeded in restoring democracy in Nepal after 30 years of absolute monarchical rule under the party-less panchayat system. Though NC and the Communist Parties had ideological differences, they united in their goal to liberate the country from an active monarchy. Together, the NC and ULF sought to construct a democratic system in 1990. However, Baburam Bhattarai led the UNPM, which was composed exclusively of ultra-left political groups, including the CPN (Mashal) under Prachanda and the CPN (Mashal) under Mohan Bikram Singh,26 both of which had adopted a comprehensive democratic agenda. After the formation of the interim government led by Krishna Prasad Bhattarai, the leader of NC in 1990, there was an understanding between NC and leaders of ULF to form a democratic constitution that had issued a directive to the interim government and stated that the King must be a constitutional monarch and act only on the advice of Council of Ministers. Their common agenda was the new constitution must be fully democratic and every law and institution that is in opposition to the democratic values needs to be changed. Every citizen must have access to democratic rights.27

Nepal’s political parties had an enormous task following the restoration of democracy in 1990 and the democratic movement successfully swapped the active panchayat system which had ruled Nepal for three decades. Restoring multiparty democracy, lifting the prohibition on political parties and curtailing rather than toppling the King’s authority were the goals of the 1990 pro-democratic movement. Only with the united action, the political parties could guarantee freedom and equality for all citizens, but it could not happen in practice. It was the main problem of Nepalese political parties that brought a political rift to fuel the grounds for radical communists. For the radical communist parties, the political movement of 1990 could not address the common political reality as it had accepted the political boundary of constitutional monarchy, it automatically widened the gap among the parties. G.P. Koirala, General Secretary of NC, remarked in a mass gathering in Kathmandu, stating that the objectives of the movement were to establish democracy and draft a new constitution, ultimately bringing an end to the constitutional monarchy. Since the King’s promises have been broken numerous times throughout history, NC ensured that these demands would be fulfilled this time. The leaders could only accomplish these objectives by remaining united.28

Even after the successful political movement of 1990, Nepal faced several hurdles in its ways and people experienced political crises that fuelled the ground for political insurgency. The Constitution of 1991 ignored the voices of common people and the left political parties. Lok Raj Baral (2004) compares the Nepali parliamentary system with the British system and concludes that Nepalese political leaders could not perform well for the people. He notes that the political power struggles of leaders among leaders have led to corruption, fostering an environment where corrupt practices can thrive in the pursuit of power. The likelihood of a stable government and its capacity to carry out policies in an environmentally sound manner have been significantly undermined by the power struggle. Instead of having meaningful and long-lasting policy measures, there have been frequent shifts in the rhetoric surrounding policies from one government to the next.29 Political party is a part of every modern society and it functions as a group of people organized to achieve constitutional objectives and defend the sovereignty of the people. In a multicultural country like Nepal, political parties play major roles to integrate the citizens and access them to the services of the governing institutions. Samuel P. Huntington observes, ‘The function of the party is to organize participation, to bring together separate interests and to serve as the link between social forces and government.’30

The CPN (Maoist) adopted the principles of Marxism, Leninism and Maoism as ideological guidelines of the party. After 1990, it regenerated its name from the CPN (Unity Centre) to CPN (Maoism) as its extended Congress decided to adopt ‘Marxism-Leninism-Maoism’ as the ‘ideological direction’ of the party. On 22 April 1949, the CPN(CPN) was established and the party accepted ‘agrarian revolution as the main policy revolution in Nepal’.31 Since the beginning, the CPN had adopted to establish ‘New People’s Republic Political System’ guided by the political doctrine of Mao Zedong—and the CPN (Maoist) followed the same guideline when it initiated a decade-long political movement in 1996. The first pamphlet of the CPN in 1949 acknowledged the necessity of launching an armed struggle as the people of Nepal fought for civil liberty through a revolutionary movement. Thus, it should not be restricted to protest using non-violence alone. The anti-people policies of the Rana oligarchy were boycotted by revolutionary committees for civil liberty in every village, town, district, school, college and factory. The party appealed to declare their laws and regulations, organize protests and meetings, seize control of multiple locations to address the demands of labourers, peasants and other workers.32

Political parties in every developing country grapple with peripheral political policies and when they do, it can foster consensus among them on major public issues. Social security, economy, health, education, transportation, communication, drinking water and delivery of basic public goods must be the parts of the common agenda for political parties. In Nepal, even after the political movement of 1990, establishing a consensus ruling parties was not a common goal. This lack of unity contributed to the rise in support for the radical communist party, which played a significant role in the political conflict of 1996. In this context, De Sales (2000) argues that the Maoists garnered support largely due to economic grievance, socio-political inequality and injustice.

POST-1990 COMMUNIST PARTIES IN NEPAL

There were many challenges before drafting the constitution in 1991 and a clear rift between left and right-wing political parties was observed. Since the political parties had their political demands they wanted to be incorporated into the Constitution of Nepal which pushed the political parties on different political grounds. Thapa and Sijapati (2006) state ‘. . . the left was not too happy about a constitution drawn up by a committee and had instated on an election to a constituent assembly, with the radical [political parties] from the UNPM raising up a chorus in support’.33 The promulgation of the new constitution of Nepal in 1991 had not brought political cheerfulness among the country’s different minority groups and radical communist parties which condemned the declaration of the constitution as their demands went politically unfulfilled. Nickson writes the structural problem of Nepalese politics fuelled the ground for radical communists to increase the political support of common people. He states that this process of political polarization is aided by the failure of the parliamentary system to bring about structural reform and the continued decline in the economic status of urban youth and the rural poor, even without any attempt by the monarchy to reclaim its authority. However, if the latter occurred, the message conveyed by Masal 34 regarding his disdain for the 1991 Constitution and the parliamentary democracy it established would undoubtedly gain new significance. These factors suggest that, in contrast to worldwide trends, Nepal’s medium-term Maoist prospects are far from exhausted.35

The CPN (Maoist) had already begun an open conflict in the rural districts of western Nepal especially in Rolpa and Rukum and according to the party ideology of ‘Marxism-Leninism-Maoism’, it was keen to implement the path of protracted people’s war. At that time, it mobilized the mass and embraced the revolutionary plan for Nepal. It shaped Nepal’s strategy and tactics during the People’s War in 1995—the party reaffirmed its unwavering commitment to theory of people’s war, developed by Mao as universal and unbeatable theory of warfare in China. The commitment was central to their historical initiation of people’s war, adopted by the CPN (Maoist) Central Committee in September 1995. This plan is based on the strategy for encircling the urban area from the countryside, tailored to the unique circumstances of our nation. On the other side, the CPN (United Marxist and Leninist) practiced open politics and participated in the government forum. People’s multiparty democracy was the NC’s political doctrine, and it ultimately agreed to reach a political understanding with the CPN (Maoist) for the peace process. Various political struggles, including those of 1968, the political movement of 1990, and the decade-long movement from 1996 to 2006—especially initiated by the CPN (Maoist) with a forty-point demand presented to the Government of Nepal in 1996—focused on achieving civil liberty through struggle.

The political history of Nepal is marked by various constitutional delays. For the first time, King Mahendra promulgated a new constitution at the beginning of 1959. Then, in 1990, King Birendra accepted constitutional change and it laid the ground for democracy. It accepted the multiparty democracy, with the King serving as Head of State and an executive Prime Minister. It could not endure the political system for a long time and the country experienced a decade long political conflict from 1996. The rise of Communism, even more than the rise of traditional Democratic Party, e.g. NC was the most important political phenomenon of the last decade of the twentieth century and the first decade of the twenty-first century turned out to be a much stronger and long lasting political movement in Nepal as the CPN (Maoist) initiated ‘People’s War’ in 1996. The twelve-point-understanding36 signed by the seven political parties and the Maoists, which preceded the Comprehensive Peace Agreement (CPA) 2006 pledged to uphold democracy and peace for the advancement of the nation and its citizens. The agreement was signed on 21 November 2006 by the Government of Nepal and CPN (Maoist). An interim constitution was also issued. On 20 September 2015, the new constitution of Nepal came into effect with a resounding majority of votes cast by CA members.37 Nepal’s parliamentary democracy faced Maoist’s decade-long political conflict during 1996-2006 and it was followed by the CPA 2006 alarmingly helped increase the supporters of communist groups. Maoist leaders had convinced the people that their demand for CA would offer all sorts of rights to the common people. From the election of the first CA to the present general election, communist parties got the support of rustic as well as urban people. Finally, the country was declared a Federal Democratic Republic, the 240-year-old monarchy was overthrown on 28 May 2008, when the recently elected CA proclaimed Nepal a Federal Democratic Republic.38 As assumed by the CPN (Maoist) before 1996, their leaders were moving ahead to fight for the people and they believed it would bring real political change in Nepal.

LEAP OF COMMUNISTS IN NEPAL

In the elections to the House of Representatives in 1991 and 1994 more than 33 per cent voters opted for the Communist Party. They received more than 60 per cent of the votes in the post-2008 elections. Even after the political movement of 1951 and general elections of 1958 the socio-economic structure of the country remained same and the feudal group was internally resilient. Due to the political coup by King Mahendra in 1961, the public’s perception towards the Communist Party turned positive. In 1963, the NC postponed the political struggle convened by Suvarna Shamser due to national and international political reasons, particularly the 1962 war between Nepal and China which increased the votes of the communists. The communist movement in Jhapa started in 197139 and got the support of Nepalese youths. However, due to the national reconciliation policy adopted by the NC in 1976, the policies of the NC became unattractive among the youths. Even the new constitution adopted in 1990 could not change the social and economic structure of Nepalese society, which fuelled the ground for communism in Nepal.40

The former CPN (Unity Center), which later became the CPN (Maoist), did not accept the constitution of 1990. The party used the first parliamentary election in 1991, based on the 1990 constitution, to subvert the political system. According to Lenin, new stages of revolution emerge from changes in the power balance at the international level.41 In 1996, the Maoist struggle took place at the same reality of Lenin’s understanding of political revolution. In order to get the people’s support for the movement from the stages of the revolution, the United People’s Front had submitted forty-point-demand to the government of Nepal in 1996.42 The leftist political parties in Nepal always focused on the issues of revolution, political guidelines, struggle and transformation of society and connected events like nationality, e.g. the Mahakali Treaty, the killing of the royal family in the palace and the demand of people’s rights. Before the 19-day movement in 2006, seven-political-party and the Maoist agreed to hold the round-table conference, announcement of the Interim Constitution and a new constitution drafted and promulgated by the Constituent Assembly got people’s verdict in 2008 to receive great support from the Nepalese people.43 The proclamation of the new constitution by the Constituent Assembly in 2015 and the 2017 elections made Nepal’s communists a strong political force, but they failed to use the people’s vote.

The history of Nepal’s Communist Parties is factionalism.44 However, they drew the attraction of huge number people in their political organizations and justified that they would be a power centre to change the society.45 Nepal’s peace process was theoretically initiated in 2005 after the signing on the 12-point understanding between the political parties and in Nepalese politics it was a turning point in the history of Nepali politics. Members of civil society, human rights workers and the political leaders of Nepal played a positive role in initiating the dialogue among the parliamentary parties and rebel groups. Even after the initiation of the peace process in Nepal and during the time of both elections of the Constituent Assembly, CPN (Maoist) frequently used it for the new people’s movement to bring to a logical end of bringing political achievement in Nepal through the peace process. In a press statement, the leaders announced that the nation now demands that the armed conflict can be resolved amicably and that a lasting peace be established. Therefore, the party is fully committed to ending the autocratic monarchy, holding elections for the Constituent Assembly and ending the current armed conflict through a progressive political outlet with the establishment of a full-fledged democracy to bring about permanent peace.46

The political agreement made an understanding to integrate the combatants into Nepalese security forces. It was the victory of Maoists that helped justify their political movement as the previous political movements of Nepal that occurred in 1951 and 1990 failed to address the people’s demands. The interesting and unique part of the peace process was the initiation of an open fight between the parliamentary political parties and the Maoists against the monarchical political system of Nepal. After signing the 12-point-agreement, the seven political parties agreed to construct a new political alliance between the seven political parties and the rebel forces that aimed to establish a federal republic country. Ian Martin (2012) acknowledges, ‘The 12-point understanding had included the commitment to international supervision of the two armies during the election.’47 As this, the decade-long political uprising brought some structural changes in legal statutes to mark the socio-economic transformation of Nepal. Earlier, Nepalese intellectuals were the political opponents of Maoists as they had a fear of its political doctrine. Despite that assumption, Maoists were politically admired by many people as they contributed to bringing about many social reforms and changes. In the past, several women, indigenous groups, Madhesi and Dalits were under socio-cultural domination as they felt political marginalization. The Maoists recruited thousands of women in their people’s liberation army and it forced the government to recruit women in Nepalese security forces. Baral (2007) considers that Maoist’s principle based on Marxist, Leninist and Maoist is redefined to practice in Nepalese context. He further said the Maoists were flexible enough to accept a multi-party system and pluralistic society, which made them compatible to cooperate with the other bourgeois parties. In the first general elections of 1959, CPN had bagged only 4 seats out of 109 whereas Communists got 61.5 per cent votes in the first CA of 2008. After the announcement on new Constitution in 2015, all at once, two largest Communist Parties, i.e. CPN (UML) and CPN (Maoist Center) made a political alliance for the election in 2017 and got 63.27 per cent seats in the Federal Parliament. The decade-long insurgency spawned a political climate that brought about changes in legal statutes, marking socio-economic transformation. In the political arena, it summed up the lived experience of progress for marginal groups from a bottom-up approach, leading to a substantial political shift and the establishment of new power relations. The present Constitution of Nepal endorses a commitment to socialism based on democratic norms and values,48 reflecting the current ideological and cultural principles of the Nepalese Communist Party.

COMMUNIST PARTIES AT PRESENT

The new post-conflict constitution has fulfilled the expectations of people, which were earlier overlooked in the 1991 constitution. It has set out an overhaul of the political system, devolving significant power from the centre to the seven provinces. People now perceive this as a political tactic of the Maoists, who ultimately succeeded in forming the largest ruling communist party in Nepal. The new party follows the ideological philosophy of Marxism–Leninism, with its political line centred on‘People’s Democracy’. Their merger to become the Nepal Communist Party has gained political momentum in the country, making it easier to address the people’s agenda. Adhikari (2014) states, ‘The large proportion of Maoist rank and file belonged to historically marginalized caste and ethnic groups’49 and with the support of marginal groups, poor people and women, Maoists (Communist) gained popularity in Nepalese politics.

Democracy is always for the happiness of people and every political movement should be for bringing changes in the country. The two remaining communist parties, CPN (UML) and UCPN (Maoist) formed a Left Political Alliance to contest in the recently concluded elections for Nepal’s parliament and provincial assemblies. They bagged nearly two-thirds of the seats in the 275-member Federal Parliament and formed governments in six out of seven provincial assemblies. The Left Alliance theoretically was an unexpected coalition between Maoist and Marxist-Leninist parties and now the communist movement in Nepal is facing a crisis as they are not in the same position. The constitutional announcement and the general elections of 2017 politically ended an eleven-year transition from monarchy to federal democracy after a brutal civil war between Maoists and the state. The election is the symbol of the country’s socio-political transformation and is an objective of a decade-long conflict. However, the fundamental issue of peace process, i.e. the cases of victims is still unresolved.

After ending the civil war, holding the election of constituent assembly and three levels elections in 2017 and 2022, Nepalese left politics have entered to new phase. Political ideology is shifted to democratic values and norms. The positive development in Nepalese politics has brought sound prospects for strengthening democracy in Nepal. The future course of democratization in Nepal will be measured as a successful tactic if the left political parties uplift the lives of the poor and transform the Nepali economy into a prosperous one. However, the communist parties typically start out with radical and revolutionary rhetoric before gradually transitioning to a social democratic paradigm. Eventually, even the parties that persist in supporting revolutionary socialism will turn into a space for neo-bourgeoisies. The two most powerful moderate communist parties now are UML and Maoist Center, in the realm of legislative politics, these parties are fighting for a position and now they are experiencing an existential dilemma. In Nepali politics, triangle polarization means that there will not be two powerful, moderate communist parties for a very long time. Since one of UML and the Maoist Center will undoubtedly merge. The political coalition between Maoist Center and UML in 2017 was a part of rebirth of communist movement in Nepal and their rift in 2021 was a part of crisis in ideology.

CONCLUSION

Nepalese Communists have achieved significant success in global politics. The promulgation of the Nepalese constitution through CA and inclusion of combatants in security forces legitimized their political agenda of equality, justice, freedom, inclusion and empowerment. By adopting Maoist principles alongside leftist social democratic ideology and transitioning from a decade-long political struggle to the practice of a parliamentary system, they gained widespread popularity at the ballot box. Their experiences from violent war to the peace process, have demonstrated the political struggle of Communists of Nepal in all forms of struggle. This identity of Communists in Nepal remained strong for good reasons. The Maoist Party, an isolated faction of the CPN, was not popular among intellectuals as other political parties of Nepal before 1990. Its regular movement vowed to stand up for the rights of people who were politically voiceless and deprived among others. The Maoist insurgency gradually expanded across the country and finally succeeded in uniting with the next largest communist party is itself a historical move to get the huge support of the Nepalese people. Though, NC had the goal of multiparty democracy, it failed to grasp the support of poor people and stood second in the elections of 2017.

To explore the history of socialism as outlined in the Constitution of Nepal, Nepalese political parties must unite. Now is a time to follow the principles of people’s participation in every sector of citizen’s lives and implement the constitution in practice. From this analysis, the best conclusion is that Nepalese people got many things for the first time in history that should be sustainable to reconcile Nepalese politics in a post-conflict country like Nepal. If the political leaders of Nepal work to lead the country toward economic development, the model of peace process could serve as an asset for other countries that have experienced prolonged political conflict. The rise of Communists in Nepal and their support for communist ideology aim to prevent future conflicts. However, Nepali people have endured a long political transition and Nepalese political parties failed to bring the peace process into a logical conclusion risks plunging the country back into a dark future. The rise of the Communists in Nepal during the 2017 elections positioned them as a mainstream political actor, with the expectation of governing for five years by a single political party. Voting for the communists was perhaps a means for political stability and economic prosperity aligning with the promises made by the Left Alliance UML-Maoists before the elections. However, the current communist movement in Nepal is in crisis and to avoid this political dilemma the Maoist Center and UML are likely to unite.
