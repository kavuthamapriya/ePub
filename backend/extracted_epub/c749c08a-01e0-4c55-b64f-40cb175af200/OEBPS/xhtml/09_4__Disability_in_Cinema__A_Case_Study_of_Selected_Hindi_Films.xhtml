Disability in Cinema
A Case Study of Selected Hindi Films

KURUKHETRA DIP

INTRODUCTION

Cinema is considered one of the most acceptable forms of art in the contemporary era. In cinema or through cinema, we can see what is happening in society and aspire for society through cinematic imagination. The study of films provides opportunities to connect history and technology vis-à-vis social changes and ideological impetus. Over time, cinema production has also gone through several changes due to changes in technological advancement. The relevance of watching traditional theatre is also changing due to the availability of DVDs/CDs and other online streaming mediums for watching movies. In other words, films or cinema is more accessible to general contours than ever before. With time, the study of films and film studies has also changed. Now many traditional disciplines like philosophy, political science, sociology, etc., are studying film/cinema from their disciplinary point of view.

Different disciplinary perspective gives a slightly different reading of a film. For example, ‘Anthropology of Film’ might integrate ethnography into films and read it as ‘seeing culture’ of a particular period or point in time. At the same time, from the ‘sociological lens’, the reading of the same movie might look upon the sociological aspects, i.e., gender, race, community, etc., interpreted in that. For instance, in the film Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge an anthropological argument would be how the Indian diaspora can inculcate their cultural values, whereas from the sociological point of view, how ‘patriarchy and power’ work in modern/advanced society. In the history department, films can be viewed in their historical context. In the mass media and cultural studies departments, perhaps the cousins of sociology in terms of film analysis, the reader is asked to consider social and cultural powers in making a film and the film industry. Further, sociologists can engage in decoding a film and explain how films elicit structure and facilitate the expression of different emotions to which human are subjects to. Reading films with a sociological eye makes us conscious of ourselves as we watch movies. As we are being entertained, we are also introduced to the worldviews and social theories embedded in the films.

Sociological reading of films asks us to look beyond common sense thinking and long-held interpretations. If we take film/cinema as text or literature, it speaks a lot about social issues and turbulences that society experiences. In this context, cinema as literature is appreciable not because of its capacity to tell us a good story but rather how ‘the scriptwriter’ reflects and approaches the social context, social/psychological struggles, etc. One can understand a social class to which we generally do not belong and do not associate our feelings with it, through the vantage point provided in cinematic narratives. Like, a novel’s interpretation lies on the readers; a movie’s readings also depend on its audience. It depends on how the audience decodes it and how it imagines itself with the ‘galaxy of images’ presented in a movie. Further, it also depends on how the audience thinks of movies as comprising visual languages, verbal systems, dialogue, characterization, narrative, and story (Slied: 2001, 3).

In artistic and expressive mediums, i.e., cinema and television1 they use narratives with little tweaks that are already popular in larger imagination. The appearance of disabilities in these imaginations is quite different from physical disability and cognitive disorders. Physical disability appears to be a challenge and tragedy whereas, in the case of cognitive disorder, it appeals in a somewhat different role. Audiences are typically asked to respond in a restricted number of ways, ranging from sympathy or pity to some sort of charitable granting of limited personality to such characters. They avoid complicated characters and adopt a simple loving protagonist that can be understandable, so the show will succeed in masses. Furthermore, these narratives ease the audiences about their idea of ‘normalcy’. In many cases, if the movie’s lead character is a ‘cognitive disorder’ or ‘madness’, i.e., depression, schizophrenia, etc., the protagonist becomes a tragic stand-in for its audiences. In this regard, Davis (32) argued that the line between tragedy and redemption, as well as between tragedy and comedy, seems to be blurred by obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). In one scenario, the person with OCD is transformed into a cute little nut and called a disability ‘mascot’ (ibid.), and this mascotization of disability produces a loveable and cuddly represent-ation of disability.

Similarly, if a side character has a disability and the movie is not about disability, then the viewers may be inattentive because of the disability narrative. In an ableist culture, they would question why the ‘normalcy’ of the movie is being altered. In this view, a disability is an allegory because it must represent something else, i.e., weakness, insecurity, bitterness, fragility, evil, innocence, etc., and be the setting for the moral lesson that people are good and can overcome obstacles, and that we shouldn’t be prejudiced or dejected.

DISABILITY NARRATIVES IN HINDI CINEMA

The majority of people in India misunderstand disability because of a lack of awareness and as a consequence, people with disability live in constant marginalization. Looking into this lack of awareness one can take the help of a representational medium that can provide a powerful platform for the dissemination of knowledge and reach out to the masses in a short period. In this context looking to the outreach of Bollywood or Hindi film industry, one can use it as an effective tool to connect people and sensitize about disability and its related stereotypes. Indian cinema in general and Hindi cinema in particular, in their earlier narratives, adopted a normative approach to ‘fixing’ disability, in which a disabled person is introduced to an able-bodied person who teaches them how to put aside their impairment and leads happy lives. The following paragraphs offer some examples of such.

In early narratives, disability can be seen as a punishment (Jeevan Naiya, 1936; Kashish, 1980; Dhaiwan, 1981). But in Netrikakan (1981) the message was both punishment as well as desexualization of persons with disabilities. Another narrative is that being disabled is a worse punishment than death (Sholay, 1975). Apart from these narrations, disability can also seen as stigma, mockery and restoration of normalcy (Guzaarish, 2010; Koi… Mil Gaya, 2003; Pyare Mohan, 2006; Tom, Dick, and Harry, 2006).

Until the 2000s, the representation of disability in Hindi films was very offensive, and caricatural in nature. Things have changed with the new wave of cinema, the representative narration changed, now it has become sensible and directly engaged with the subject. Even in the new wave cinema, certain films continue the old fashion. The primary objective is to use the platform to increase public sensitivity and awareness of the issue. Additionally, it appears that the way a director views disability has shifted from being something to exploit to being seen from the perspective of the disabled person. This change in the line of thinking can be dated back to 2005 when the nation’s first international film festival for people with disabilities was held by the Chennai-based NGO Ability Foundation. The festival’s goal was to dispel misconceptions about people with disabilities and raise awareness of disability issues through an extraordinary showcase of international films (Indiaglitz). Since then, this festival has been held annually and has attracted several notable producers and directors.

Along with increased sensitivity, mainstream movies about a variety of ailments have recently come out, including Progeria, Alzheimer’s, Dyslexia, Asperger syndrome and Cerebral Palsy. It is encouraging to see that many of these movies are edging closer to presenting disability as an integral part of society. Although some of these films have been criticized for stereotyping, it is still important to appreciate them because the ideas they explore have never even been depicted on film.

AS LANGUAGE

Like silences, discourses are not subservient to or opposed to authority. Discourse communicates and creates power, it maintains it, but sometimes it undermines and exposes it, weakens it, and allows it to be thwarted. Similarly, silence and secrecy provide a haven for authority, cementing its prohibitions, but they also relax its grip and allow for relatively hidden pockets of tolerance. Dialogue is linguistic usage, in whatever form and via whatever means, concerning social, political, and cultural formations; it reflects social order, but it is also language producing social order and defining social life negotiation. Studies from racism2 language in usage can impact people’s lives in both ‘good’ and ‘bad’ ways.

Regularized and ritualized ‘negative’ change has the same power to harm, oppress, and exclude racial and ethnic groups on both personal and institutionalized levels as the practical hurdles to inclusion that people with disabilities encounter in their daily lives. Nowhere is this more evident than in the lives of those who are denied access to the use of language or whose access is limited to the ‘basic’, ‘ordinary’, and ‘daily’ due to discriminatory educational and societal practices.

However, this narrow focus for linguistic oppression analysis may be a red herring in the context of resistance politics given that the production of meaning, cultural value, and patterns of access to discourse are inextricably linked with social relations of domination and subordination. Nevertheless, many sociolinguists concur that persuasive language is necessary to bring about significant social change in the face of oppressive structures of any kind. It needs to interact with the dominant discourses defining the key spheres of its social reproduction to compete in mainstream society. In last, Marx may have missed the fact that ‘to interpret it persuasively is to change it’ when he launched his famous war cry against conventional philosophy (the goal is not ‘to interpret the world differently but to change it’, as repeated by Oliver and Barnes).

DISABILITY AND SOCIAL ATTITUDE

When the elevator of her college stops working one day, Laila is hoisted up the steps in her wheelchair. This act calls into question the ableist culture's perception of staircases as ‘beautiful architectural elements [...] that raise key state buildings’. They are shown in the film as tyrannical and bigoted toward Laila, who has a physical impairment. Stairs signify a ‘commonplace cultural assumption’ in our society, ‘an embedded aspect of the interpretive frameworks sanctioned by culture’ that supports a specific view of the human body and its powers. This understanding of ableism as a practice working at the level of everyday discourse also offers options for us looking for alternatives that contradict ableist normativity, such as the presence of an elevator as an alternative.

Furthermore, scenes featuring Khanum and Laila playing miniature golf and swimming in New York and cricket in Delhi illustrate the film’s ableist discourse, which frequently dictates the underlying social structures of sport. There is no denying that certain sports favour and reward specific abilities or physical capacities. In this regard, Cherney’s (2011) views can help us understand the more nuanced of how societal attitudes help to create more hurdles for persons with disabilities. According to him,

The system of sports shapes expectations of physical capacity by designing games with rules that create spaces where individual performances and abilities appear salient, and it labels those whose bodies prevent them from participating as ‘disabled’ or ‘incapacitated’. (p. 6).

It might be claimed that comprehending the nature of such a system’s activities opens up opportunities for alternate methods of practicing existing sports and new ones. Furthermore, at Laila's college’s rock band competition, the woman handing out the top award to her college team states that it was granted to them because a girl with a handicap wrote the songs. In the film, the woman presenting the award tells Laila,

This competition is a milestone. When I was informed that a disabled musician had written the song’s lyrics, we had to give the award to her college. Your journey must have been different from other normal children. Would you like to share anything with us? (Bose: 2014).

To accommodate the different aspects of ‘persons with disability’, the role of other people’s attitudes towards them contributes a lot. Some of the films take such a stance where some of the other characters play that role to accommodate the difference. One such film is Taare Zameen Par where the story revolves around an eight-year-old Ishaan. He slowly lost his interest in study but was unaware of his dyslexia. Later, when his parents enrolled him in a boarding school, he could not cope with the new challenges thrown at him. However, his life changed when he met a temporary teacher Ram Nikumbh (Amir), a temporary relief teacher for ‘special needs students’. Ishaan can rediscover his self-confidence and overcome an impairment that his parents were unaware of with Nikumbh’s aid and advice. Aamir Khan’s directorial debut film shows Ishaan’s dyslexia sensitively and gives an emotional look at life as a youngster humiliated by an ignorant world. In his family, the dyslexia of Ishaan is unacceptable. When his father is not ready to accept that Ishaan has been suffering from dyslexia for a while, his mother accepts it and plays the sincere caregiver to her son. Later on, his father also accepts when Nikumbh confesses his suffering from dyslexia.

The film’s brilliance rests in Khan’s visual storytelling, which combines animation and live-action to provide viewers with insight into the mind of his young protagonist. His concentration on nuance prevents the picture from devolving into melodrama. He lets his gifted actors do the rest, particularly Safary, who gives an outstanding performance as the damaged but intelligent boy attempting to take his place in the world. Khan, a special-needs sibling, criticizes the Indian school system for producing geniuses while rejecting those it considers to be failures. In addition, the film critiques Indian society for failing these intelligent youngsters by ignoring their latent potential.

Another film, Main Aisa Hi Hoon (Baweja: 2005), shows multiple aspects of the problems faced by persons with disability. In this film, nobody is ready to understand or to say nobody is ready to accept Indraneel, an autistic single parent who is raising his seven-year-old daughter by all means. He works as a waiter in a coffee house, and his life revolves around his daughter and the coffee shop.

DISABILITY AND SEXUALITY

Quoting Merleau-Ponty (1963), Shildrick (2005) implies that sexuality is nothing more than a ‘mode of existence’. Shildrick also refers to Foucault, implying that bodies are equally made and susceptible to perpetual modification rather than fixed (Shildrick, 2005: 116). Based on all of these ideas, every type of disability provides its own unique set of sexual options within specific time-space settings that may or may not restrict other parts of identity performance. Because most sexuality is fundamentally about the interdependence and transmission of bodies, wills, and intentions, as well as an inherent vulnerability in which the boundaries of the Self and the Other are unpredictable, the subject is never settled or limited but intricately interwoven with the other in a dynamic process of becoming. Suppose one looks at the history of cinema carefully. In that case, it shows that many films have been made on the issues about women with disabilities from time to time as one of the most powerful and influential representational mediums for a while. The cinema both underrepresented and often misrepresented the issues regarding women with disabilities. Further, this notion is substantiated by how women with disabilities have been portrayed in the movie Barfi! (Basu: 2012) and Margarita with a Straw (Bose: 2015).

In these movies, the everyday language, attitudes, and actions depicted in films generate and reinforce sexist, ableist norms, resulting in various forms of discrimination and exploitation across specific locations and generations. In Barfi!, when Jhilmil realizes she has affection for Barfi, she attempts to mimic Shruti. She, like Shruti, strives to suck down a whole panipuri.3 Moreover, she tries to drape a sari but cannot do so independently. When the camera concentrates on scrutinizing her waist in the mirror to impress Barfi, her sexual impulses are accentuated. In Barfi!, a scenario in which the potential for sexual exploitation of disabled women in public places is highlighted by Barfi and Jhilmil as they are lying in a car while the man sitting next to them ogles Jhilmil’s bare calves in a voyeuristic manner. Although Barfi's mocking reaction of baring his legs and pointing the man to leer at them challenges the ableist patriarchal order of society’s ‘primordial desire for the pleasurable looking’ and sexual objectification of women, given his role as Jhilmil’s bodyguard, it fails to bring about any significant shifts in the ableist patriarchal order of society (Mulvey: 1975, 9).

Margarita with a Straw dares to deal with the disregarded and demonized component of sexual pleasure in Laila’s life. When she tries to tell her mother about her bisexuality, she displays a frequent hesitation to use the term ‘bisexual’ within ableist language. ‘Aai, I have become a “bi”.’4 She informs her mother after much deliberation and effort. Underlining an interesting wordplay, Laila’s mother misinterprets ‘bi’ as the Hindi term ‘bai’ for ‘servant’ and mockingly says, ‘Even I am a bai’. No matter how well-educated these Hindustani men are, they all refer to their spouses as bai (Bose: 2014). This demonstrates her mother’s ignorance and the danger to heterosexual ableist standards. Under the ableist umbrella of the film, it also carefully ties together LGBT and class problems. Furthermore, this scene is shot in a house courtyard, a woman’s place and an in-between area that, while open, is not a public space.

When Laila tries to confess her bisexuality for the second time, she does it in the confines of a bathtub while trying to bathe her ill mother. The bathroom, where the body is king and where secrecy, privacy, sexuality, and cleanliness are most openly practiced, is expertly employed as a venue that discloses one’s innermost impulses and secret kinds of pleasures. On the other hand, her mother fails to notice and instead repeats the earlier ‘bai’ joke, claiming that Laila has unquestionably become her ‘bai’ now that she has to care for and bathe her cancer-stricken mother. Furthermore, Laila’s slurred voice in the scene appears to show underlying inhibitions about using specific labels to describe sexuality. ‘Not the servant, bai?’ Her mother inquires. ‘What are you on about, bai?’ ‘What exactly does that imply?’ (Bose: 2014). Laila eventually tells her mother who Khanum is and her girlfriend. Her mother's initial response is revulsion, exclaiming, ‘Chiee, this is not natural!’ ‘This is what the world said about me as I grew up, and you fought with them,’ Laila says, reacting to contempt. This is hypocrisy since you are now behaving as if you were one of them’ (Bose: 2014).

As a result, if a person’s image is experienced and represented as a complex and ever-changing combination of physical, mental, and social mechanisms, a more nuanced understanding and tracing of both ableist constructs and mechanisms that question them is required. Both Jhilmil and Laila require an assistant or caretaker to assist them with dressing correctly, negotiating bathroom facilities, requiring physical provisioning and assistance in achieving comfortable sexual positions, and fulfilling other bodily demands. They participate more explicitly in such networks, which might be described as a type of tangential desire or desiring production for transformational possibilities of being and being in the Deleuzian sense (Deleuze & Guattari: 1987).

DISABILITY AND CHALLENGE TO
ABLEIST IDEOLOGY

Although they are scarce, it is vital to call attention to the tangential instances in the films covered here that poke and challenge the ableist discourse’s predominance. For instance, in Barfi!, when Jhilmil repeatedly sings her favourite song in a way that her class disapproves of during a birthday celebration, some of her snobbish society members make fun of her, Jhilmil responds by calling them out. Furthermore, the theme of glass surfaces, mirrors, automobile headlights, and a glass paperweight, which Jhilmil receives from her grandpa, gives imperfect views of objects and unsettles the film’s ableist discourse by altering the audience’s order world view. Jhilmil is frequently shown playing with a glass paperweight that serves as a prism or lens, allowing her to perceive objects or surfaces that do not exist or cannot be seen explicitly.

In Margarita with a Straw as well, the focus on Khanum’s use of wearable accessories and applying makeup, as well as Laila’s use of a swivel spoon and her augmentative alternative communication device, draws attention to the challenges that people with disabilities face when participating in various daily and leisure activities like playing musical instruments, choosing clothing, and using particular types of make-up products, utensils, and cutlery. Both the films (Barfi! and Margarita with a Straw), in some cases, create significant concerns: What is a ‘routine’ day-to-day activity? What activities may be classified as ‘day-to-day’? Who makes the decision? How? Is there a time and space limit for this kind of activity? Both attorneys and victims of handicap prejudice in India are frequently confronted with these complex concerns. When films address it using particular cinematic tactics and fictional tales, they bring these issues to the forefront and into public debate.

In the movie Main Aisa Hi Hoon, Indraneel fights to control his daughter. Because of the inherent apathetic attitude and stigma, Indraneel is about to lose the case, not because he is less capable of caring for his daughter. Instead, the role of the state and those in the various state apparatus and their no-ableist notion of thinking. Though here and there, the movie challenges the ableist notion of society, the end of it somehow establishes the hegemony of the ableist idea. The court granted custody to Indraneel after his advocate Niti married him and produced the marriage certificate in court. One way or the other way, the victory of Indraneel to take custody of his daughter is nothing but the victory of the ableist ideology of society, especially the way the case turns up at the end of the movie.

Nevertheless, there are other two films, i.e., Iqbal (Kukunoor, 2005) and Black (Bhansali, 2005), that challenge the ablest idea of society and bring a new perspective. In the movie Iqbal, Iqbal, a ‘deaf and mute boy who inspired to be a cricket player, faces an acute challenge from both his father and society at large. In his initial phase, the only solace for him is that his mother and sister are die-hard supporters of his cause or dream. He lived up to his dream and finally got selected for the Indian cricket team after defeating every hurdle thrown at him by society. He champions because of the environment created by his sister and coach Mohit, a drunkard but formerly a state champion. Putting the whole concept of the movie into a sentence; if a conducive environment is given to a ‘persons with disability’, s/he can defy the inherent ableist notion of society and can achieve their goal and set an example to society. Another movie, Black, has many facets to it. It is a highly complex movie giving interpretations like child abuse, to mould to live a ‘normal’, and many other facets. However, the movie’s undertone also has the same lines as Iqbal, Taare Zameen Par and Apna Aasmaan. The storyline of Black revolves around Michelle McNally, a deaf and blind girl. Though Hellen Keller inspired the story, the movie itself revealed so many facets of what a ‘person with disability’ goes through in their everyday life.

To conclude this chapter, cinema is one of the essential representational mediums and plays a very crucial role in presenting various social realities on the screen. As cinema in general, and Hindi film in particular has a large audience and a significant global reach, it is essential to examine the nuances of its operation and appreciate its ideological implications and the hegemonic discourse of ableism within which it exists. Responding to cinematic images of specific problems that arise in specific times and locations that influence the way experiences of disability are theorized, experienced, and lived may be more beneficial.
