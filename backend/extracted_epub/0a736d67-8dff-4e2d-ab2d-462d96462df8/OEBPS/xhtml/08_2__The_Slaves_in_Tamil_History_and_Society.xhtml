CHAPTER 2

The Slaves in Tamil History
and Society

Puranaanuru refers to ilipirappalan (man of low birth) the lower classes and servants in Tamil country, in the ancient period.1 Adimai employed by Tiruvalluvar refers only to a servant of low rank.2 The couplet says that if the citizenry turns indolent, it would enslave them to an enemy king. Hence the view of K.K. Pillay who cites this as the existence of slavery is called into question.3 Many Tamil literary texts such as Pattinapaalai, Maduraikanchi, Pathittrupaththu and Manimekhalai refer to women captured after successful battles.4 Tholkappiyam refers to slavery but it was not widespread.5 Slavery had developed gradually as an institution in the early medieval period. Hence the view of V. Kankasabhai that slavery was unknown to the Tamils may also be questioned.6 Slavery existed in the post-sangam period in some form. Men and women offered themselves as slaves to individuals and families. Since the slaves were apparently the people of the lowest status possible in Tamil society; inscriptions mention their sale or transfer deed to identify them precisely as property. The identity of the slave had been defined and specified just as the exact limits of a piece of land donated to the temple.

Numerous inscriptions mention the terms adiyaar and adiyaazhl (serving) referring to the fact of subordination. It signifies exclusively ‘slave’ rather than indicate another meaning of the term that is ‘devotee’. A record from Tiruchirappalli area mentioned a Chandrasekhara arasa mayinthan of Nandivarman mangalam, the village headman who purchased Ooransolai and her daughter and granddaughter Velanpratti and Amainthankandi by name as kuli azhl (wage labourer) in AD 942. They were employed for six years. He decided to sell them in AD 948 and sold all the three of them to the temple of Vayalur to sing hymns in the temple and be kavarippina varkal (flywhisk women) for the deity.7 Again one finds an inscription of South Arcot area in the early eleventh century which states that the mother of a vellalar was given to be an adiyaazhl for the temple deity and that her son was to be responsible for the cultivation of the temple garden that had been donated.8 Thus vellalars were also donated as slaves to temples and they worked there.

The term adimai had also been used exclusively in inscriptions referring to slave. One inscription mentioned and used the term uvachcha adimai (a male drummer).9 Thus adiyaar, adiyaazhl and adimai had been used interchangeably, referring to slaves. These men and women slaves were un-free, employed in the beginning on a large scale in agriculture and also in the houses. They were given shelter to live in the backyards of the houses, fed and given clothes for maintenance. As these slaves could not be liberated at all they were given or resold and came to be attached to the temples.

Slaves were grouped along with the land and cattle as property and this is revealed from an inscription of AD 1150.10 Another inscription mentions the inheritance right of a slave who was a married woman and had no issue.11 A Brahmin of Nangur possessed 15 men and 7 women slaves and he decided to sell all of them as he could not afford to maintain them anymore. The cultivating tenants of the place in AD 1219 purchased 5 men and 5 women, and paid 1,000 kasus as the price. The buyers were allowed to retain the ownership of slaves on a hereditary basis.12

THE SALE OF SLAVES ENGRAVED ON STONES
AND EMPLOYED BY TEMPLES

The temples in Tamil country became owners of slaves mainly in three ways. At first one finds receipt of slaves whom people had gifted to the temples.13 It is mentioned in a record that a Brahmin who owned slaves had sold 2 women and 5 men for 30 kasus, again 15 persons for 2 kasus, and yet again 6 persons for 13 kasus to the temple. The Brahmin also remitted the entire amount to the temple treasury. The sale of slaves was generally conducted through the registration of a deed on a palm leaf in Tamil prepared at the time to avoid problems and complications in future. In an instance, it is reported that the seal of the aranmani (royal palace) was affixed on a palm leaf record by mistake when slaves were sold. A rectification to this effect was later made by stamping the seal of the temple since the sale of the slaves was done to the temple.14

Secondly, in many cases people voluntarily sold themselves as slaves to the temples. It is reported that eight persons sold themselves in Thanjavur area to a temple but the prices are not stated.15 Two women, their dependents and relatives sold themselves to the same temple; while seven of them were sold for 30 kasus, 15 of them were sold for 30 kasus.16 It is found again that six persons were sold to the same temple for 13 kasus.17 Thus the minimum sale price of a slave was around 2 kasus and the maximum was 4 kasus excluding registration charges of the sale deed. The price of slaves had increased in AD 1175. One finds four devaradiyars who were employed were sold to Tiruvalangadu temple for 700 kasus.18 During the famine of AD 1201, many people had voluntarily become slaves and a vellalan (cultivator) sold himself along with his wife and two daughters to escape starvation after borrowing 110 kasus from temple. It is said that 3 nalis paddy was sold for one kasu which was very expensive at that time and so they had sold themselves.19 Marutha Rajayakondan sold his daughter Ramadevi in AD 1425 to the temple of Ariyamangalam near Tiruchendur.20 Five girls of Rajarajapuram enslaved themselves in AD 1448 to god in Appan temple and settled as irandam kudi (second settlers) of Sermaadevi village.21

Third, it is noticed that temples had come to purchase slaves as they needed them for work.22 An inscription in AD 1196-7 gives a list of 100 slaves purchased by the temple of Viratanesvara both males and females, from several places by an order of the king.23 The slaves sold to temples were marked with sulam (trident).24 They were differentiated from the slaves who belonged to the king marked with ilaicinai.25

The slaves in temples were assigned several duties. Some female slaves dehusked paddy.26 Some washed the clothes.27 Some looked after filling the lamps with oil.28 Thus they did divine services in temples. They were given accommodation, food and clothes for maintenance but not given any wages.

SLAVES SOLD AND EMPLOYED
IN MADAMS

Women attached to a madam in the twelfth century were part of a list of people who were bound themselves in service to the temple in perpetuity.29 A record mentions women given as hereditary servants to a madam at Tiruvaikkali in Thanjavur area in the thirteenth century.30 One finds that a kal thachar (stone mason), his wife and their four sons were sold as adimai (slaves) to the madam at Achutha mangalam in Thanjavur area.31 It is reported in an inscription that Vaiyiradha Rayar had a number of slaves who formed part of the dowry of his wife. He sold them with the consent of his wives to a madam.32 Thus it is seen that men and women who were sold or given to madams in perpetual servitude.

SLAVE SALE DEEDS ON PALM LEAVES: SLAVERY
AND THE SOCIAL HISTORICAL PROCESS

Three palm leaf manuscripts describe in detail the condition of slaves during the period 1509-29. They record that one Thiyagraja Mudaliar sold a slave named Rayaan son of Santhosi, of the parayiar caste in the presence of an arbitrator for 10 pons. Ramachandra Nayinar, a resident of Reddi Kadalangudi village sold four of his slaves called Muthan, Vellichan, his wife Parvathy and a male slave to Subramanya Mudaliar of Manganallur for fourteen pons. Subba Padaychi sold his slaves called Raman, his wife Mukkatai and their children to Appachi Mudaliar of Ganja village for 80 pons. These evidences show that the family as a whole entered into slavery in the Thanjavur region where rice cultivation was intensive.33

These deeds recording the sale of slaves were called aazhl-vilai-piramana-isavu-thittu. The sale was done through an auction and the prices were recorded in the presence of an arbitrator. These manuscripts display the signature of slaves, witnesses and that of the writer of the document.34 A standard clause in the sale deeds was that the slaves were to be property of the purchaser as long as the mountain, river Cauvery, the earth and the grass lasted. The sale of paraiyar had increased in this period. In one instance a family of seven paraiyars was sold for eight and a half pagoda in 1593.35 Most of the slaves in the rural areas were owned by the local elite and wealthier members of the Tamil society. However, it is unclear from records whether owning a slave in that period was perceived as a symbol of social status.

Slavery developed because of socio-economic distress, poverty, famines, drought and debt. According to records, the owners of the slaves found in the medieval sale transactions, were mainly landlords. Thus, the most odious form of private property existed in the form of the sale of human beings. A slave was said to be a saleable person who was then regarded as the property of the buyer. The Tamil society had an open system of slavery with an established hierarchy which placed slaves at the bottom of the social order. Thus there is no lacking of indigenous Tamil primary sources to understand the society than to depend on the accounts of foreigners. Untouchability and slavery came in the way of the material well being of the people and also deprived them of civil rights.
