CHAPTER 12

Conclusion

Closing the Curtains

Slavery was a recognized institution in the Tamil country since medieval times. The slaves came to be employed in the temples and with madams and were engaged in many kinds of labour. The institutions, being masters, had unlimited power over the slaves who had been placed at the bottom of the Tamil society. There had been no concrete evidences of manumission of slaves in medieval Tamil country. The causes for slavery show the influence of local practices of social mobility as well as debt bondage in determining the condition. Inequalities such as caste, indebtedness, slave lineage, birth rights and stigmas in addition to conflicts such as warfare and punishment had also been attributed.

The papal bull, ‘Dum Diversas’ issued in 1452 had authorized the Portuguese to conquer and enslave the Saracens, the pagans and other unbelievers inimical to Christ. The Portuguese missionaries in India faithfully applied the principle of religious conversion to slaves. It was believed that once a slave got converted to Christianity there was always the option of freedom. Portuguese traders began to transport slaves from various places to their own trading settlements. The African slaves brought to Goa were called coffrees. It must be said that the Portuguese considered Tamils a race superior to Africans and other Asians. Slaves converted in the Tamil Coast area were given Christian names with surnames which reveal the region or place of origin such as Coromandel, besides Negapatam (Nagapattinam) and Tuticorijin (Tuticorin). Missionary records mention that a large population of domestic slaves had lived in the Portuguese port settlements of the Tamil Coast.

I

The Jesuits on the Tamil Coast noticed the slave trade of the private traders who purchased men, women and children from natives. Since Tamil slavery was mainly domestic in nature, the missionaries reacted sharply when large scale exports from the Tamil Coast to Goa, Cochin and Melaka was carried on. One finds that at this time slavery and slave trade had developed as a theological problem for the missionaries. Time and again the provincial councils of Goa discussed the medieval notion of slavery. It challenged the local enslavement practices which could not be considered for they were unethical for the Christian recognition. The resulting decrees and the constitutions of the Archbishop of Goa became the general guidelines according to which disputes were to be resolved by ecclesiastical authorities. The priests debated the relation between slavery and the sacraments, the limits and rules governing master-slave relations, as well as the relations with non-Christians. The condition of the domestic slaves was reported to be very different from that of rising colonial slaves.

The Portuguese missionary letters and the Danish-Halle missionary reports provide information on the slave trade, slavery, social conditions and indigenous forms of bondage. These details had been found missing in the sources of the East India Company. These missionary descriptions provide information on the pattern of slavery and the system present in Portugal and Germany linking to Catholic and Protestant Christian religious view indicating the need and importance of making efforts to stop slave trading from the Tamil Coast extending throughout the world.

II

Portuguese private traders began slave trading within Goa, Cochin, Melaka and Manila. The Spaniards extended it by transporting Tamil slaves crossing the Pacific linking with colonial Mexico. The Spanish records show the nature, routes, and logistics of these voyages in detail and the importance of private slave traders in expanding the slave supply in the seventeenth century. Finally, both these circuits supplied slaves to meet the Portuguese and Spanish demands around the globe.

In early years of Dutch expansion on the Coromandel Coast between 1621 and 1651, in spite of commercial rivalry and competition with the Portuguese, the Dutch Company successfully purchased and transported plenty of slaves from the Coromandel Coast to Indonesia, Myanmar and Sri Lanka. It mostly happened when people were forced into slavery as a result of impoverishment and starvation after famines broke out. The Dutch transported slaves to Jakarta and also to increase the population in Sri Lanka after a series of conquests. On the other hand, there had been increase in slave trade and transport of enslaved people in groups with many voyages that were primarily fitted out for Dutch Company purposes. Such trade and transport of enslaved people subsequently became widespread in several ports on Coromandel. The flows gradually accumulated to bulk slave trade that grew to full cargo slave trade.

The quantifications found in Dutch records contribute to the growing insight that the slave trading by the Dutch Company was frequent and widespread between Coromandel and Indonesian archipelago. Carving out the patterns of slave trade through quantitative means, one finds local forms of bondage developed in response to global capitalist needs. The Dutch Company documents mention the names of the port, the number of slaves purchased in total for export along with the total amount paid. Since details of such expenditure incurred had been found, the price of each slave on an average could only be calculated though the price of men, women, boys, girls and infants would be varying.

It is gleaned that during the same year, in different months at the same port, the prices of slaves had varied. Further, prices also differed at various ports during the same year. The escalation may be due to various factors such as availability and non-availability of slaves in the market. According to the master list of slaves, the Dutch had given the classification of slaves in Pulicat as Hindus and Muslims.

Long voyages were carefully planned by the Europeans while slave trading to take maximum advantage of the seasonal changes in wind direction, with special reference to the monsoons of the Indian Ocean. The southwest monsoon assisted outward voyages between April and October in the region. Europe bound ships via Cape Town made use of the northeast monsoon during the remainder of the year. The heaviest concentration of sailors was therefore to be found at the Cape of Good Hope between late December and early May. Improvements in ship construction in the interests of greater safety and efficiency took place. Indian teak began to replace oak and copper sheathing to protect it from the ravages of the worm in tropical waters. Crowded slaves and insanitary conditions caused the spread of disease and the medical attention was rough. Fever was common on the ships, respiratory complaints and dysentery was widespread and dietary deficiency led to scurvy. Meanwhile, slaves died unnecessarily and the death rate was often staggeringly high, even on voyages of moderate length. One finds 131 ships in total had sailed between June 1621 to November 1665 and 1,379 slaves had died during the voyage.

The cost of clothing a slave had been also mentioned. The slaves were commonly clothed in dungarees (dongrys), a simple coarse cotton cloth, much like a loincloth. Sometimes it was dyed red with a black stripe running through the middle. Dungarees were also used as sail cloth and for packing material, especially for wrapping textiles. The price of a piece of dungarees fluctuated from as little as florin 0:15:00 to as much as florin 1:10:00. Guinea cloth (guinees lywaet or negros kleden) for slaves has also been mentioned often. This was a coarse, cheap striped or chequered cotton cloth woven from yarn dyed in bright colours and cost around florin 0:11:00 per piece. It was often exported to the Gulf of Guinea, which gave it its name.

The cost of feeding a slave had been recorded. Each slave had a daily ration of 1.13 lbs of rice costing on an average about florin 0:00:07 per pound. The total daily cost of feeding a single slave including rice (kanji), dried fish (karuvadu) sesame oil was about florin 0:05:05. Dutch shipping lists regularly mention gingely or sesame seed oil which the slaves put on their bodies and its cost ranged from florin 0:09:00 to florin 0:16:00 per litre. The food and clothing from time of purchase till the slaves shipped at Devanampattinam in 1635 in total had been mentioned and the expenditure for each slave had been 33.08 florins. The documents also mention the cost of food: 17 parra of rice (714 lbs) incurred in total since slave arrival at Karaikal in 1651. The record also shows that a toll of 1 pagoda (4.04 florins) was paid for each slave in 1652.

III

Large scale export of slaves from various ports of Coromandel to Dutch settlements in Indonesia, Myanmar and Sri Lanka helped and facilitated the fortification works, paddy cultivation and agricultural works that incorporated slave labour. One finds that slaves exported at times found useless by the Dutch Company, were sent back from Syriam and Jaffna to the Coromandel Coast. It is to be noted that long-term slave based systems of agricultural production were perhaps absent in these places. The indigenous slavery in these regions must have merged earlier into a kind of serfdom or household membership. After the arrival of the Dutch in Jakarta slave holding practices were shaped profoundly particularly because the slaves were employed in pepper and nutmeg gardens, animal husbandry and fortification works. Slaves of Pazhaverkadu were employed in the Banda islands where nutmegs were produced in the estates. Chinna Thaai the female slave hailing from Nagapattinam was sold thrice by the Dutch and ended up with the Christian name Rosa and she was employed in 1763 at Groot Constantia’s wine estate located outside the suburbs of Cape Town.

Slaves from Madras were sent by the English Company to St. Helena island for development of sugarcane plantations. Thus one finds that within the established accounts of slavery, there are not many geographic and historical variations which are to be usually acknowledged to European slave trade practices from Tamil Coast. One finds a distinct form of colonial slavery links in tune with the New World and Africa, where European systems were imported wholesale. However this system has been a form of exploitation of slave labour unheard of in medieval Tamil country.

There has been some kind universal method of application of slave labour by the Europeans. One finds European expansion leading to the growth of slave based production of key global commodities, such as pepper and nutmeg by the Dutch in Indonesia, sugar in St. Helena Island by the English, coconut palm cultivation plantations in Manila and Mexico by Spaniards and development of wine estates in South Africa through employment of Tamil slaves. This happened owing to the rising demand for labour that fuelled the long distance slave trade, which in turn stimulated enslavement through warfare, raiding, or the exploitation of local castes or bondage systems and debt.

IV

A striking aspect of the effect of political dynamics on slavery and slave trade happened when the rulers of the Coromandel Coast put up a strong resistance to European slave trading. Krishnappa Nayak the ruler of Gingee when approached by the Dutch for royal permission to undertake slave trade, he vehemently condemned it as a universal crime and shame. Shivaji the ruler banned the Dutch export of slaves totally from the ports of Devanampattinam and Porto Novo in his kingdom. The Nawab of Arcot did not encourage the French in Pondicherry but he was somewhat lenient and said that that no Muslim slaves should be transported from his territory and exported overseas by the French Company. Pratap Singh the Maratha ruler of Thanjavur expressed his grief over Danish slave trade and as a result the governor of Tranquebar prohibited it issuing an order in 1745. The European slave trade in turn, had a massive effect on local economies. Large-scale kidnapping, abductions and enslaving impacted the Tamil society deeply. Inhuman activities and slave trade by native agents and brokers were condemned. The king imposed severe punishments for those who indulged in the slave dealing and profited from the commercial transaction.

V

Christianity speaks of ethics where slaves have to be manumitted after a period of time as stipulated in the Old Testament (Exodus 21:2). The Tamil slaves transported to Sri Lanka and employed in the houses of the Europeans, came to be manumitted. A legal way of freeing a Tamil slave thus developed under the Dutch, but the transition to full freedom was generally very slow and the situation could be reverted if the ex-slave failed to show gratitude or to be helpful to the former master when required. In Dutch records one finds that according to the custom a master manumitted his or her slave in Sri Lanka, hailing from Tuticorin by declaring that he/she would be free only after his/her (the master) death. It was not a very welcome change of status for old slaves who could then hardly find a new buyer or master.

Manumission practice required almost always that the slave handed over a part of his value in cash to the former master. This was almost a kind of self-sale that permitted the master to buy a new slave. Manumissions without such value exchange were exceptional. These were motivated by pious intentions of the masters of gaining merits before God. Also such cases were often delayed till the slave was old and was of little use in terms of labour that could be exacted from him or her. In reality, manumission did not always correspond to the good intentions and legislation.

Manumission had been found conditional at times, requiring service during the life time of the owners and at times the condition of service could be required from the children of the manumitted slaves. But there had been cases in which the masters helped the manumitted slaves to be able to continue working to gain their livelihood. Though in rare cases, some masters gave a house and a piece of land. While emancipation deeds of slaves in Sri Lanka provide a wealth of information on name, regional origin of slave mentioning the name of the ports like Nagapattinam and Tuticorin, the gender, age and circumstances leading to emancipation, what happened once freedom was given to emancipated slaves and their families, is not recorded.

VI

The tendency of the slaves to run away was found common. The legislation took measures to block any assistance to such runaway slaves. There were even extradition laws to guarantee return of the slaves who crossed the borders of the country like that of Francisco the slave of Pondicherry found in France. Again the journey of Samuel Johannes the slave of Tuticorin to The Netherlands, his escape in Germany and finally travelling to Pennsylvania in British North America, point to the impact of the expanding long-distance slave trade and also on the local conditions and the strategies employed by slave owners.

The presence of Europeans on the Tamil Coast had led to an integration and intensification of slave trade systems throughout the continents of Asia, Africa, Europe and America. The chapters in the book had helped to increase one’s understanding of the mechanisms of the systems of slavery, modes of enslavement and trade. In doing so, it accentuates the local contexts of bondage and how they were folded into transregional or transoceanic networks. The study of the specific routes of entry and exit into and from slavery, sheds additional light on the ways that different forms of slavery and bondage were interconnected and interacting.

VII

A slow end of European slave trading came about when the flow of supplies to the slave markets dried up with time. There was the emergence of a market for land and territorial conquest where the self-sufficiency of the labourers was a fact. The Anglo-French rivalry in the Carnatic region led to recruitment of African soldiers and brought to Fort St. David at Cuddalore besides maintaining native armies of sepoys. Artisans and skilled labour besides coolie (wage) labour became the need of the hour and so migration came to develop on these lines. Hence the French signed contracts and the labour migration spread from Pondicherry to Reunion Island between 1841 and 1883. Indenture labour migration developed from Pondicherry and Karaikal to French Guiana in South America between 1854 and 1872, to Martinique Islands in the Caribbean between 1854 and 1879, to Guadeloupe Island in the West Indies to be employed in sugar plantations between 1851 and 1890. Coolies were sent from Madras to Mauritius between 1842 and 1880. Wage labour migration from Tamil country to work in the coffee plantations developed under the British between 1828 and 1883. Here one finds return voyages of the coolies back to Tamil motherland in the nineteenth century. However, all these new developments happened by replacing the earlier slave mode of production introduced by the Europeans in the overseas. Enslavement and slave trade happened during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries and it preceded migration of indentured labour on the Tamil Coast. Slave trade could not expand, spread and progress with the great movement of emancipation of slaves.
