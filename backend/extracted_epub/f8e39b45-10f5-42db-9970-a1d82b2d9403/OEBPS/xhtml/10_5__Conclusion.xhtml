CHAPTER 5

Conclusion

The facts, narratives, and debates in the preceding chapters of the book have successfully established that Ambedkar has been the victim of reductionism, erasure, and stigmatization. We have proved this based on an analysis of historical, sociological, and literary books and texts written by journalists. The erasure of Ambedkar from the creative field, like the Richard Attenborough directed epic film Gandhi, and the intrusion of Gandhi in the Jabbar Patel directed film Ambedkar further prove the point of reductionism of Ambedkar. However, we are aware that the empirical data for the aforementioned discourse may not look exhaustive, but it is just enough to put the point in perspective.

The available empirical data has helped us to highlight the fact that academia, intelligentsia, and media persons have contributed their bit to the aforementioned processes surrounding Ambedkar. A point to be noted here is that Ambedkar has been taken here only as a representative case to understand the discourse on how icons belonging to marginalized sections of Indian society have been victims of the same processes as Ambedkar. If that is true, then the book suggests that we should engage ourselves in analysis of the impact of such processes surrounding the marginalized sections of Indian society.

Against the processes of reductionism, erasure, and stigmatization, the book explores how Babasaheb’s academic works have been received and acknowledged by international institutions and scholars across the globe. The book has probed how, on the one hand, the foreigners have engaged with Babasaheb Ambedkar’s writings on several issues—social, political, religious, constitutional, democracy, and Social Justice, to name just a few. We can see that there is a string of Western social and political scientists like— Owen M. Lynch, Eleanor Zelliot, Gail Omvedt, John C.B. Webster, Simon R. Charsley, Christopher S. Queen, Maren Bellwinkel Schempp (see Ram 2008), Barbara R Joshi, Christopher Jaffrelot, Perry Anderson, and Cosimo Zene, to name just a few, who have engaged with his theoretical, philosophical formulations and social movements from a universalistic perspective.

On the other hand, we have also observed how Ambedkar’s busts have been installed in various universities in the UK, USA, Canada, Australia, Japan, and Vietnam, which proves the point that his life and mission are being celebrated in those countries. Memorial lectures instituted in his name in various foreign universities substantiate the aforesaid point further. If there is any doubt regarding the acceptance of his greatness, then the description of his persona in the citation letter of his L.L.D. degree ends the debate. In this citation, he is addressed as ‘a framer of the constitution . . . a great social reformer and valiant upholder of human rights.’ In the same vein, Anderson (2012: 56) praised him by treating him ‘intellectually head and shoulder above most of the Congress leaders’. On the intellectual plane, Anderson eulogized his book Pakistan or Partition of India in no uncertain terms by emphasizing the fact that the book, ‘. . . stands as a devastating indictment of intellectual poverty of Congress and its leaders’ (2012: 89).

Jaffrelot (2005) treats Ambedkar as a sociologist who ‘confronted head on Western authors at the age of twenty-five’. Jaffrelot, who was interested in Ambedkar’s sociological thoughts, was introduced to Ambedkar by another fellow French anthropologist, Oliver Herrenschmidt, who was in Paris. In his acknowledgments, Jaffrelot accepted that ‘I gradually discovered that he (Ambedkar) played a pioneering role in the sociology of India’. However, he felt that ‘Dr. Ambedkar remained one of modern India’s most neglected public figures’. That is why he emphasizes that ‘I decided to write this book, first of all, to do justice to a then marginalized key personality of Indian History’. Jaffrelot also accepts that while writing his book, he benefited from the erudite insights of American historian Zelliot (see Zelliot 2001) and American anthropologist Lynch (see Lynch 1974) on Ambedkar. These examples are enough to prove the point that how Western scholars have accepted Ambedkar’s academic depth and strength to understand different types of particularistic and universalistic facts.

Apart from highlighting the paradox surrounding Ambedkar’s persona and his production of knowledge, the discourse in the book also underlines the proper framework to understand the functioning of several institutions in Indian society. For example, if one wants to understand the caste system of India, one cannot understand it, according to Ambedkar, as an isolated and independent unit. Ambedkar’s scheme is unique. It begins with the individual’s place in the Hindu social order. Then, he analyses it and declares that it is not a free social order as it makes the individual dependent on his varna. Then, he defines the origin and nature of varna with the help of both Rigveda and Yajurveda (White Yajurveda and Black Yajurveda). Further, Ambedkar, with the help of his unique anthropological and sociological methodology, defines the origin of caste. The discourse on caste is his original theory which includes the imposition of endogamy over exogamy and the propositions of ‘surplus man’ and ‘surplus women’.

A related corollary to this discourse is the relationship between caste and subjugation of Hindu women. Ambedkar opines the emergence of practices like Sati, girl marriage, and enforced widowhood was the result of the maintenance of the caste system (Ambedkar 1979: 13). In a way, Ambedkar proves the point that patriarchy and caste are intertwined. Lastly, Ambedkar’s proposition of the spread of castes across Indian society is also innovative. He uses Tarde’s law of imitation for the spread of the caste across Indian society, which is very different in comparison to mainstream sociological theories that hold Manu and Manusmriti responsible for the spread of caste in India, specifically, Manu’s varnasankara theory.

After this, logically, the discourse on the caste system should have included the emergence of Shudra and untouchables as well. However, we have not included that here because the focus of the book is different. Finally, we should also include the Annihilation of Caste in this framework of understanding the caste system in India. But we should understand that the Annihilation of the caste system should not be treated as an isolated exercise and end. Also, reading Annihilation of Caste does not mean one has understood Ambedkar and his philosophy in totality. I am underlining this point here because I have observed that many students, researchers, faculty members, journalists, and even bureaucrats boast that they have read and understood Ambedkar if they have read Annihilation of Caste.

The book, then, relates to the discourse of Hindu social order with ‘Social Justice’. It is pertinent to engage with the concept of social justice because Ambedkar has not given any specific theory of social justice in the caste-ridden society. Yet, drawing an analogy with Plato and John Rawls, we can find several tenets of social justice in the writings and speeches of Ambedkar, which were published posthumously by the then Government of Maharashtra. In this context, Ambedkar has highlighted the unique features of the Hindu social order that no Indian or Western sociologists of repute have identified and analysed. To disseminate social justice in society, Ambedkar opined that society must be based on equality, liberty, and fraternity. For him, inequality in the Hindu social order is not simple; rather, it is graded inequality. Again, this is a unique formulation that no sociologists have discovered.

Discourse on the nation and the notion of nation building, according to Ambedkar, is also related to establishing equality, liberty, and fraternity. The trinity cannot be divorced from each other, he adds. However, Ambedkar asserts that he has not drawn this proposition from the ‘French Revolution’ rather, he has borrowed it from Buddhism. It is so because, according to Ambedkar, in Buddhist formulation, ‘fraternity’ assumes prominence over equality and liberty. We can make a law for liberty and equality; however, we cannot make a law for ‘fraternity’, he asserts. Establishing fraternity is a very critical characteristic of nation building. The annihilation of the caste system reappears again in Babasaheb’s scheme of nation building. He emphasized that a society divided into thousands of castes cannot be a nation. castes breed animosity and jealousy among the people. Hence, Ambedkar argued for nation building, we must mitigate social divisions among Hindus and Dalits, Hindus and tribals, and Hindus and Muslims. We have observed that Ambedkar’s theory of nation building is reconciliation between Mauss’s ‘Primordialist Theory’ and Gelnner’s ‘Institutional Theory’ (Jafferlot 2002). For nation building, Ambedkar not only demanded the establishment of equality, liberty, and fraternity via annihilating caste differences among the different social communities, but he also asked to establish the rights of every marginalized section of society, especially the Dalits, Tribals, Other Backward Castes, Minorities, and women.

By now, we are aware that Babasaheb was not in favour of the abrupt Partition of the country. Can it not be considered that it was because of nation building? He had a different scheme to solve the Hindu-Muslim dispute by creating two federations—Hindustan and Pakistan, under one strong central government. He wanted that these federations should be given at least ten years to function. Babasaheb believed that after governing their affairs for a decade, Hindus and Muslims would realize that a united India would be better for both. They will develop confidence in each other. We are aware that in his book Pakistan or Partition of India, he uses the definition of Renan, and argues that race, language, and country do not suffice to build a nation.

Ambedkar defined the nation as a ‘living soul and a spiritual principle’. The nation, like the individual, is the outcome of a long past of efforts, sacrifices, and devotion. That is why Anderson (2012: 89) acknowledged Ambedkar’s vision in the following words,

Ambedkar alone was early on clear-sighted. . . . In 1944, at a time when the very idea of it was taboo in Congress and still more than a vague slogan in the Muslim League itself, he published the only serious work on the issue that would determine the outcome of the struggle for independence. . . . Critical to Muslim introversion, alert to Savarkar’s Hindutva, contemptuous to the myth of pan-religious amity, Ambedkar did not advocate separation of two communities.

However, Ambedkar’s profound wisdom and referential knowledge have been erased in the annals of Indian academics.

The discourse in the book surrounding Ambedkar’s larger concerns in the production of his knowledge, like Hindu social order, social justice, democracy, or nation building, rather than restricted to the analysis of caste only, has underlined another fact, along with many others. That is reductionism and contraction of the thought processes of Dalits to the analysis of the caste system in academics. We have observed that the Dalit academicians have restricted themselves, to a large extent, in the university system in teaching, debating, and researching the caste system since Independence. However, we have not seen any significant progress in reducing its impact and intensity; rather, caste has changed its nature and the catchment area and has become more subtle and sophisticated.

Parallel to the aforesaid caste discourse in which Dalits are ‘trapped’, the so-called upper castes have incorporated the varna/ caste system in academics without any criticality and reflexivity (Kumar 2016). It seems that they have done so to maintain the status quo. Instead of engaging critically with Rigveda, Purush Sukta, the tenth mandala theory of caste, they have accepted it, as it is in toto. Further, to strengthen its structure, they have adopted a functionalist model and added concepts like jajmani and Sanskritization to the caste system, as well. In this way, the caste has been given sanctity and legitimacy in academics. A natural corollary to this development is that through this status quoist engagement of caste, the so-called upper-castes have maintained their caste superiority and hegemony in society. If that is true, then how can we annihilate caste or bring substantial change in the caste system and caste-ridden society where people do not have ‘Consciousness of Kind’, but caste consciousness? (Ambedkar 1979).

To undermine the legitimacy extended to caste by the so-called upper caste and to challenge their superiority and hegemony in academia and, thereby, in society, Dalits must come out of the ghetto and rut of caste studies. Dalits will have to herald a new chapter in the academic arena for their emancipation and, thereby, the emancipation of the whole society.
