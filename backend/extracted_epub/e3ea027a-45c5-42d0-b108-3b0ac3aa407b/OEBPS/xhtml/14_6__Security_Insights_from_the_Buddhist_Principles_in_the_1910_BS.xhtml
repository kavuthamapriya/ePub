CHAPTER 6

Security Insights from the Buddhist
Principles in the 1910 BS
(1854 AD) Muluki Ain*

SHIVA PRASAD PAUDEL

HINDU SCRIPTURES WERE THE FUNDAMENTAL
SOURCE OF STATE LAW

In ancient Nepal, when it was ruled by Kirats, the Mundhum, a religious text of Kiratis, was believed to have ruled the country. (Wasti(1) 5). However, there was not adequate evidence of whether Mundhum was a normative standard or in practice. Some historians argue that Mundhum was the basis of day to day ruling of the society. Mundhum has a chapter of Kharom Thim, which deals with the system of justice (Wasti(1) 20).

In the time of Lichhavis, there was no reference to one law or code. The existing historical evidence are inscriptions alone. Most of them underline the establishment of Guthi (endowments of land) for the operation of Vihars and the performance of religious rites. These Guthis were kept for conducting pujas of Hindu and Buddhist Temples and for maintenance and operation of Hindu and Buddhist Temples and Buddhist Sangha and Vihars (monasteries).1

* This paper was presented at a seminar of academics and students at Lumbini Buddhist University on 10-11 March 2024 at Lumbini, Nepal.

In medieval Nepal, Jayasthiti Malla enacted a code, called Manav Nyayashastra. This Nayashastra is based on the Hindu belief system. (Wasti 21). It has referred to Hindu rituals, such as yagya (fire rituals), dana (donation) and the Hindu Varna system. It has quoted Narad Rishi and claimed that the code consists of dharmas propounded by Dharmadev. (Manav Nyayashastra, Wasti(2) 270). However, the account of the transaction, etc. also reflects that some of its provisions on property management and marriage were the insertion of the local customs and practices rather than the direct instructions of Hindu scriptures (dharmasastras). Balaram Kayastha has noted that Jayasthiti Malla cannot be entirely accused of destroying Buddhism in the valley. He was a devout Hindu practitioner but respectful of other religions. The restoration of Swyambhunath Chaitya after the attack of Shamsuddin was carried out in his time (Kayastha 141).

The rulers of Gorkha were also Hindus and they also adhere to the Varna system. The Thitis (a kind of royal decree) issued by Ram Shah mentions the enforcement of the Varna system (Muluki Ain 1910 BS: Annex 1). The appointment of Brahmins as Rajgurus, provision of danas (gifts) to Brahmins; and prohibition of capital punishment to Brahmins were specifically reflected in his Thitis. Prithvi Narayan Shah spent most of his life conquering other kingdoms, his ideas and values are reflected in his divine advice (Divya Upadesha). His Divya Upadesha reflects that he was an ardent Hindu devotee and he also instructed to keep a pundit/priest in every court to dispense justice as per the Nyayashastra/scripture). His advice clearly instructs the rulers and citizens to follow the traditions and religion (Kula Dharma) of their forefathers (Divya Upadesha, Wasti1 76, 81).

Rana Bahadur Shah’s sawal of 1863 BS reinforces the caste system, forbids the killing of cows (except Chauri, i.e. mount Ain cow/ Yak/Nak) and marriage against the established social practice (nachalyakorit). The sawal (a royal decree) of 1863 prohibited to destruction of the rest house of travellers (pati-pauwa), the religious site (dewal ) and the footbridge (sanghu) (Rana Bahadur Shah ko Bandobasta, Wasti1 76). Another piece of legislation before the Ain of 1910 BS was the Bandej (a typical form of administrative law) of Ujir Singh Thapa (Governor of Palpa Gauda/area), dated 1879 BS. In this Bandej, he had specifically mentioned that if someone unintentionally does an unlawful sexual act, which takes away his or her caste, s/he should be pardoned as per the scriptures (dharmashastra). (Fourth Bandej, nos. 17 and 20, Wasti(1) 105). This means that he is referring to the Hindu dharmashastra which provisions for the Varna system. However, his bandej also clearly mentions that adhering to the customs is not punishable, such as a marriage between cousins through maternal uncle and paternal aunt.2 (Fourth Bandej no. 19, Wasti(1)).

It is clear from the royal decree issued by Ram Shah, a royal order of Prithvi Narayan Shah and the administrative order issued by Ujir Singh Thapa that customary law was made part of the Nepalese legal order. However, a detailed study is yet to be carried out to what extent the provisions of Hindu scriptures were modified and customary laws got a greater space into the Nepalese legal system. Making an exception of killing cauri (Yak) from the offense of killing cows is a good example of such modification.

THE (MULUKI ) AIN OF 1910 BS: THE FIRST
COMPREHENSIVE CODE BASED ON HINDU
SCRIPTURES AND CUSTOMARY PRACTICES

The (Muluki) Ain or the National Code, 1854,3 (hereinafter the Ain) ruled the country until it was replaced by the new Muluki Ain of 2020 BS (1963 AD).4 The Prime Minister Jung Bahadur Kunwar Rana was the main initiator behind this codification (Wasti

25) (Khatiwoda 3). Wasti has named Lokpati Jha and Leshpati Jha as drafters of the Ain (Wasti 25). The Ain was discussed in the council of 219 office-bearers and was issued with their names printed in the preamble. The e-copy published by the Nepal Law Commission has included three royal orders issued by Shree Panch Prithvi Bir Bikram Shah Dev. The second royal order of 1943 BS records a major revision of the Ain in the same year. This rewriting was not only made to remove the repetitions but also to include well-established practices and customs into law.5 This order also allows the prime minister and the council to make changes in the law as per the customs and practices. Earlier to this date, two royal orders of 1910 BS and 1942 BS did not have any content to allow the prime minister and the council to accommodate the customs and practice into the law.

The Ain had regulated civil matters such as property, cash transactions, wages, adoption and marriage; criminal matters- such as gambling, witchcraft, rape, sexual intercourse, incest, murder and maintenance of caste-based-order; and Dharmadhikar, court procedure and evidential matters.

The preamble emphasizes the equal application of the law among people of the same caste, while formally accepting different treatment of individuals belonging to different castes as a legal principle. Thus, the Nepalese polity until 1963 was accused of ruling based on the Hindu belief system,6 which reinforces caste-based social order, non-killing of Brahmins, cows and women, etc. Despite the claim, the Ain fails to prescribe the comprehensive list of four Varnas and the six castes and their subgroups. Khatiwoda et al. have counted that the Ain has referred to sixty-three castes besides the castes within the Newar community (around twenty-six castes within Newar alone) and approximately seven ascetic groups (Khatiwoda 29). There could be two reasons for this anomaly. The first one is that the Varna system was developed in the southern plains long ago and they were ignorant of the diversity between people that existed in the northern Himalayan range and other far-off places. The second reason could be that over time practices of drinking alcohol and eating meat developed even within some communities, including Khasa, which traditionally fall either in Brahmin or Chhetri communities (Khatiwoda 30).

Interestingly, the four classifications of people among Hindu Scriptures—Brahmin, Kshetriya or Chhetris, Vaishya and Sudra, are not found in the Ain. The primary classification made by the Ain is—tagadhari (sacred thread-wearers), matawali (alcohol-drinkers but touchable and perhaps water-acceptable), and (fully) untouchable caste. Muslims and Christians were put under touchable but not water-acceptable castes. The Ain has used the term ‘European’ at a place and ‘mleccha’ at a couple of other places, which seems to mean Christians only.7 Except for one provision, which shall be discussed in another section, other provisions where Muslims, Christians and Europeans are mentioned for offenses, such as illicit sexual intercourse; protection from violence such as beating, being sold by the husband and goods that can be touched by Muslims and Christians.

The Ain is not entirely the reinforcement of Hindu practices. It has also given some space, though it looks contradictory to the modern belief system and customary practices. For example, the Ain has given the freedom to people to choose their livelihood as a cobbler, tailors and other forms of labour work.8 From Hindu Dharmashastra’s point of view, such acts should be prohibited for upper castes such as Brahmins and Chhetris.

SPECIFIC PROVISIONS ON THE DHARMIC PRACTICES
OF NON-HINDU TRADITIONS AND ASCETICS
WITHIN HINDU TRADITION

The Ain has a few provisions that can be interpreted to allow the customary practices of non-Hindu religions. However, the Ain are careful in preventing Hindu ascetics or non-householders from expanding their constituency.

In the chapter of Dharmadhikar, it is very affirmatively stated that all four castes including Muslims and Europeans can ‘perform any act that is in accordance with their family traditions and beliefs and leads to Dharma’ except slaughtering the cow.9 The Ain criminalizes an act of disturbance on the performance of such a dharmic act. This provision is very broad to provide everyone including Christians and Muslims to carry out tasks according to their family tradition.

There is only one direct Buddhist reference in the Ain, which does not read encouraging. Section 35 of the chapter on Dharmadhikar reads as follows:

When hearing the mantra (i.e., during the initiation), the members of Sacred Thread-wearing castes, including Brahmins and those belonging to Alcohol-drinking castes for whom it is customary to receive the initiation, shall hear and recite the mantra given by their guru according to their own tradition as followed by their fathers and forefathers (purkha). If they do not recite the mantra according to their own tradition as followed by their fathers and forefathers and follow the Buddhist path or any [other] atheistic jhanna-panna doctrine 411, a fine of 50 rupees shall be imposed on them. If they have themselves consumed cooked rice from a follower of the jhanna-panna doctrine and if they have also contaminated others [by feeding them cooked rice], their share of property shall, in accordance with the Ain, be confiscated. Their sacred thread shall be removed and they shall be degraded from their caste status.10

The above section can be divided into three parts. The first part promotes the continuation of the practice of receiving initiation and hearing and reciting mantras, which was carried out by their fathers and forefathers as a tradition. The emphasis was on the continuation of the same tradition without any alteration. Hence, the importance is given to the exact mantras given by the guru or the master. This practice is not only limited to the sacred thread-wearer but also to all the members where such tradition existed. The second part is applicable in a condition where a Hindu refuses to recite the mantra and converts to Buddhism or any other atheistic religion. In such case, one shall be imposed a fine. In the third part, one must lose property and caste, if one becomes a follower of the jhanna-panna doctrine together with eating the rice cooked by other followers and feeding the same to others.

Another provision of the Ain also prohibits a sacred-thread-wearer householder from hearing mantras11 primarily from the non-householder person or Fakir, which includes Sannyasi, Vairagi, Nanaka, Kanaphatta, Jogi, Jangam or Sebada. If a householder hears the mantra from these Fakirs or accepts them as their guru, he shall be fined (The chapter on Dharmadhikar, section 36). Likewise, these eight and other robe-wearers are prohibited to convert anyone with force or lure. Only persons above sixteen (including widows) were allowed to be converted into these practices if they wished. [The chapter on conversion to ascetics (Fakirsita Mudinya)]. The Ain notes that there were three kinds of Fakirs—one is the wanderer without anything, the second has the religious institution and the third one has the house and property. The Ain has prescribed separate punishment for these three types of Fakirs for making conversion [The chapter on conversion to ascetics (Fakirsita Mudinya), section 1]. In the context of death-related purification (Aasouch Barne), the Ain accepts the presence of Brahmacarin (celibate) in the then society but does not deal with it any further.

There are several references to above mentioned eight groups of Sannyasi, etc. and many other robe-wearers in the Ain but none of these provisions indicate that these renouncers were from the Buddhist faith. Such unclear provisions can be interpreted both in favour or against the Buddhist practitioner.

In the wake of having no provision in the Ain prohibiting customary religious practice, it can be stated that the Buddhist population or any other population can continue with their customary practices. However, if the Buddhist practice requires to carry out conversion of laity or people from other religions to the Buddhist path, this seems to have been prohibited by the Ain. The scheme of prohibition to conversion is not only applied to Buddhists but also to the other ascetic paths that traditionally fall under Hindus as well.

However, it is reasonable to accept that the Ain does not deal with the Buddhist Bajracaryas and the rest of the Buddhist Newars (Khatiwada, 33). It is also reasonable to state that the Ain neither wished to highlight the Buddhist practices of Kathmandu valley and elsewhere by putting a separate chapter nor risked forbidding these century-long practices.

The administrative power of the Buddhist Sangha has not been recognized by the State since the time of Manav Nyayashastra. It cannot be said that the administrative power of Sangha mentioned in the inscription of Pashupatinath-Vajraghar was an exception or a mainstream practice.

ESTABLISHMENT OF GUTHI AND CHARITABLE
ENDOWMENTS: A UNIQUE AND ESSENTIAL FEATURE
OF NEPALESE RELIGIOUS PRACTICE

The establishment and maintenance of Guthi have been an important feature of the promotion of religious and cultural practices in Nepal. It is well known that the Lichhavis had kept several Guthis for the pujas of temples and maintenance of Sangha and Vihars.12 Mostly in the times of Mallas, the inscription suggests the maintenance and restoration of the Buddhist and Hindu Temples and donation to public works, such as making wells and rest-house for travellers (pati-pauwas).13

The Ain has promoted the establishment of Guthi on Nepali soil and forbids establishment of Guthi in Indian religious places, namely, Kashi and Kedar. It provides two factual accounts of the confiscation of a Royal Guthi established by Rana Bahadur Shah and the denial of protection of the right to land and Shivalaya and a Dharmashala built in it by a former prime minister by the British India Government (The chapter on Datta Guthi, section 1). The Ain presents that Nepal has significant religious sites compared to India. It acclaims the high religious purity of Nepal by describing it as the only Hindu Kingdom in the Kali era. It praises the piousness of the country having Jyotirmaya Shree Pashupatinath and Shree Guheshwori and proclaims Nepal as a sacred place (punya bhumi). It clearly states the protection of Royal Guthi established from the time of Dravya Shah but does not refer to the protection of Guthi established before the Shah period. (The chapter on Datta Guthi, section 5). The Ain fails to recognize the historic contribution made by the Kings of Kathmandu Valley and other parts of Nepal in ancient and medieval times. Likewise, the Ain also does not admire the religious sanctity of Buddhist chaityas or shrines. Khatiwoda et al. have argued that ‘(t)he legal recognition of Guthi land can even be read as the integration of different (divine) forms of spatiality embedded in the state space’ (Khatiwoda 27), however, there is no specific reference to Buddhist stupas, chaityas that existed in the Himalayan, Hill and Terai region. Even if you believe that the rulers had a deep respect for Buddhist deities, that is not reflected in the black letter of the Ain.

Nonetheless, several provisions in the Ain protect the Guthi that were established from the time of fathers, forefathers and ancestors (The chapter on Datta Guthi, sections. 6-27).

The Ain promotes Guthi both for temples and other public works, etc., and charitable endowment (sadabarta) for paupers and Fakirs, etc. (the chapter on Datta Guthi, section 2). Khatiwoda et al. summarize the provisions in the Ain that highlight mainly three primary purposes for establishing Guthi and charitable endowment: religious and ritual purposes, such as the maintenance of Shiva temples, pilgrim’s refuges (Dharamsala) and the performance of compulsory, occasional or annul worship of deities (nitya- and naimittika-puja) (the chapter on Datta Guthi, section 1); welfare purposes, such as the daily distribution of meals to orphans, elderly people without family or disabled persons [the chapter on Poor (pauper), section 6] or the arrangement of funeral ceremonies (the chapter on Funerals, section 5); and, finally, the maintenance of public facilities, such as wayside shelters, fountains, bridges, ghatas, wells, ponds, paths, resting places or gardens (the chapter on Making Houses, section 9) (Khatiwoda 51).

The Ain documents the Nepalese tradition of establishing Guthi being carried out by Kings and Citizens from ancient times. However, there seems to be a difference in the trend. In the time of Lichhavis and Mallas, the Guthis were established for the performance of puja, maintenance of the temple and Buddhist Sangha. However, the Ain promotes charitable endowments for paupers and mendicants.

The Act has not failed to prescribe the protective measures to upkeep the Guthi as well as the punishment for harming Guthi. It prescribes that if kings and ministers confiscate Guthi or don’t give a replacement of Guthi land when confiscated, they will accumulate demerits (paap) and open the door to hell (The chapter on Datta Guthi, sections 1 and 25). The Ain established a separate administrative body (the Guthi Janch Adda) and a special court (the Guthi Kancahari) for the upkeeping of endowments (The chapter on Poor, section 6). Such offices were not known to have been established in the ancient or medieval period.

SOME OTHER CONSIDERATIONS TO INTERPRET
THE MULUKI AIN OF 1910 BS

The following two considerations are important to get a balanced picture of the reality before and during the time of Ain.

THE CONTRIBUTION OF SHAH KINGS TO THE
PRESERVATION OF BUDDHIST PRACTICES

There can be no doubt that the Shah Kings were Hindus. However, the words of Prithvi Narayan Shah, who is the founder of modern Nepal, did not have a policy of suppressing the customs and practices that were in operation from the time of their fathers and forefathers. It is said that he ordered the continuation of the Samyak Mahadan, which was discontinued after his victory over Kathmandu Valley and imposed a fine on those who refused to participate. He also did not stop any Buddhist religious festivals in Kathmandu Valley. Rana Bahadur Shah was known to have performed the puja of Mahamaya Bhairavi in accordance with a Boudha tantra. He also donated land to a Tibbetan Lama to establish a Guthi in 1786 AD. He also permitted the Tibetan Lama to establish the Dev Dharma Monastery and a temple of Mother Harati (Ghimire 144).

Surendra Bikram Shah is also known to have constructed a chaitya and put idols of Vajrapani and Dipankar in the Chaitya in Yatkha Bahal, Kathmandu. After the establishment of democracy in 2007 BS, King Tribhuvan visited Anandakuti Vihar on Buddha Jayanti. He was the kind who declared a national holiday on the day of Buddha Purnima.

Swaym bhunath Mahachaitya went under several renovations over a couple of centuries. For sure, a couple went under the Rana rule. Ranas did not oppose a major renovation which took place in the time of Rajendra Bir Bikram Shah (Shakya 2004).

RANA REGIME’S PROSECUTION OF THERAVADA MONKS

Xuanzang or Hiuen Tsang, in his travel account of the second half of the seventh century, also mentions his visit to two places in the present Nepal the country of Kapilvastu and the country of Nepal.14 (Li Rongxi 145, 171). Visiting Kapilvastu he mentions the condition of two palace cities which were completely deserted. In relation to the Buddhist practice, he writes ‘(t)here are more than one thousand ruined foundations of old monasteries and beside the palace city is a monastery inhabited by over three thousand monks, who study the Hinayana teachings of the Sammitiya school. There are two deva temples, with heretics (Hindu) living together’ (Li Rongxi 152). Regarding his impression of Kathmandu valley, he mentions ‘(t)he monasteries and deva temples are so close together that they touch each other and there are more than two thousand monks, who study both Mahayana and Hinayana teachings’ (Li Rongxi 192). It is also believed that out of nine missionary groups commissioned by King Asoka to propagate Buddhism in different places, one group of four monks led by Ven. Majjima came to Nepal and succeeded in propagating Buddhism in Nepal. It is popularly believed that Theravada Buddhism in practiced during Asoka’s time, which was also spread to Nepal. Unfortunately, for some unknown reason, the popularity of Buddhism declined and Vajrayana became the main Buddhist practice in Kathmandu Valley.15

An attempt at its resurgence took place in the early twentieth century but without success. In 1924 CE (1990 BS), Nanikaji Shrestha (known as Mahapragya), with other four monks—Mahachandra, Mahagyan, Mahavriya and Mahaksanti, wearing Buddhist robes, went on to the street of Kathmandu for alm collection. It was an unusual scene in the Kathmandu Valley. Prime Minister Chandrashamser exiled these five Buddhists for the crime of converting themselves to Buddhism (Ghimire 139). Mahapragya Nanikaji Shrestha together with another Bhikshu Amritananda (Lalkaji Shakya) came to Bhojpur from the Indian side to preach Buddhism in 1927 CE. The chief administrator (Badahakim) of the region arrested Mahapragya after learning that he was formally exiled by the government. They were exiled again to India upon receiving the order of Prime Minister Juddha Shamser (Ghimire 140).

However, these exiled Bhikshus did not give up. They organized themselves under the leadership of Bhikshu Amritananda. They mobilized international Buddhist communities to persuade the Nepalese government for allowing Buddhist practices. Bhikshu Amritananda was finally successful in establishing the first Theravada Bouddha Vihar in Swyambhunath in 1948 CE with the permission of the time of Prime Minister Mohan Shamser. Prime Minister Mohan Shamsher also permitted to give an official holiday to Buddhist followers on the day of Buddha Purnima (Ghimire 142).

CONCLUSION

The Ain uses the term ‘Buddhist’ only in one place. The use of the term also does not favour the promotion of Buddhist faith. However, it would be unsuitable to conclude that Buddhist practices were forbidden by the State. The account of Hiuen Tsang records the decline of Buddhism in the Kapilvastu area. Balaram Kayastha has also noted that Jayasthiti Malla cannot be entirely accused of destroying Buddhism in the valley.

It is well-established that the Buddhist religious practices started from the Lichhavi period. Hence, this was already established as the customary practice before the promulgation of the Ain. Hence, most (if not all) of the Buddhist practices should be well-protected under the recognition of customary religious practices under the Ain. Buddhist scriptures do not support killing in general and therefore the prohibition of cows cannot be interpreted as an interference with their religious practice. In terms of the public preaching of Buddhism, which has been an established practice since the time of Gautam Buddha seems not to be favoured by the Ain. The exile of Theravadin monks by the Rana regime in 1924 and 1927 AD, indicating the ban on Buddhist propagation can be interpreted as the suppression of Buddhist practices. The permission for the establishment of Anandakuti Vihar in 1948 can be taken as the beginning of the Theravadin practice in Nepal. Regarding, Himalayan or Mahayana Buddhism, their practice was not known to be interrupted by the Rana regime. They had legal protection under the rule of customary practice and their activities were also mostly unnoticed by the central administration.

Adopting the policy of promotion of the Guthi system by the Ain can be interpreted as non-interference to the established religious practice of Buddhism. This benefitted in continuing the practices of Buddhism. The donation or offering, though limited, made by the Kings and Ranas directly for the pujas, maintenance and renovation of Buddhist Chaityas and approval of offerings by other donors to maintain and renovate the Chaityas during the time of the Ain, does underline that the public policy was not against the Buddhist practice.

Though the Ain failed to include Nepal’s Buddhist glory in its text, neither Ranas nor Shahs were known to have suppressed any customary Buddhist practices. They might have made very little financial contribution but they were known to discourage the contributors as well. Nonetheless, we could sense from the reading of Muluki Ain that Buddhist communities must have had a tremendous struggle to avoid the cloud of fear hanging above them.
