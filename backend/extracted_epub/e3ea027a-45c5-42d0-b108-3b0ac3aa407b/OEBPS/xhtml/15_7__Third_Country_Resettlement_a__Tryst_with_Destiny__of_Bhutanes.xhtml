INTRODUCTION

With a population of 8,21,329 (Bhutan Population, 2018), Bhutan has predominantly been a multi-ethnic society. Rich in natural resources including water, Bhutan is one of the countries where the parameter to index the country’s growth stands as Gross National Happiness (GNH) unlike Gross Domestic Product (GDP) followed around the world. Considered as one of the happiest and most peaceful places in the world, its past has a different narration of history. Major being the expulsion of the Lohtshampas in the 1990s. While the royal state machinery worked round the clock to ensure their expulsion, it became one of the biggest humanitarian tragedies of the time. Further, it took the shape of a serious ethnic conflict which was rooted in the majority that the Drukpa community’s fear had about the domination by the migrant Nepalese Lohtshampas whose population increased phenomenally over the years. To counter this demographic challenge, Drukpa restricted them to the disease-prone southern Bhutan, their movement inside Bhutan and they were not allowed to purchase land and property in other parts of Bhutan (Rose, 1977). As a result, Lohtshampas were isolated socially, culturally and economically isolated.

Bhutan’s Lohtshampas are the descendants of peasant farmers from Nepal who began to migrate to southern Bhutan after the Anglo-Bhutanese war of 1865. Successive generations cleared the forests and formed agrarian communities that quickly became Bhutan’s main producers of food. Their numbers were augmented by later migrants, who continued to be brought in by licensed contractors until about 1930. The Nepali settlers became the kingdom’s main source of cash income: unlike the Drukpas of the north, who paid their taxes in kind right up until the late 1950s, British colonial records show that Nepali settlers in southwest Bhutan were paying taxes in cash even before the Bhutanese monarchy was established in 1907. Until the 1950s, Bhutan was not a unified polity; different systems of administration were maintained in different parts of the country. In the south, the local contractors and their descendants remained powerful. The revenue raised in certain southern districts was submitted not to the central government but to a local governor based across the Indian border in Kalimpong (Hutt M., 2003, 2005).

In the 1950s, major administrative shuffling took place under the leadership of King Jigme Dorji Wangchuk and Prime Minister Jigme Palden Dorji. Several programmes concerning the development of the country and after due process, these programmes were institutionalized. As a result, a National Assembly was elected under the royal rule and the enforcement of Bhutan’s Nationality Law. While the state machinery was institutionalized and their functionaries were clearly defined to protect the ethnic diversity in the country, protecting the rights of minorities was emphasized upon. Also, it was under the 1958 Nationality Law that granted Lohtshampas full citizenship. Meanwhile, to understand the issue of the Lohtshampas, it is relevant to mention the Nationality Law.

The Nationality Law of Bhutan, published in 1958, was reiterated by the Bhutanese government after the 1990s (Hutt M., 2003). In 1958, during the 11th session, the National Assembly of Bhutan enacted the Nationality Act of Bhutan, 1958. It granted the Bhutanese nationality for the first time to all Nepalese who had settled in the Bhutanese Kingdom before 31 December 1958 and applied under naturalization (Hutt M., 2003). Under the following provisions:

1. A person would be considered a Bhutanese national by birth if his/her father was a Bhutanese national, regardless of the person’s birth.

2. A foreigner could become a Bhutanese citizen by presenting a petition to an official appointed by His Majesty the Druk Gyalpo and takes an oath of loyalty according to the rules laid down by the official. It again required:

(a) Resided in Bhutan for more than 10 years, i.e. age of majority.

(b) Owns agricultural land within the kingdom.

(c) Has served satisfactorily in government service for at least five years.

3. Foreign wife of a Bhutanese national also required a petition to the king and taking an oath of loyalty.

4. A person could also be deprived of citizenship by becoming citizenship of another country, leaving the country permanently and abandoning the agricultural land (MHA, GoB, 1958).

The government decided to bring the Bhutanese of Nepali origin into the mainstream Drupka culture. The king of Bhutan introduced an ideology of ‘One Nation, One People’ which led to the imposition of a code of conduct, including Drukpa language, religion and dress for all Bhutanese citizens through a policy of Driglam Namzha (a revival of traditional Bhutanese culture). The primary objective of this policy has been to establish national homogeneity and cohesion among the various linguistic and ethnic groups in the country. In addition to the Driglam Namzha Policy, the government adopted various policies and passed several resolutions in its National Assembly that went against the interest of the Bhutanese of Nepali origin (MHA, GoB, 1958).

In general, the nationality law of 1958 of Bhutan provided three ways to attain citizenship. Firstly, by birth to a father who is a Bhutanese national, secondly, a petition to an official to provide certain conditions of residences and government employment were met and a petition to an official upon marriage to a Bhutanese national. Also, a petition was required on the oath of the loyalty (Hutt M., 2003).

To restrict fresh entries of migrants to Bhutan, the King introduced a new Law in the year 1977. The Citizenship Act of 1985 created more hurdles regarding both the acquisition and termination of citizenship.

THE CENSUS, 1988

The census of 1988 was implemented on the basis of the 1985 citizenship Act. Firstly, the census was conducted by the Bhutanese government only in the five southern districts of Bhutan. Secondly, the authorities excluded ethnic Nepalese from becoming naturalized citizens, as approved for under the 1985 Act.

Instead, the authorities restricted Bhutanese citizenship of ethnic Nepalese who had records, such as tax receipts, to prove residence in Bhutan in 1958, i.e., 30 years before the census. Bhutanese officials refused to accept residency records from the years 1957 and 1959 indicating residency in 1958 to establish citizenship (Human Rights Watch, 2007). They disregarded the citizenship identity cards issued after the previous census: the authorities classified people who could not prove residence in 1958 as non-nationals, ‘returned migrants’, or other illegal immigrant categories, even if they possessed a citizenship card (Amnesty International, 1994). In 1989, the government undertook a survey and realized that the number of Nepali-speaking people was rapidly growing. It led them to adopt a policy called ‘Driglam Namzha’ or ‘One Nation and One People’. Generally, it seems reasonable for a country to adopt such kind of policy but it was not suitable to Bhutan due to the existence of different groups with different cultural practices, religions and languages the government wanted to impose the Nagalong culture, on every citizen.

In 1991, there was a protest against the policy and peaceful demonstration for supporting human rights. But the Voluntary Migration Form were printed in Dzongkha language, which most of the Nepali-speaking people were unable to read. As a result, many who filled it were indirectly giving up their citizenship of Bhutan. This led to the creation of many refugees in Bhutan. Apart from that, the Indian government denied asylum for the Bhutanese refugees and consequently, they had to leave for Nepal and are still remaining in camps since the 1990s.

Though the Royal Government of Bhutan has its own version of the exodus, accounts given by refugees, interviews with refugees’ leaders, reports by various human rights organizations and media reports lead us to the conclusion that the exodus of refugees from Bhutan has been broadly in four categories (Kharat, 2007):

• Bhutanese who had been forcefully evicted.

• Bhutanese who had voluntarily emigrated.

• Non-Bhutanese.

• Bhutanese who had committed criminal acts.

Out of these four categories, Thimphu agreed to the return of the people who belonged to the first category, although it felt that most of the refugees in United Nation High Commission for Refugees (UNHCR) camps in eastern Nepal were originally Nepalese and Indian citizens looking for free food and shelter and health care. However, in subsequent bilateral talks, particularly during 1993-6, both Bhutan and Nepal took a more rigid stand regarding the repatriation of Bhutanese refugees (Kharat, 2007).

Till date, in the last 20 years, 15 bilateral talks and many other informal dialogues have taken place. But the government of Bhutan and Nepal could not reach a consensus and conclusion to find a permanent solution. The situation remains unchanged.

The UN convention on Refugees after the cold war in the 1960s and 1980s came into for that reason. However, the repatriation process was adopted in Nepal in 2007. In Article 1 of the statute of UNHCR there are three options for a durable solution:

• Repatriation to the country of origin.

• Rehabilitation in the host country.

• Resettlement in a third country (Refugee Watch Online, 2007).

ROLE OF UNITED NATIONS HIGH
COMMISSION FOR REFUGEES

More than 1,30,000 Bhutanese people of Nepali ethnicity have been compelled to live in exile for more than ten years. About 1,00,000 of them live at present in the UNHCR organized refugee camps in Morang and Jhapa districts of eastern Nepal. With the refugee crisis becoming more serious, some of the countries like United States, Canada, Australia, Denmark, Norway, New Zealand and The Netherlands have shown their sympathy and interest in resettling these refugees in their country. In 2006, US Secretary of State Population announced to resettle 60,000 Bhutanese refugees (Human Rights Watch, 2007).

After exhaustive efforts towards repatriation failed, several countries came together, with UNHCR and took initiatives for resettlement of refugees. In November 2007, the government of Nepal publicly accepted and supported for third country resettlement. Thereafter, the Nepal government, UNHCR and the International Organization for Migration and the resettlement countries began resettlement activities. To enjoy the activity and to enjoy the safety of the applicant, prevent threat and violence and anti-resettlement goods, the government established the armed police in the camps by the third country resettlement. UNHCR remains the first way to contact the refugees in order to be eligible for third country resettlement (UNHCR, 2018).

The world food programme works for food, ration and education for the refugees. They undertake the transportation of food in the camps. The Lutheran World Federation distributes the meals to the refugees, every two weeks and the association of medical doctors is responsible for medical services in the camps, CARITAS Australia is responsible for the education of refugee children, Bhutanese refugees serve as team teachers and the curriculum in accordance with the Nepalese public school system and request the livelihood of the repatriation the Bhutanese refugees remaining in Nepal. Although Bhutanese refugees considerably enjoy a bold attitude by the policy of opening camp, technically Bhutanese refugees are not eligible to work, earn property and run a business the situation has remained like this for the past two decades.

At present, many uplifts are found in the refugee camps. The elders and youths wants to return Bhutan to as grounds for hope. They want a quick solution. The elderly have begun to say, ‘If our bodies cannot be buried in our motherland, then everything is meaningless.’ They are fed up with living for long in troubled camps. They are deprived of citizenship and other political and human rights in Nepal. Moreover, they have bitter experiences and memories of displacement imposed upon them by the autocratic government of Bhutan. Therefore, refugees have a miserable life, are also becoming a real problem for Nepal as their presence in Nepal as a receiving country has led to economic, social, cultural and environmental crisis. The UNHCR and Nepal provided asylum to these refugees on a humanitarian ground.

Resettlement of the Bhutanese refugees in the third country is seen as the only solution, or an option for them as they are fed up with the long run camp lives with deplorable condition. For example, UNHCR staff member Tulsi Limbu was just a child when he saw the refugee arriving in Nepal. He adds:

I was astonished to see their challenging situation in terms of food and shelter. After spending 20 years in the refugee camps, resettlement is a great option in helping them to start their lives a fresh. I feel very proud that, somewhere, my small efforts were a part of this successful operation (UNHCR, 2013).

CAPACITY BUILDING

As a core activity in strengthening ‘capacity building under the international protection no. 10: claims to refugees’ status within the context of Article 1A(2) of 1951 convention and the 1967 protocol relating to the situation of refugees of 3 December 2013, seeks to help in the protection of refugees though livelihood programmes by UNHCR, develop the capacity to strengthen and to make people self-sufficient in their places.

Capacity building activities are basically ensured under the law of protection for handling refugees’ issues, care and self-reliance but it cannot be generated without donor agencies, humanitarian assistance, civil society, NGOs and with the refugees itself.

Due to lack of income and means to earn a living, Bhutanese refugees are entirely dependent on relief agencies as they are not permitted by the host government to engage in any paid work or to produce goods for sale and work outside the camps. So, they work within confines of fields (Women’s Refugee Commission, 2006). Over time, they have developed alternative livelihood strategies, such as:

• They produce crops in the camps area and livestock activities.

• Receive incentives from aid agencies and remittance from family members abroad.

• Run a small-scale business in various sectors including small shops, drug stores, the sale of telephone cards, small restaurants and bars, transport of goods and persons, repair services (bicycles, cars and radios), hairdressing, money exchange, phone services, security and language tutoring or interpreting.

• Their production activities include carpentry and furniture making blacksmithing and welding, production of bricks and soap, baking and food processing and production of baskets, leatherwear, textiles and clothes.

• They work with humanitarian agencies or incentive work where formal employment is not allowed which provides a share of refugees with a regular income.

• They work in the position as teachers, nurses, community workers, office staff, cleaners, cooks and gardeners (Bruijn, 2009).

Sometimes refugees do adopt negative coping strategies when none of the alternatives provides structural relief or when households or individuals are faced with immediate crises. These strategies include selling off vital assets such as domestic items, clothes and blankets or part of the food rations, or reducing food intake and negotiating loans that cannot be repaid. Hence, they are forced to indulge in crime and illegal activities, such as fraud (e.g., receiving cash and material benefits for repatriation and then returning for re-registration) and theft (crops, cattle and other assets), or to selling sexual services and trafficking (Refugee Watch Online, 2007). Similarly, camps situated in areas are prone to violence and instability which are not favourable to establish productive and structural ways to earn a living. With the passage of time, the support system in the camps has come under increasing strains because of ‘donor fatigue’ which cuts in the provision of essential services, including food, fuel, medical care and shelter materials (Lænkholm, 2007). Again, the standard services extended to every refugee have now been limited to only most vulnerable ones.

Finally, it is evident that the level of development of the country of asylum is an essential factor in determining the well being and status of the refugees as the country’s capacity to assist refugees sets a standard for the living conditions.

Self-management skills are provided to vulnerable refugee women under the Bhutanese Refugee Women’s Forum operating in both the camps (UNHCR, 2000). It gives training for various sectors in business management; awareness-raising programmes on gender and domestic violence and leadership training, childcare centre, income generation for women and fire prevention are run for Refugees (UNHCR, 2000). Women’s groups are established in each of the camps and informal skills training, social awareness and functional literacy classes are provided to women with specific needs. Foundation of Bhutanese Refugee Aiding the Victims of Violence (BRAVVE) was founded to provide training to mentally challenged people, many of whom were tortured in jail, traumatized and disabled. People are provided training in tailoring, weaving, embroidery, shoemaking, typing and computer literacy (WHO, 1999). Practising these skills enabled the mentally challenged individuals to be productive. Establishment of a credit scheme helped them purchase sewing machines, equipment and set up small businesses. Both UNHCR and WFP provide funding to Caritas for Vocational Training (VT) to youth from refugee communities who have dropped out of high school. WFP supports for the training of medical staff, implemented by the AMDA. Some 40 women of an equal number of women from the refugee population and adjacent host communities participated in an eighteen-month course in midwifery and nursing (UNHCR/WFP, 2012). WFP with LWF and UNHCR as implementing partners supports a home gardening project to supplement the basic needs.

SOLUTIONS

According to the refugee-budget of UNHCR for Bhutanese Refugees has been increasing almost annually since the year 2000 such as 2000-USD 5.1 million; 2001-5.3 million; 2002-5.6 million; 2003-5.5 million; 2004-6.9 million; 2005-6.8 million. 2007, 2008 (UNHCR, 2000), instead, believing that return provided the only humane solution to refugee problems, the UNHCR essentially ran long-term programmes in an emergency mode which were damaging to the long-term welfare of refugees stuck in protracted camp situations. They have already spent years in remote and forgotten camps and lack in wage-earning opportunities, self-sufficiency. So, it needs a solution where they can become productive and contribute to their host societies until the refugees are willing or can return home.

Refugees usually return when they consider it is safe to do so when other interests are met. Moreover, the UNHCR is not in the best position to determine when it is best or most appropriate for repatriation. It has remained only as an organization in a more general humanitarian emergency. So, it has compromised its capacity and willingness to provide protection and has put the agency at the mercy of a much broader set of political and strategic calculations. Because UNHCR needs to raise more and more voluntary funds for its operations, obtain access to increasingly volatile internal situations, win the confidence of competing actors and promote compromise solutions, the agency is not well placed to stand up for protection principles regarding refugees (Loescher, 2001). The office also lacks a strong self-identity and remains uncertain about the precise role it should fulfill with the broader framework of international relations.

DURABLE SOLUTIONS PROMOTED
BY UNHCR

The UNHCR has outlined three options to residents of a refugee camp of protracted refugee situation (PRS). These are:

• Voluntary return (repatriation) to their home country.

• Local integration in the country of first asylum.

• Resettlement in a third country.

REPATRIATION

Voluntary return or repatriation to their home country in conditions of safety and dignity is the most desirable outcome for the individual, their society and the international community. Most refugees want to return to their homes when it is safe to do so. Support for reconstruction, re-integrating and reconciliation helps ensure the success of repatriation as a durable solution. Regarding the local integration of Bhutanese refugees, though it may appear as a solution to the problem, it will legitimize the state of Bhutan to denationalise its population, in the long run ‘it could encourage more displacements’ (Chetri, 1997). Refugees insist they won’t go back to Bhutan. But they want to back to their land and houses and with some assurance of safety. They would like the office of the UNHCR together with that of the UNHCR to be involved. ‘We’re not asking for the sky,’ says Ratan Gazmere of the Association of Human Rights Activists, based in Nepal. These conditions are unlikely to be met, however. As Bhutanese refugees were forced to leave their homes, land and properties in Bhutan large numbers of houses and land left by the refugees in southern Bhutan have been confiscated by the government and have been used to relocate people from other parts of Bhutan or for government purposes (COHRE, 2008). So, it would be difficult to relocate them in the kingdom of Bhutan.

However, in August 2005 the United Nations Principles on Housing and property restitution for refugees and displaced persons were adopted, ‘Pinheiro principles’ (COHRE, 2008). These principals provide clear guidelines to make it possible for the Bhutanese refugees to return home. Such possibility applies to all refugees irrespective of which option repatriation, local integrating or third country resettlement, the Bhutanese refugees choose. Most importantly, making repatriation possible, the Bhutanese governments should recognize that the Bhutanese refugees have a right to return. All refugees have the right to go back voluntarily to their former homes, lands or places where they used to live, in safety and dignity. That mean that persons displaced against their will must be allowed to return to their former homes and lands, if and whenever they wish to do so.

LOCAL INTEGRATION

Local integration in the country of first asylum is the second option suggested by UNHCR when voluntary repatriation is not possible. It is so because the country of first asylum is usually in the same region to refugee’s home country and cultural ties can make the integration process more accessible. In the case of Bhutanese refugees, their first logically, the country for asylum is India, but they have their camps and livelihood in Nepal. Local integration is the process in which refuges settle permanently in the host country (Jacobsen, 2001). However, due to political reasons, regional integration in Nepal has not the preferred solution.

The first obstacle is that Bhutanese refugees are perceived as a temporary inconvenience to the host country because they rely heavily on government and international assistance. It creates the sentiment that if locally integrated refugees are not able to sustain themselves without outside assistance that was available in the camps, they will consequently become a permanent burden to the host country.

Second, refugees lack of belonging to the host country due to discrimination. These are great obstacles in the way of local integrations, who often prefer to return to their home country as soon as they deem it safe or live in the hope of being resettled in a third country.

Third, for local integration to take place, it is fundamental to have the participation and willingness of the host government. Preliminary conditions that need to exist before local integration can take place include the involvement and cooperation of the host government.

RESETTLEMENT IN A ‘THIRD COUNTRY’

Resettlement in a third country is used to protect refugees whose life, liberty, safety, health or fundamental human rights are at risk in their country of asylum. It is promoted by UNHCR where returning to their home country, or local integration may not be suitable or as part of a responsibility sharing arrangement. Due to protracted situations resettlement has become a key element in the system of refugee protection. In TCR, refugees are transferred from the country in which they found refuge to another country which agrees to welcome them. The UNHCR generally gives priority to individuals with a high protection need, as well as women and highly vulnerable families. This kind of resettlement is usually recommended for populations that are victims of protracted situations and when neither repatriation nor local integration seems possible.

The effective implantation to a third country presents numerous challenges. First, it depends on refugees to integrate into the new country. They must be able to adapt themselves to the life, culture and socio-economic structures in the host country. It implies that refugees need to take on a wage-earning activity having a minimum level of education that is necessary. But in Bhutanese refugees’ situation, they have a language barrier to integrate.

PROTECTION

These are violence that are directed against a person on the basis of their gender or sex. It includes acts that inflict physical, mental, or sexual harm or suffering, threats of such acts, coercion and other deprivations of liberty. Examples of gender-based violence are sexual violence, domestic violence, emotional and psychological abuse, trafficking, forced prostitution, sexual exploitation, sexual harassment and harmful traditional practices, e.g. Female genital mutilation, or widow cleansing. The women’s commission for women and children defines the term ‘gender-based violence’ as, physical, sexual, psychological and economic or socio-cultural attacks by strangers, by family members, by armies and police forces, even by peacekeepers. It includes harassments, sexual exploitation and trafficking (UNHCR, 2001). Though GBV can occur to anyone, i.e. male or female, young or old it disproportionately affects women and girls.

Similarly GBV is unique to refugee situations. Human rights watch examines the uneven response of UNHCR and the government of Nepal to GBV in refugee camps in Nepal despite reforms by UNHCR though one can experience some positive aspects in the camps but cannot be confirmed. Due to unemployment people are not able to carry their socio-economic roles and increase of family tensions. This results in the subsequent rise in the use of alcohol by men within the refugees’ community and leads to aggressive behaviour towards women and results in SGBV.

It has been argued that gender relations in refugees camp are influenced by traditional ideologies and are shaped by memories of home as a domestic sphere of the patriarchal family. And it is well known that the Bhutanese refugees’ culture is a patriarchal dominant. So, within the camps, there is a tradition where women bound to remain within the periphery of household activities and dependent on men in the process of getting aid. They have a lower and subordinate position and are subjected to various forms of violence and discrimination. In the case of women the main causes of being the victim are the economic hardship and lack of education and skills. Many women are forced to cross the border to seek better and secure lives are involved in sex businesses workplace harassment and abuse, vulnerability, personal safety and security question of sustainability of income and assistance have become a daily challenge to human right watch reports, SGBV is widespread and have been occurring among Bhutanese refugees in many forms including domestic violence, e.g. fighting and sexual assault, harassment, trafficking, enforced prostitution and sexual violence. Also in the camps, cases of polygamy and child marriage against refugee women, including refugee girls, were reported. Perpetrators of this violence against women include not only partners but also humanitarian aid workers, sexual harassment com-mitted by Nepalese government officers, most of the teachers, working for NGOs implementing partners. In 2001, an investigation team from the inspector general’s office investigated all camps with a particular focus on the incidents of SGBV against female refugees, including children. The team reported a total of 47 cases in which ten refugee teachers; one refugee health worker, one sanitation volunteer and two Nepali government officials were said to have been addressed (UNHCR, 2001).

According to WCRWC, incidents of gender-based violence in camps include an attack on women and girls collecting firewood located near to forests (UNHCR, 2006). These are again the results due to a shortage in kerosene supply and which have necessitated firewood collection mainly by those refugees without having sufficient means (UNHCR, 2006). Despite all limitations and discrimination, some definite improvement and steps towards empowerment are seen. Women hence are managing to have a good education and raising their awareness. In recent years, they have found along with colleague ‘Voice for change’, only independent women’s organization in the camps which advocates for women’s rights. It has given women the opportunity to express themselves and defend themselves against violence. It has also helped to raise women’s voices and has created a platform for discussion, for sharing experiences and finding solutions. Its primary objective is to highlight the issues, inform women and help them understand their problems. Their advocacy activities have been sustained by local NGOs such as TEW Nepal, by the Global Fund for Women and INGO. Hence it can be said that the external influences of international aid workers have made changes through programmes which include training, information support services and for local women and public education events. Gender-based violence cases have been given the priority in the third country resettlement.

The OHCHR plays a role in monitoring and investigating human rights abuses, including those directed against women and girls. UNICEF, for its part, responds to the specific needs of refugee. NGOs are also crucial protection sectors. They partner with UN agencies and work on their own to implement programmes to prevent and respond to SGBV in refugee settings.

HEALTH AND SANITATION

The refugee experiences have significant impact on health due to several adverse situations in their country of origin and during their flight and still in the life in exile. The following are some of the deprivation, civil unrest separation and loss of family and friends, imprisonment, threat, beatings, torture and rape, overcrowded, poor hygiene and undernutrition, inadequate health care due to the distraction of infrastructure, disruption in preventive services and limited health services while fleeing or in exile (Mitchell, 2003). Some of the common health problems seen among Bhutanese refugees are: Psychological; issues including PTSD, anxiety or depression; due to torture, poor health as a result of poor nutrition, diet, lack of fluoridated water, infectious diseases including ongoing risks from tuberculosis and chronic intestinal parasites, underimmunization, under-managed chronic condition such as hypertension, diabetes and chronic pain and lastly, delayed growth and development in children including deficits in hearing and speech (Mitchell, 2003).

In 2010, due to all allegation of the disproportionately high number of suicides among refugees resettled in the US, in January 2011, the IOM in coordination with UNHCR conducted a three-week assessment on psychosocial well-being and suicide risk factors among refugees in Nepal and the ones resettled in the US. Results for this confirm that the number of suicides among refugees in Nepal camp is disproportionately high and the rate among refugees resettled in the US is even higher (Schininà, Sharma, Gorbacheva & Mishra, 2011). Besides, health programmes and awareness are provided under UNHCR among refugee camps mainly on subjects related to health education, including HIV/AIDS prevention, family planning, inoculations, mother and child health projects, ambulance services and maintenance of health facilities (UNHCR, 2008) although, different health and nutrition programme are implemented in the camps. In 2007, the Women’s Refugee Commission launched a major research project to assess the situation for those living with disabilities among displaced and conflict-affected populations. Because people living with disabilities may be left behind during flight, or may not survive the journey, they are often not identified or counted in registration or data collection exercises; they are excluded from or unable to access mainstream assistance programmes and forgotten when specialized services are set up.

The WRC was particularly concerned about discrimination by their gender, age and social status, as well as their disability. Some positive examples of the refugee community outreach health programmes for persons with a disability were found in the refugee camps (Reilly, 2007). Regarding the availability of inclusive education for children with disability in refugee camps in Nepal, there was successful early childhood intervention programmes to identify children with disability and help them integrate into mainstream schools (Reilly, 2007).

Health status of refugee is a complex issue as it is related to numerous factors, such as food supply, water and sanitation, shelter, education, environmental factors, health services, etc. So to understand the issue, one needs to have a comprehensive approach. Primarily, refugee camps are too compact and overcrowded, which may result in easy spread or transmission of infectious disease. The provision of adequate sanitation services is crucial to prevent communicable diseases and epidemics while ensuring good health and dignity. This part of the study includes human excreta disposal, waste water disposal, solid wasted and clinical waste management and hygiene promotion as a part of sanitation. The collection and disposal of waste should not endanger the individuals and the community. In Nepal, sanitation facilities are designed with women’s needs in mind and to avoid vulnerability to SGBV. All families have designated restroom that is shared with one other family and this has provided adequate sanitation coverage. On critically reviewing the sanitation situation in the refugee camps, camps are adequately planned with adequate infrastructure with the refugee managing most of the relief activities with agency coordination. Solid waste management and vector control were carried out through the construction and repair of latrines spraying in the camps. Camps sanitary conditions remained good; vector-borne diseases were kept at negligible levels (UNHCR, 2004). Restrooms were maintained, garbage was collected and clean-up and campaigns conducted regularly within the camps. Construction materials were provided to refugee families to replace over 2,400 latrine roofs and sanitation volunteers helped refugee families to shift close to 3,400 latrine pits. In 2004, a joint solid waste collection, transportation and disposal project was implemented for refugees and host community residents in Beldangi and Sanischari camps (UNHCR, 2004). Shapkota Prajapati describing the Water Supply and Sanitation of Refugee Camp in Nepal writes, ‘Excreta disposal, solid waste management and wastewater disposal have been found satisfactory and people’s participation is an example to be followed elsewhere’ (Kota & Lee, 2006). UNHCR/ WFP Joint Assessment Mission Report founded that in general the activities related to water supply and sanitation were functioning well. Periodic clean-up campaigns are organized and garbage is disposed at proper site safe for both the refugee and local population (UNHCR, 2008).

FOOD AND NUTRITION STATUS AMONG REFUGEES

In the refugee camp situation like in Nepal, people are entirely dependent on humanitarian assistance and food aid. This is primarily because of the host country’s policies which restrict income-generation and agricultural activities of refugees. Hence, they entirely depend in the WFP assistance for food which ensures access to adequate quality of food to support life and preserve dignity of refugees. It has the responsibility for the management of the entire food delivery and distribution whereas UNHCR supplements the rations of people with specific needs such as dried skimmed milk was provided to malnourished refugee children. Safe drinking and domestic water supply are provided in all camps.

UNHCR recommended that each refugee receive more than 2,100 calories per day, but often camps fall short of this standard. Even if a refugee receives the recommended number of calories per day, caloric intake is further reduced as refugees tend to sell food rations for other non-food goods. Moreover, it is not only the quality of food that is insufficient, but lack of food variety, fruits and vegetables also causes many refugees to suffer from deficiencies in essential vitamins and minerals leading to variety of diseases. However, a market in the camps and gardening provides refugee families with fresh vegetables, condiments and other items to supplement their diets. But the lack of farmland restricts families from growing more vegetables. To minimize this deficiency among refugee children, all adolescent girls received iron tablets and all children attending school received deworming tablets (UNHCR/ WFP, 2012). Hence over the year the provision of vegetable provided has been reduced, it forced people to earn income and buy food to supplement their ration and to ensure that their diet remains healthy. Similarly, a nutrition and food security strategic plan has been developed in coordination with operational partners to provide improved the nutrition situation among refugees in Nepal (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

WFP, UNHCR, the RCU and LWF participate in the committee and together agree who would be added or removed from distribution and registration lists in the camp. The committee review the list of departure to third country and adjust the food distribution lists accordingly whereas Asian Medical Doctor’s Association (AMDA) is responsible for post distribution food basket monitoring (UNHCR/WFP, 2012). Lastly, as noted above, all the overall food and nutritional status of the refugees is satisfactory.

UNHCR is responsible through its partners for the distribution of non-food items. To prevent environmental degradation particularly from the forest and mainly the increasing fuel prices the regular supply of kerosene to the camps ceased in December 2005. It was used as the most viable source of fuel for cooking in the camps since 1992. Kerosene, replaced by briquettes has hugely impacted on the refugees as briquettes burn slowly, produces a foul smoke and cannot be used as lighting fuel. It prevents children from studying after dark.

REFUGEE WELFARE

Nepal is not signatory in the 1951 refugee convention or the 1967 protocol. Refugees are considered foreigners and are not allowed to engage in economic activities or own any kind of property (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

Since 2008, the most significant change in refugee welfare has been seen in remittance from abroad. Many shops advertising money transfers are located outside of the camp. Although, these services are utilized by beneficiaries of both the communities. For increment of their diet they use to purchase food and non-food items. They are also used to pay off debts, to provide loans to others, or set aside for contingencies such as fire (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

Some refugees have started business or open shop in the camp. Other have used the fund to pursue their skill training or advance education. The standard of living has increased in the camp population; it is clearly visible to mission members in the electricity supply, TV sets and satellite dish antenna (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

SKILL AND VOCATIONAL TRAINING

Skill trainings are very useful for the refugees so that they can be considered for the resettlement. Here the list of skills includes computer software and hardware, finance, driving, tailoring, housekeeping, hair dressing, beautification, masonry, carpentry, plumbing, electric wiring and repair of radios/electric equipment (UNHCR/WFP, 2012). Although, some refugees have complained that in many courses they were not eligible for selected courses as they did not qualify the eligibility criteria (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

With the support of WFP, the Bhutanese Refugee Women’s Forum (BRWF) implements a Loan Scheme. Single women, large families, families without extra support are the beneficiaries. Apart from that at the large scale most of the refugees hesitate to take advantage of the scheme (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

UNHCR has invested in extension in capacity building, Health workers by means of training, orientation and regular monitoring and supervision by the staff, but as per resettlement health workers poses a serious challenge to the goal of maintaining the quality of health services to the camp population (UNHCR/WFP, 2012).

CONCLUSION

The efforts of the UNHCR for well being of the Bhutanese refugees in Nepal, who moved from Bhutan in 1990, have been tremendous, meanwhile they have been constrained by the situation rising on ground. Having been thrown away from their homes and motherland, livelihood and for individual development of the refugees, are critically disrupted and they have been largely dependent on humanitarians’ assistance and policies and local conditions in the host country. It is commendable that the UNHCR through its programmes namely basic needs and assistance, capacity building, has attempted to provide durable solutions like repatriation and their local integration. These solutions have eased the settlement, but refugees still face the tough times in fulfilling their wish to go back to Bhutan and settle on their erstwhile homeland, especially the land where their ancestors had settled centuries ago which they are denied now. It is notable that a third country resettlement of the refugees around the globe requires protection, health and sanitation, food and nutrition and other welfare programmes and in the case of the Bhutanese refuges, efforts of the UNHCR and other NGOs and International NGOs can neither be denied nor be ignored. Among the many of the UNHCR led programmes, the most effective contribution has been the financial assistance provided by the UNHCR. In Nepal, to facilitate refugees’ integration into the local context and to avoid resentment among the locals surrounding refugee camps, residents for certain assistance are targeted, such as, for example in health care, education, water and food supply. Community-based projects in the camps has been often more efficient in providing services. Implementing partners are working in closer cooperation with the refugees themselves. Without adequate and timely protection mechanisms and proper community-support, the protection and assistance to these vulnerable sections would be more critical.

Also, it is not the issue of the third country settlement and improving the life quality, the other aspect of the Bhutanese refugees in past three decades has revolved around the question of the life with dignity and access to civil, political, economic and educational rights that have been guaranteed under the universal declaration of the human rights, convention on civil and political rights, followed by the convention of the economic rights. While the refugees hold no political rights, allowing them to participate in the process of the governance, they are left to rely on the goodwill and efforts of the agencies like local NGOs and the international agencies like UNHCR. Further, since the ousting of the Nepali-speaking Bhutanese refugees to a third country, their situation has remained static rather turning into complete change. Also, a third country settlement of the refugees does not guarantee the change into their lives, it only guarantees shelter and food with other limited options for education and skill development. With efforts made at the non-governmental level, Nepal has hosted them for three decades now, but the local resistance in the host country is wide open. The Maoist insurgency in the past decade and unstable political turf in Nepal, options for the refugees in terms of political will to pressurize the government of Bhutan to accept them and even improve their life quality in Nepal remains a challenge. Hence, the political will and government response of the host country is the need of the hour for the wellness and hopes for the repatriation of the refugees in Bhutan in near future.
