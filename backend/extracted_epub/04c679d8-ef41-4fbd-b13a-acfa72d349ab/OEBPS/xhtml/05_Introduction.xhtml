Introduction

MANOJ KUMAR JENA

Media has always been one of the central questions to social structure and its processes, and is integral to the social world. It mediates the social, political and economic relationships and reshapes our imagination of them. Many of the cultural commentators have often argued that ‘we’ live in a world of situated culture, and within a ‘culture of mediation’. As one of the fundamental social units, the media plays a critical role in creating, shaping, and reflecting public opinion in a democratic society, however, public opinion is the engine that keeps the wheels of democracy turning.1 In a profound sense, media/communication is one of the unique products and producers of society’s development.2

The medium, message and meaning are essential components of communication process. Marshal McLuhan argued ‘medium is the message’,3 one can move further towards the question of meaning which is central to social relations. Messages and Meanings are vital components of the social world, as Stuart Hall argues, the meaning is a social product associated with social practices, and language and symbolizations are the certain means by which meaning is produced.4 The media, in any form, whether cinema, visual, print, or theatre, are carriers of meaning and are part of social structure and functions. The study of media and communication process is dynamic, because of changing nature of message and meaning processes. For instance, semiotics sees media as associated with social meaning and as the product of the relationships constructed between the ‘signs’. The ‘sign’ is a basic unit of communication, which could be in the form of a photograph, a traffic signal, a word, a sound, an object, or a smell, whatever the culture finds significant.5 The mediated image has multiple meanings that vary from place to place, and person to person. The content created by media and the flow of information services shaped/reshaped the social terrain; it has its own time space and ideological specificity, the routine social life revolves around the media identities, media fantasies, and media constructed realities. The fundamental question in the present context is whether the media is constructing a representational reality in the form of media reality or vice-versa. Whether media is structuring or restructuring people’s sensibilities and taken for granted realities? This is indeed an ontological question. Jim Willis argues that, ‘today, what we know comes almost entirely from the news media. However, since the media gives us only a representation of reality—a kind of shadow world with varying degrees of accuracy—our behaviour and reactions may or may not be valid or consistent with what really has transpired.’6

Willis also argues that, the media sets the public agenda, and the role of the media is to mobilize public support for special interests that dominate government and society.7 There are also questions of reproduction and legitimation of dominance, and how growing technological innovation brought about more and more control of the medium, which is ideologically specific and market-driven. Media production is intrinsically interlinked with capital, in fact, global capital with hegemonic ideological base. As has been argued, in a democratic set up, the media is the foremost tool for public discourse activities, however, the existing public sphere in a democratic social space is always contested in spite of a controlled situation where few control many. Hence, it is essential to critically analyse media and marginal question which is intrinsically associated with medium, message and meaning.

Questions related to marginals are always topical in sociology and media studies. The question about marginal and mainstream needs to be appropriately addressed. The term ‘marginal’ according to the Merriam-Webster Dictionary is ‘of relating to, or situated at a margin or border, excluded from or existing outside the mainstream of society’.8 According to Collins’ dictionary, marginal can be viewed as insignificant; they are not involved in the main events or developments in society because they are poor or have no power’.9 They are socially stigmatized and vulnerable socially, economically, and politically. The question of marginal is also embedded in sociological writings. According to sociological literature, the marginal man concept relates to Robert Park’s original interests in race and ethnic relations and migration.10 Park (1928) highlighted the question of marginal man; for him, ‘the marginal man; the man who, emerging from the ghetto in which he lived in Europe, is seeking to find a place in the freer, more complex and cosmopolitan life of an American city.’11 C.A. Goldberg argues that although Park conceived the marginal man as a general social type, he closely associated the type with the Jews of the modern era. Park was a student of and strongly influenced by Simmel, he explicitly identified the marginal man with Simmel’s own notion of the stranger, the ‘classic example’ of which for Simmel ([1908] 1971:144) was ‘the history of European Jews.’12 E.V. Stonequist’s work The Marginal Man is more on the sociological analysis, as ‘it was a kind of sociological frame of reference’, he argued ‘the marginal man is a personality type that arises at a time and a place where, out of the conflict of races and cultures, new societies, new peoples, and cultures are coming into existence. The fate which condemns him to live, at the same time, in two worlds is the same which compels him to assume, in relation to the worlds in which he lives, the role of a cosmopolitan and a stranger’.13

The marginal could also be considered as a sociological category in the Indian context on the basis of complex social differentiations, based on sexuality, gender relations, physical and mental disability, caste, class, ethnicity, religion, minority, etc. The difference and distinctiveness as a social category are intersectional and intermixed with various other categories. The marginals in India, particularly women, Dalits, disabled, and various ethnic and religious minorities, are socially, politically, and economically marginalized. The communities, groups, and individuals on the basis of caste, gender, ethnicity, religion and disabilities categories are classified as marginal on the basis of their identities and social locational specificity. They are subjected to a dominant and ossified reality constructed by the upper echelons of society. Also, they are pushed towards a culture of marginalization. These marginal groups form a major chunk of the population in Indian society, which were excluded and discriminated against on the basis of their structural position. Their voice and sufferings are never adequately represented in mainstream media. They are out of the medium, message, and meaning processes. This issue is related to historical, situational, and textual contexts. The existing dominant institutional structure, values, norms, and ideologies downgrade the marginal position.

Because of its structural locations, marginals are differently segmented, and are neglected in media production process. Here, one could see through the sociological lens how they are not only adversely affected by entrenched structural inequality but also suffer by systematic social exclusionary process. The marginal groups often confront challenges on the basis of their primordial identity; most suffered are Dalits, women, and religious minorities on the basis of their predetermined differences and negation. The dominant groups control medium message and meaning in the mainstream society. On one hand, the dominant groups are idealizing and glorifying their past and foregrounding their lineage, and on the other hand, they negate the marginal because of their lineage and social locations.

The book is an attempt to explore the relationships between marginal and different forms of communication processes. Furthermore, this book tries to unravel the linkages between the media and the caste, class, gender, minority, political and ideological structures of Indian society in general.

ABOUT THE BOOK

This book attempts to highlight the role and relationship of media and marginals in India precisely in terms of gender, Dalit, disability, labour, the Sikh minority and ethnic minorities in India. The book addresses issues about the interrelationship between the communication process, the economic, political and ideological structures of Indian society, and their relationships with marginal, particularly at structural and meaning levels. It also tries to determine how the mediated phenomena have led to a shift in the discourse on marginals. It aims to present new and emerging analyses of caste, gender, and minority questions in the Indian media pertaining to structure, process, representation, production, consumption, and meaning-making process. It also tries to figure out how the mediated phenomena have brought a shift in the discourse on the question of marginal in India.

The book is intended to understand the key concerns of marginals in India, specifically with reference to women, Dalits, minorities, marginal labourers, and people with disabilities in various mediums, including cinema and theatre. The nine chapters of the book from well-known scholars in sociology, media studies, gender studies, labour studies, and linguistic studies from India and abroad, focus on several new dimensions in the Sociology and media research with a particular thrust on areas like women, Dalits, and minorities, including Sikhs and Muslims. These chapters reflect on a range of topics affecting the life of the marginals by the existing social realities, structural imbalances, violence, reinforcement of stereotypes, negations and negotiations. All the chapters illustrate conceptual and methodological questions related to the social positioning of the marginals covering empirical and theoretical aspects. The book’s strength lies in exploring the experiential narratives and lived realities of marginals. The issues of women’s agency and empowerment through cultural production, representation of gender, caste, and violence in popular culture are integral part of the book. The book provides a newer perspective on the sociology of media.

The first chapter, Gender, Sexuality and Cinema: Reinforcing the Stereotypes deals with the issue of cinema, gender and sexuality. It focuses on how violence is inherent in the actual depiction of women and mainstream media, including defining and redefining their identity. The chapter analyses the relationship between mainstream media and women, particularly how violence occurs inherent in the very depiction of women and mainstream media, as well as in defining women’s identity.

The second chapter, Hindi Cinema and Caste Question: Ideology and Reproduction of Dominance analyses Hindi cinema from a sociological vantage point, particularly Hindi cinema as an institution and art form, as well as its social, cultural and ideological role in society. The chapter tries to understand narration, image/photography, the power of visual imagery, technology and creative processes. Furthermore, it addresses the questions of ideology and the reproduction of caste domination in Hindi cinema. It argues how the cultural domination of Hindi cinema produces a subservient and passive self, contemporary cinema producing and reproducing dominant characters, normalising violence and subjugation through various representations. It is often argued that Hindi cinematic communication produces a dominant ideological pattern that negates marginal subjects. It has been observed that Hindi cinema storylines are more or less centred around upper-class/caste narratives, and reproduce a specific class and caste ideology.

The third chapter, Realities of the Marginal Worker, as reflected in the Medium of Cinema argues that, initially, cinema was a medium of entertainment. However, gradually, it became a powerful analytical tool for examining and reflecting the conditions of the marginals of the society. The marginals are the ‘Dalits’, sex workers, disadvantaged workers such as agricultural labourers, distressed marginal farmers, slum dwellers, street vendors, small artists and many others. This chapter provides an overview of the cinema industry and examines how some films reflect and narrate the realities of the existence of marginalised groups in Indian society, through some movies produced in Hindi. The purpose is to see to what extent the films narrated the existing conditions and what message the directors and producers attempted to deliver to the audience.

Chapter four, Disability in Cinema: A Case Study of Selected Hindi Films argues that cinema is an essential manner of representational medium, and is crucial in presenting various social realities on the screen. As cinema in general, and Hindi film in particular, has a large audience and a significant global reach, it is also essential to examine the nuances of its operation and appreciate its ideological implications and the hegemonic discourse of ableism within which it exists. In this context, the chapter highlights the question of disability in Hindi cinema and critically analyses the disability narrative in Hindi cinema. This chapter has studied selected contemporary Hindi Cinema to understand disability questions.

The fifth chapter, Epistemic Ashrāfiya-Morality and Urdu Theatre Public Sphere in Nineteenth-Century Bihar: Muslim Internal-Decoloniality argues that the introduction of Western-style stage theatre in nineteenth-century India emerged not only as a site to legitimise colonial power but also to contest colonial rule and ideologies. This chapter through the prism of Urdu theatre in the last quarter of the nineteenth century in the Indian province of Bihar, argues that beyond this grand binary division, the theatre also became a site to contest local caste-class-race-religion-based ideologies of domination. It examines how epistemic ashrāfiya morality that excluded shudra-dalit Muslims from knowledge production responded to the popularity of theatre and its ‘corrupting influences’; participation of ‘lowly’ women and men in theatre; threatening ashrāfiya moral authority; casteist prejudices; ashrāfiya collaboration with colonial power; marketised desire and consumption and other related issues.

The sixth chapter, Consuming and Producing Feminism through Indian Media: Analysis of Empowerment, Commodification in Culture Industry elucidates the ‘girl power’ rhetoric articulated in the popular and feminist discourses. While using the idea of commodification, this chapter demonstrates that not all ‘girl power’ rhetoric extends the desired opportunity for women to transcend individual consumption, proposing collective change in social relations. Furthermore, this chapter binds together the concepts of agency, empowerment and commodification in subsequent sections. This chapter demonstrates the fierceness of the ‘girl power’ rhetoric. With the support of empirical examples, this paper depicts the extent to which the commodified ‘girl power’ rhetoric has distorted feminist notions.

The seventh chapter, Mediations and the Body-Politic: The Problem of Representation Revisited argues how the question of representation has always been central to the analyses of media texts, especially in the context of studying images of the body. Subjecting textual configurations towards unravelling body politics in the context of analysing the ‘marginalized’ social groups remains a common way of approaching the problem of representation in contemporary media studies. This chapter takes a slight turn in its approach to the problem of representation and body politic by attempting a methodological realignment to underline the practices and effects that stem from text and its surroundings.

The eighth chapter Media, Minority Consciousness, Memory: Sikhs and 1984 addresses the question of Sikh minority and media, and what were some representations that Indian mass media constructed and popularised? How did these texts create a process of Sikh marginalisation? Why are these representations being contested most emphatically in the diaspora, and what are some components of new narratives that diasporic Sikhs are creating via digital technology? This chapter draws on a combination of methods, including content analysis of print media (English dallies), radio and television reports, commentaries from journalists and scholars, narrative analysis of 1984 websites and personal interviews with Sikhs in North America, the U.S. and Canada. This chapter also provides a critical appraisal of media imagery while highlighting the hitherto neglected Sikh point of view.

The ninth chapter Media, Social Media and the Gorkhaland Agitation in Darjeeling highlights the media and Gorkhaland agitation in Darjeeling. The chapter argues that media, primarily print capitalism, is considered an essential medium through which nationalism and collective identity were forged, but not until the advent of social media and 24×7 news channels did Gorkhaland movement receive any attention from the fourth pillar of democracy. This happened not only because of the ignorance of the region and the cause of the people by the media, but also due to the appalling situation of the media itself. Though the media plays an important role in a movement, it did not contribute anything to propagating the issues of the Gorkhaland movement at the national level. The use of social media helped the movement in taking the ground realities to people across the globe. With the help of social media, the movement gained momentum and protests and demonstrations were held not just in Darjeeling and other places in the country, but across different countries where even a handful of people from Darjeeling were residing. The use of social media in the agitation of 2017, and helping the leaders and, most importantly, the majority of the people to be legally, constitutionally and socially erudite and fight the state in their just and rightful demand of separation from the state of West Bengal.
