CHAPTER 1

Gender, Sexuality and Cinema
Reinforcing the Stereotype

RENUKA SINGH

INTRODUCTION

In 2015, a man in Australia escaped conviction on charges of stalking. Sandesh Baliga, who arrived in Australia from India three years prior, was arrested on charges of aggressively pursuing two different women—whom he made acquaintances with on separate occasions—while referring to himself as their boyfriend.1 In court, Mr. Baliga confessed that his behaviour was influenced by Bollywood movies which convinced him of the efficacy of relentless pursuit of women; the defence argued that obsession in such matters is ‘quite normal behaviour’ for Indian men.2 Acknowledging the peculiarity of the defendant’s cultural background, the Magistrate’s Court exempted him from conviction, on condition of good behaviour for the next five years. This event could be interpreted variously as a testament to the formidable influence of mass media in determining social norms and behaviour, as well as its capacity as a framework for constructing cultural stereotypes. Generalization of a deviant act—despite appropriate punishments existing—by describing it as ‘normal behaviour’ for Indian men, and the Court’s subsequent decision to sustain that argument in favour of exemption, is certainly a testament to a patriarchal substratum underlying modern jurisprudence, transcending socio-political and cultural boundaries.

However, the conviction in such obsessive and relentless pursuit of romantic gratification is not particular to any region such as India, nor is its depiction in mass media. The idea of a woman’s predisposition to succumb to the force of relentless pursuit is a ubiquitous conviction that forms much of the basis of modern sexual practices and performances of gender roles. Modes of pursuit and their varied depictions are periodically updated, revolving perpetually along the orbit of resolute male desire and a woman’s fait accompli of giving in to the same. The stubbornness of male desire and a woman’s inability (or desire) to overcome it, is understood as innate characteristics of masculine and feminine sexuality, and respectively act as boundaries for both. This natural and body-based understanding of social behaviour has neither been perpetual nor linear. Mapping the origins of social choices on a framework of gender is a practice that has a genesis, and is entwined with the evolution of a certain socio-political dispensation central to which is the concept of a self-conscious and knowing subject. Normalization of gendered discourses and gender identities routinely influence juridical decisions and punitive quantum. Conversely, such excesses get absorbed within the folds of embodied identities, as part of the law’s normalizing discourse. These discourses in turn constitute a larger vision of collective existence central to which are the requisites of confession and consent. Within this vision, violence, especially that of different forms of sexual crime and harassment, is understood variously, as the breakdown of the law’s deterrence against an individual’s natural and embodied instincts, as well as a lapse in social control. And it is within the moral contours of this vision that consent and its breach are appraised.

In contractarian and self-governing societies, mass media assumes its importance in terms of both, as a mirror of reflecting popular ideas as well as constructing and disseminating them. The scope of a particular medium is determined by the rules that govern the object of its engagement. In terms of research, Michel Foucault’s works provide a particularly well-calibrated framework to theorize the discourse between mass media and gender and sexuality. In terms of method, it implies that instead of the intent behind a certain discourse, it is the technology of the latter that is the unit of analysis. This at once relativizes agency to a certain technology of power rather than to certain biological and cultural determinisms and frees up space for more expansive mapping of gender politics.

SOCIAL BEHAVIOUR

The Industrial Revolution and the emergence of capitalism could be an ideal point of departure in constructing a genealogical understanding of the relationship between mass media and social behaviour. With the onslaught of the revolution, the state progressively assumes an inward gaze, preoccupying itself with problems on settled populations, increasingly routinized through strict discipline enforced in workhouses and factories, and generally in the course of everyday life in the nascent metropolis. The breakdown of feudalism followed by the emergence of free enterprise gave rise to new relationships of exploitation between individuals as well as, between individuals and their environment. Laws and customs, from this time on, are progressively aligned with the maintenance of this emerging relational network which simultaneously sustains the structure of civic life, while remaining at the centre of its engagements. However, the tautological nature of this emergent socio-economic order entails that its problems are also lingering. Due to the persistent nature of these problems, the concepts of degeneration and endemic serve as powerful models for explaining and dealing with them (Foucault: 2003). It also affects an inversion in terms of social security as henceforth, the logic and possibilities of both, crisis and security, are increasingly to be found within the society itself. Further, management or mitigation of these problems necessitates constant surveillance instead of zero-sum measures. A civic existence, therefore, is a constantly surveillant existence whereby the inward-gazing individual is a microcosm of the inward-gazing society. Society must be defended against itself as does the individual against its own. The responsibility of self-governance predicated upon knowledge of the self and its surroundings ensures the cultivation of a society that is both confessional and consensual. It marks the commencement of the journey of the knowing subject: a subject that is inward gazing while at the same time, responsible for and partaking in a collective and mutually consensual existence. Henceforth, it is this collective (population) that is the object of law; individual interest is displaced as the target of law, by what Foucault defines as ‘man-as-species’.3 It marks the emergence of a new form of rule—focussed upon fixing the parameters of a self-contained collective existence—which Foucault terms ‘biopolitics’.4 Thus, the knowing subject is essentially biopolitical, inducted into the process of knowing itself and its milieu from the earliest stages of its life. Public space is cultivated as a confessional space as it would constitute an important part of an individual’s learning discourse. However, neither is public space the only one for discursive learning nor is confession within this space unbridled by rules.

Public and Private Domain

The production of public-private dichotomy and characterization of the public space as liberating was achieved in no small measure by arrogating sexuality to the private domain. However, such a characterization of the public could find moral purchase only based on various confessional practices in myriad discourses of civic life. It is one’s confession of individual choices and practices of the private—expressed through various walks of public life—is what discursively integrates the public space as one of accountability, liberty and equality. Therefore, the true nature of public space derives its authority from this elementary factor of confession which in turn, derives its moral authority from the Christian concept of liberty through confession. The privation of sexuality, however, posed a problem for exhaustive confession in public discourse. Specifically, confession or expression of sexual matters had to bypass the prohibitions of bourgeois etiquette, yet be expressive enough to be considered as true. This was important because not only were the sexual practices and choices of the population increasingly becoming a core concern for the modern state, but because the very practices of expression and confession were instrumental in constituting certain discursive mechanisms and institutions that would assume authority in matters of determining meaning in modernity.

Rules of etiquette, decreeing a code of silence around sexuality and sexual matters in public discourse, and sourced out of old ascetic views of treating the body as an adversary, compelled innovation of counter codes, based upon two fundamental principles: first, fashion silence itself as a language of expression and second, secularize the body, consequently turning it into an object of legal rather than religious intervention. As an object of right, therefore, the body is personal. This is both, the formative concept of individual rights, as well as the stepping stone to a public life. Similarly, the practice of self-control in public life is intrinsic, both to individual character development as well as to the internal cohesion of society. However, the secularization of the body—a process that commenced with the Renaissance—never totally displaces religious and ascetic ideals but only renders their visibility much more obscure (Synott: 1992, 90-1). Thus, traditionally extolled virtues of heterosexuality, monogamy and so on, were institutionalized as ideal practices while prohibited practices were divided and re-channelized variously into domains of the law and those of the Church.

A basic modern impulse to keep public discourse sanitized and civil required proactive and constant surveillance against disruptive possibilities. The resultant vigilant character of the society was reified through incitement to discourse (Foucault: 1978)—something that is also key to understanding how meanings come to proliferate and normalize through the evolution of modernity. Essentially, not only had the society to be defended against imminent and ongoing attacks, but newer possibilities were to be apprehended and neutralized. Privation of sexuality, coupled with the essential value of its control to the health of the population, necessitated a paradoxical obligation to maintain a civilized silence around it while concurrently talking about it in as many discursive ways; this widespread proliferation of sexuality in public discourse occurs in the way of a stocktaking, as a practice in accountability and hence, the obligation to do so.5 The gaze that took stock of the body through various institutional mechanisms stripped the body of its various cultural attributes and replaced them with biological determinisms. This delocalization of the body from its cultural contingencies is precisely what secularizes it. Accordingly, sex is no longer simply an object of condemnation, but of administration and optimization. It is towards these ends that increasing policing of sex from the eighteenth century onwards is directed at, transforming it (sex) into a ‘public potential’.6 Thus, the essentialization of the public as an expressly confessional space wherein everything—tastes, opinions, practices—is considered to be a conscious avowal of what one truly is. It is also a pedagogical process as every decree and obligation to confess comes with the rider of knowing oneself, facilitating a better understanding of one’s true impulses and potential. This is therefore a society in which authority is predicated predominantly upon knowledge and predictability.

While modern, secular law becomes one of the most prominent institutions to discursive the body, the latter’s objectification and display in public discourse also occurs majorly through the discourse of medicine. Discovered as an object of medical intervention, the body is conditioned by that true scientific discourse as the boundary of individual existence; sexuality, sexual desire and pleasure become the basis of all motivation and therefore, their control and management accrue as matters of social control. In medicine, a certain power develops that is predicated upon the act of investigation, of prying open the human body and laying bare the roots of every anatomical working that simulates various unnatural pleasures (homosexuality, bestiality, necrophilia and so). The authority of medicine is owed to the existence of the body as its object, while at the same time, medicalized descriptions of human behaviour and choices increasingly gain currency in public discourse as reflections of true discourses. Consequently, morality itself, deviance, and crime are increasingly discovered as owing out of biological impulses and stimuli rather than out of the displacement of faith. Cumulatively, these practices constitute a normalizing discourse, central to which is a double impetus of pleasure and power.7 Accordingly, medicinal power derives its vitality and specific pleasures from monitoring, questioning, prying open, and having authority over the most personal of objects—the body—and its minute and obscure workings. Against this, a very different kind of pleasure emerges, one which derives not out of conformation but out of evasion of not just medicinal but various other kinds of normalizing discourses. It is important to note here that the scope for the latter kind of pleasure is determined by the points on which a certain mechanism chooses to apply itself. In essence, the normalization of heteronormativity and monogamy in modernizing discourses, has had just as much effect in expanding the scope of sexuality and sexual choices by having placed the body in the middle of a tug-of-war between pleasure and power. It is within the binding effect of this dual pull that modern subjectivity emerges. With every prohibition leading to the possibility of a concomitant evasion, modernity emerges as an optimizing discourse.

BODY AND THE GENDER DICHOTOMY

Secularization of the body is generally considered as a transformative step through which the body—otherwise a private object—is discovered both as the basis of truth, as well as a yardstick against which truth or authenticity can be measured. This discovery of the ubiquity of the body with its relationship to truth serves as the basis of society’s justification for monitoring, controlling, classifying, and managing bodies—all as matters of social security which is an obligation borne out of the social contract to constantly guard against possibilities of exposure to the state of nature. Reduction of the body as an a priori object of nature, and conflating its control with social security, conceptualizes it (body) as the very fabric of social existence; thus, the body is the first line of security against the state of nature as well as something which would invariably challenge social cohesion if not controlled and channelized. This is also how sex is transformed into a public potential, its control accruing as a matter of sustaining a certain vision of existence that is predicated upon constantly mapping the body in search of signs of truth. The matter of investigation and permissibility of margins of error in its methods derive their authority and sanction from the supreme obligation to defend the social contract. As such, these are necessary preconditions to the enjoyment of rights. The cartography of the body, however, is not an open-ended exercise; the scope of mapping the body is determined along the lines of gender binaries. Accordingly, masculinity and femininity are the basic yardsticks against which narratives of normality and abnormality or marginality are thrashed out. The stabilization of gender dichotomy as true, is caused by what could be termed as a pan-sexualization of the body,8 a process which simultaneously naturalizes the body while at the same time places it in an adversarial position vis-à-vis the progress of civilization. Reduction of the body to its sexuality on one hand, and the discovery of bodily desire at the basis of every human motivation on the other, cumulatively authorizes the secularization of the body as well as its channelization into productive categories. Gender binaries, heterosexuality, public-private dichotomy and so on, are categories that are stabilized through optimization and channelization, a process that Menon describes—in the context of law’s normalizing discourse—as violence of categories (Menon: 2007, 17). This foundational violence is constitutive of both, individual rights and the public-private dichotomy. Appropriation of women within the realm of the private and projection of the public sphere as most appropriate for the man is achieved based on perceived gender-based determinisms: male bodies, perceived as clearly bounded and solid, are considered as lending naturally to control and hence, reflecting the ideal condition of a well-guarded public space. On the other hand, women’s bodies, perceived as disorderly, penetrable, subject to cyclical changes and divisible through reproduction, challenge the very idea of a closed and controllable body surface. The resultant hierarchy between man and woman, and the superiority of the former in relation to the latter, is one upon which the public-private dichotomy is mapped. This perceived gender hierarchy is cemented not through recourse to religion but through science.9 Optimization and channelization of the sexualized body occur along the lines of avowedly scientific truths. Depiction of sexual violence as well as methods of such depictions, reinforce gender binaries while at the same time expanding the scope of discovering newer peculiarities that can either be explained along binary lines or referred to the normalizing discourses of medicine and/or the law. Censorship therefore is based on moral codes that claim to derive out of (perceived) true discourses and prohibit or permit depending upon what is perceived as normal or otherwise. The conflation of the natural, the normal, and the moral into a singular framework of interpretation are what colours the concepts of rights, consent, desire, and so. An incitement to discourse, therefore, is also an incitement to categorize whereby confession and control are authorized for the sake of ever-increasing scope for prediction, apprehension and prevention of threats to the social fabric. Additionally, categories also facilitate the channelization of the body into productive relationships with each other and the environment, a process that allows for seamless convergence between social security and capitalism.

REEL AND THE REAL

Over three decades after the film Last Tango in Paris (1972) was released and found critical acclaim all over the world as an excellent erotic drama, the lead actress in the movie, Maria Schneider revealed the real-life violation that occurred as part of making this masterpiece. The scene in question was a rape scene in which Marlon Brando uses butter as a lubricant before forcing himself onto Schneider. The actress, who died in 2011, had revealed that although she was aware of the rape scene, she wasn’t aware of the specific detail up until the part was being played out. The ‘sequence of the butter’, as it turns out, was an off-script improvisation that director Bernardo Bertolucci came up with and subsequently, took Brando into confidence about. In an interview from 2013, Bertolucci confessed that he did not update Schneider about the improvisation because he wanted the latter to ‘react as a girl, not as an actress’. And that he wanted her ‘to react humiliated’. When Bertolucci’s interview resurfaced on the internet in 2016, he reacted to the ensuing controversy by saying that it was based on a ‘ridiculous misunderstanding’.10

The decision to forego the consent of the actress portraying the condition of being raped, is based first, on a certain drive to distinguish the work as pushing the boundaries of authentic depiction and second, a well-entrenched scepticism about the capacity of professional performers—in this case, a woman—of being able to capture this projected reality in all its realistic authenticity, or at least not to the degree that the director deems as adequately authentic. In other words, the director wanted the actress to not just act but to feel raped, be raped. True to his expectations, Schneider confessed to feeling humiliated and ‘a little raped’.11 As much is also clear, Bertolucci’s justification of his decision—most importantly to himself—is expressed in his capacity as a director and the craft of his art. Consent can be foregone or taken for granted, only if in the name of authenticity in the pursuit of art. The setting in which Bertolucci’s imagination flared up, is also immensely relevant in the entire affair. According to the director, he and Brando were having breakfast one day during the shoot; and there was on the table, a baguette and butter. Of his submission, he and Brando then ‘looked at each other’ and both knew what they wanted.12 This apparent silence around what is, an act of fetish, viz., usage of butter as a lubricant, is in itself a confession of proscribed desires, its confessionary nature testified even further through the apparent, express, and ready convergence of both men’s assumptions and their mutual understanding and acknowledgement of the same. Further, the usage of butter as an external implement in the act of sex reflects a very particular tendency that equates the pan-sexualization of public space with liberation. This fetishization of sex by optimizing bodily functions through external implementation is what Foucault describes as the desexualisation of the body, an act that leads to an intensification of pleasure (Foucault: 1996, 212). An exhaustive codification of pleasure according to the laws of sex makes the latter the limit of the former. Subsequently, a ‘decoding’ or revealing of all secrets of pleasure (in terms of sex) is still widely viewed as ‘liberating’.13 This liberation through an active and participative process of experiencing, learning, and archiving the secrets of pleasure and sex, is what is largely responsible for not just pornographic depictions but also the deployment of taboo tactics such as non-consensual recording or documentation (as in the ‘sequence of the butter’), thereby simulating a pornographic trope in real life. While behind the lens it is this particularly calibrated deployment of a taboo that leads to the experience of rape; in front of the lens, the taboo is something that intensifies pleasure and liberates senses; the rape and its peculiarities, on screen, assumes a liberatory potential by uncovering a supposedly repressed truth.

There is another substratum of violence underlining it all, and something inherent in the pivot of filmmaking itself that is, the camera. Functioning as a symbol of the fourth wall in filmmaking, the camera is an offshoot of the neutral gaze of science, expressed in the perceived authenticity of the visual register itself. The construction of real life through the camera is limited only through the intentions of the one directing it. Conversely, the authority of the director ought to be understood as emanating out of the infallibility of the technology that he is wielding. In The Question Concerning Technology (1977), Heidegger points out that etymologically, the name technology stems from the Greek term technicon, which is an element of technē. Technē denotes both, activities and skills of craftsmen as well as arts of the mind and fine arts, and is linked from the earliest time, with the word epistēmē (Heidegger: 12-13). Technology, therefore, functions essentially in the field of knowledge but according to a very particular method. Technology is a mode of revealing that brings forth the truth about the form of something before its actual appearance. Modern technology too is a mode of revealing, but not in the more poetic sense as in Classical Greek narrative (Poiēsis) but in the form of a challenge to nature, of its exploitation.14 This conflation of the philosophy of knowledge through revelation, with the exploitation of nature (sexualized body as a natural object), and its subsequent deployment in the directed depiction of real life, is the primary source of acceptability and desire of not just specific forms of depiction but also of methods that guide the screening of such depictions. In Last Tango, the rape scene not only fetishizes and desexualizes the human body but clearly, the exceptionality of that sequence is highlighted even further in the context of Bertolucci’s decision to hide it from Schneider who in this scene, would be the one ‘taking it’ while Brando would be the one ‘giving/doing it’. After all, it is to accentuate Brando’s appendage that the butter is used. The assumed latency of Schneider’s role in the situation could be regarded as part reason for her being left out of the information regarding updating the script. Any assumption of the actress’ latency in the scene reflects an unquestionable conviction in the neutral gaze of the camera (and the crew) and a lacuna of appreciation of differences that underlie between a naked man and a naked woman when positioned in front of that neutral gaze of technology. This can be theorized in the context of Brando’s confession that he felt ‘humiliated’ while shooting a full frontal nude scene for the same movie when he failed to achieve a full erection on account of it being cold.15 First, it is important to note here that Brando’s self-consciousness and humiliation are despite being in front of the neutral gaze. Second, his humiliation is borne not per sé out of nudity but the inability to attain the full measure that is captured in the nude state. The essentialization of sexual vitality that is captured in this event is only further constitutive of ideal body types and capacities. Therefore, if Brando could indeed achieve a full erection—which he doubtless did in the later course of shooting—his nudity would verily serve as a signifier of power and virility in all its open glory. For a woman, to be revealing herself in front of the camera’s gaze in a candid fashion as such, is vastly different because the privation, concealment, and presentation of the woman’s body and its sexual being are readily conflated with her honour and dignity. The rule of modesty dictates that the visibility of a woman’s body is narrowly streamlined according to the purpose that is being served and the space that the body inhabits. It is precisely due to the difficulty of maintaining decreed standards of modesty in the public space that the latter is usually deemed as less conducive for a woman’s being. Consequently, the very desire of a woman to step out in the public sphere is variously considered suspect, risky, disruptive, a lack of self-control and so forth. The interoperability of rape, promiscuity, and moral turpitude in various discourses is a fact that is not particular to any culture or time.16 However, violence is not the essence of technology but a permanent capacity of it, something that is triggered as per the direction of its wielding, and according to the rules that guide its object. It is certainly pedagogical as positioning against its gaze instills as well as awakens the true feelings of one’s gender and its various natural attributes.

The 2000 movie Bawandar—depicting the 1992 gangrape and struggle of Bhanwari Devi, a women’s welfare worker affiliated with the Government of Rajasthan—directed by Jagmohan Mundhra, was fairly extolled for its candid and daring depiction of a violent and disturbing reality, while also being harshly criticized for being unnecessarily graphic and prurient in those depictions. The criticism assumed more heft when it turned out that Mundhra’s interactions with Devi had been limited to one meeting leading up to the production of the film, and that Devi had neither seen the film nor received any money from the director.17 Mundhra of course refuted these allegations with appropriate counters. While this certainly problematizes the entire process of making the film and casts it in a light of ethical suspicion, the particularities of the film itself inspire a strong suspicion regarding consultation with the rape survivor about the paradigms of the event. While the gangrape was perpetrated by Gurjars and doubtless had an obvious caste dimension to it, to even out this factor in the movie, Devi’s lawyer is depicted as being a Gurjar while in real life her lawyer was a Rajput.18 There is, politely put, scant reason to doubt that this rather sophisticated policy of a balanced portrayal of caste values, was run by Bhanwari Devi and sanctioned with the stamp of her approval. Further, in a much more stupendous blunder, a much younger actress (Nandita Das) was cast in the role of the survivor while the latter was forty years old at the time of her encounter (1992). Not enough can be said about the damage that this omission causes to the legacy of the survivors, particularly contingent experience and struggle. In his justification, Mundhra states that although inspired by Devi’s case, the film is not a biography and that certain amends are required to add drama to a film.19 He does not explain whether this requisite to add drama is one that is generic to the logic of Hindi movies, or one that is particular to the depiction of sexual and caste violence that is rampant in society and to which, people are desensitized; to the latter intention of raising awareness through cinematic depiction is robbed of its apparent progressive value if attempted without consent of the rape survivor. Notwithstanding his clarification, as much is clear that in the depiction of the event, the director prioritizes the power of his imagination over the actual event. In doing so, he decouples the gravity of the event from the event itself thereby, taking the final product as far away as possible from reality. This then is a truth that essentially hinges upon the imagination of the authority and authority of that imagination.

Films that normalize sexual violence in reinforcing gender binaries are dime-a-dozen and one is spoilt for choice. Let’s consider the movie Tum Mere Ho (1990) in which the lead roles characters are played by the-then-newcomers Aamir Khan and Juhi Chawla. The film is one of the several Nagin films, a fad that characterized Bollywood for quite a while. As such, the movie can already be reckoned as inside a ready trope. At one point in the movie, Khan’s snake charmer cum medicine man mentor explains to one of their clients (Khurana Saab) that on the night of nag panchami, it is a customary practice among his Pahari Kabila (hilly tribes) for the unmarried men to try and forcefully carry away whichever maiden they like, and if the hijack is a success, the twain are married off. He explains how this is not an easy task and that the woman aggressively tries to repel the man’s advances because, as he explains, women like to have husbands who are ‘stronger’ than them.20 Khurana Saab is then invited to stay back and see it all for himself. Khurana excuses himself from the inevitable, and on the night of nag panchami, Khan (Shiva), mock assaults Chawla (Paro), while the latter does everything that is expected of her, i.e. mock repel the attack on her mock integrity, finally being real-time carried away on Khan’s shoulders inside a small hut,21 while a cheering crowd cheers away almost gratefully. In this, the trope of rape is skilfully deployed yet casually, but in a way that manages to pass the censor board’s scrutiny, camouflages it under the garb of riwaz (custom) and a song and dance routine; this routine itself is indicative of the acceptability of the trope both within the story as well as to the viewers. Now, consider this as a prelude to the influence that later Bollywood movies probably exerted over the Indian-origin man arrested in Australia on charges of stalking. The masculine belief that access, not to consent, but to a woman’s sexuality can be obtained in ways that still do not pose an ethical problem, is sustained based on faith in the resolve of masculine desire and a deep-seated inability in a woman to repel that desire. The idea of woman as embodying nature and its various quirks—not least of which is explained away through the archaic but still floating concept of hysteria and a woman’s inability to cope with her sexuality—is something that is ancient and has evolved particularly to historical developments across time and space. As such, the relational essence of heterosexuality is essentially adversarial in which a man’s pursuit of a woman is essentially in the way of a conquest. A conquest of feminine sexuality, which does not lend itself too easily to control, is very much like nature itself and hence, has to be similarly opened up. The trope of adversity and conquest in Tum Mere Ho is much more explicit and self-explanatory in Khan’s struggles with his arch nemesis, the nagin (played by Kalpana Iyer; as per the industry norms in Bollywood, most human-snake hybrid creatures that end up bagging roles in movies happen to be females!). The mythical, mystical creature that the nagin is, can only be defeated by magic, faith, and resolve (achievable through, among other things), the snake charmer’s flute, and another conquered creature of nature, Shiva’s pet snake, Naagraj (Snake King/King of Snakes). Shiva is an alpha male who is scoring all kinds of wins against nature! Some, he wants to win (conquer), some, he has to!

Woman as a conduit of mystical powers and natural forces, and her sexuality as one of the chief sources as well as means of wielding that power, is a cumulative perception that is normalized in various discourses, from men’s rights activism to modern day witch-hunting, to explanations for romantic incompatibility. A most recent instance is the unfortunate death of budding actor Sushant Singh and the widespread public deliberation on the matter that has ensued following that event. Allegations about Singh’s girlfriend Rhea Chakraborty—of being a gold digger and a disloyal partner—scaled a new low when she was branded a witch, engaging in jaadu tona (black magic) to trap eligible men. The attack was specific in pointing out Chakraborty’s Bengali ethnicity while highlighting the fact that ‘Bengali girls are dominating’. There is just too much load to unpack over here but, the obvious fetishization of a woman’s sexuality and the dangers that would befall anyone who misjudges it turns all heterosexual pursuit into a conquest and a guilty pleasure for man. For him, a heterosexual desire is always under the shadow of a constant caveat of existential threat; the woman can be approached either by walking on eggshells or by proving the masculine mettle by taking nature (woman) on and following through to victory. Else, it is akin to riding the tigress. And thereby accrues the intensification of pleasure.

Further, the test of misconception is made even tougher with an absurd requisite of belief in already having married; in this, the law finds itself a squeezed space of not acknowledging the fact of marital rape, while at the same time reinforcing the institution of marriage as sacrosanct by terming sex outside marriage as ‘promiscuous’. But at the same time, the outrage and charge of rape brought in by the prosecutrix stems out of the ground as the law’s, i.e. the permissibility of sex only within marriage.
