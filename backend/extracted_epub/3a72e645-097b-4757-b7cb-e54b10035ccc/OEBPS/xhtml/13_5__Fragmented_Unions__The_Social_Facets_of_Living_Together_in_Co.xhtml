INTRODUTION

In sociology, marriage is one of the many important social institutions alongside religious, educational, political, economic and employment-related institutions. In the normative (conventional) definition marriage is a permanent (lifelong) relationship between male and female for permanent sexual relation, reproduction of human life and sharing all matters of their lives. Along with this, marriage is also considered a socio-cultural right and responsibility. These rights and responsibilities are not only towards their partner but also towards their in-laws. In common terms, marriage is a relationship between two opposite sexes, who are young by their age, sexually virgin as well as also monogamous. In this regard heterosexual marriage is often analysed as an ‘oppressive institution’ toward women from view point of Marxist and feminist perspective. Sexual relationship before marriage, without marital status and in widowhood was restricted specially toward women in Hindu religious society. It manifested in practices like the ‘Sati’1 system and widowhood2 which reflect without colour red of Hindu religion and the wearing of white dress in a wedding occasion in Christianity.3 Giving birth without wedding is legally restricted in various countries.4

In one sentence, marriage is both social and legal foundation to fulfil a person’s sexual and reproductive desire, to live together, to share and collective earning, sharing and owning of their property and mutual right and responsibility towards their partner and in-laws. Philosophical developments offer a logical understanding of the phenomena, while science and technology provide support. The capitalist mode of production has scattered individuals from familiar societies to unknown ones, leading to alienation. With these changes in social conceptions normative definition of marriage has also changed. With the growth of science and technology the forms of purity and pollution of sexuality started to be criticized and became less restricted. At present, we can see various rupture in marriage as social institution. We can see these ruptures in various aspects of society, i.e. people prefer living together (cohabitation) and many people fall in love with those people who belong from the same sex. It is also seen that many people live without marriage, without any partner and many women became mothers out of wedlock. Divorce and remarriage or late-age marriage has become common all over the world.

Marriage as social institution we can analyse through various sociological theories, such as Marxism, Feminism, Structural Functionality and Modernism. From the Marxist point of view marriage is an oppressive institution toward women and children so we need to abolish it. Feminism also has nearly the same thought that it’s oppressive through the various ways toward women. But structural functionalist perspective focuses on social cohesion and social integrity which support to maintain social order and run society functionally by marriage too. So social institution became important from the viewpoint of structural functionalism. Theories of modernism, which provide the aspect of rational thought, behave and function became modern in society with the changing social structure. Modernism specially can be seen on Gemeinschalf and Gesellschaft of Ferdinand Tönnies (1957). Mechanical solidarity and organic solidarity are a part of Emile Durkheim’s theory of the development of societies in, The Division of Labour in Society (1893).

In this article, I will provide a concise theoretical exploration of matrimony and examine the global dynamics of living together (cohabitation), with a special focus on the context within Nepal.

MARXIST CRITICISM TO MARRIAGE
AND FAMILY

At the dawn of the Marxist philosophy, with the declaration of communist movement through The Communist Manifesto in 1848, Marx and Engels declare their position on the modern bourgeois family relation as oppressive toward women and will eventually disappear with the capitalist mode of production. Marx and Engels wrote:

Abolition of the family! Even the most radical flare up at this infamous proposal of the communists.

On what foundation is the present family, the bourgeois family based? On capital, on private gain. In its completely developed form, this family exists only among the bourgeoisie. But this state of things finds its complement in the practical absence of the family among the proletarians and in public prostitution. The bourgeois family will vanish as a matter of course when its complement vanishes and both will vanish with the vanishing of capital. (Marx and Engels, 1848).

After the declaration of The Communist Manifesto Engels published his groundbreaking book Family, Private Property, and Origins of State in 1884 based on Morgan’s work and Marx’s notes. The book reached a wider audience and advocated the abolition of family structure.

However, now in the European continent there is the debate that living together (cohabitation) relation rose after the demolition of Soviet Union. Arland Thornton and Dimiter Philipov write in their article:

In central and Eastern Europe, during the late 1980s and early 1990s, there were major political changes, such as the fall of communist governments. These societies entered a new era of increased social freedom, less rigid rules and less authoritarian governments. They interacted with western Europe and some become members of the European Union. As a result, the pattern of family life has changed, marriage rates have declined and marriage was postponed to a later age. Cohabitation and births to unmarried mothers increased and in some countries the increase was very quick (Thornton and Philipov, 2009).

It shows that the communism does not force to abolish the marriage relationship.

MODERNISM ON MARRIAGE AND FAMILY

Modernity theory suggests that scientific advancement has diminished religious influence on individuals, particularly regarding sexuality. Living with emotional or physical attachment was stigmatized in Christian society too. But Pope Francis started to give marital status for those people who were in live-in relationship. According to the news published on 15 September 2014 at Australian Broadcasting Corporation (ABC), ‘Pope Francis break taboo by marrying cohabiting couples, conducts mass wedding ceremony.’ Liberal political thought also provides the ground to develop personal relationship as love partner, sex partner or other relationship.

FEMINIST CRITIQUE ON MARRIAGE
AND FAMILY

Like Marxists, feminists also criticize the concept of marriage. From the feminist corner, marriage controls the women’s dignity economically, socio-culturally, symbolically and on their identity by and under patriarchal norms, values, power and politics. It is not only in one society, nearly all over the world.

Socio-culturally, white wedding dress of Christianity is depicted as the virginity of women and non-virgin girl negatively. And also, Christianity did not allow the sexual desire of women. Clare Chambers (2013) write on her article ‘The Marriage Free State’:

The white wedding is replete with sexist imagery the father ‘giving away’ the bride; the white dress symbolizing the bride’s virginity and emphasizing the importance of her appearance; the vows to obey the husband; the minister telling the husband ‘you may now kiss the bride’ (rather than the bride herself giving permission, on indeed initiating or at least equally participating in the act of kissing); the reception at which, traditionally, all the speeches are given by men; the wife surrendering her own name and taking her husband’s.

Socio-cultural norms in Nepal, once manifested in the Sati and Widow practices, now continue through early teen Gunyu Choli and Kanyadan during marriage. Despite the legal abolition of Sati, these enduring customs still shape women’s dignity and autonomy.

Economically, marriage provides the ground for gendered division of labour, which is unpaid care work and counted as non-productive work. Talking about feminists, the groundbreaking work of Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique (1963) gives the new way of thought of marriage as the unsolved problem of materially prosperous housewife of America. She starts this book with the description she called ‘the problem that has no name’. This unnamed problem widely spread unhappiness of women in the 1950s and 1960s America and after 1990s in Nepal. Married women were unhappy despite good living condition and having a child too. On her book Friedan also questions the women’s magazine, women’s education system and advertisers for creating this widespread image of women. The harmful effects induced by this image were that it cornered women into the domestic sphere and it led many women to lose their own identity.

We can see a similar criticism in Nepali Feminist movement after 1990s political change and they have advocated the count of women’s domestic work. The publication of Status of Women in Nepal: Statistical Profile of Nepalese Women (1979), gives a logical understanding of unpaid domestic work also as work. Government of Nepal is still unable to incorporate how to recognize domestic work on system. But it creates great social understanding which provide the knowledge to develop women’s liberation and individual’s freedom.

RISE OF LIVING TOGETHER

Living together is a relationship between two individual living as a couple without socio-cultural and legal recognition and they don’t have socio-cultural as well as a legal right and responsibility too. They share everything such as household responsibility, economic condition, sexual desire and pleasure without sociocultural and legal recognition. There are various words as synonyms of ‘living together’5 but in this article all these terminologies are used as only ‘living together’.

Living together is an alternative or modern form of conventional marriage institution. There is diverse analysis on the debate that ‘living together’ is new phenomenon or old phenomenon. Some writers wrote that it’s a new phenomenon (Scott, 2008), (Chambers, 2013), (Thornton and Philipov, 2009), (Kien, 2018) and Anastasia de Waal who is a head of the Education and family Unit of Civitas, a UK-based think tank wrote in his article Cohabitation that ‘Cohabitation, when a couple live together in a sexual partnership without being married, is not a new phenomenon. It is estimated between the mid-eighteenth and mid-nineteenth centuries, either as a precursor to marriage or as an informal alternative.’

But in the same article he writes that cohabitation became progressively more widespread across society toward the second half of the twentieth century, with a significant rise since the mid-1980. So, there can be no contention that living together (cohabitation) was not a new phenomenon, it was prevalent in a scattered way before 1970s too, but it rapidly increased after 1970s. In Nepal too, living together was rising after the political change of 1990s, in the areas of comparatively developed urban areas like Kathmandu, Pokhara, Dharan. But, in Nepal we do not have any authentic data stating how many people are in live-in relationship. The big survey, such as Census, Nepal Demographic Health Survey, Nepal Living Standard Survey of government are still unable to recognize that living together has also been practiced in Nepali society.

In the absence of authentic data, we do not know how many people are in live-in relationship. According to the news published in Setopati in 2080,6 monthly ten to fifteen young girls came to the Women Commission to register complain that ‘after living together he did not get married with me’. This data indicates that there are various couple who live under the live-in relationship.

In the public sphere, we engage in the debate concerning the social change and recognition of living together. It was stigmatized as socially unsocial behaviour, cultural sin, and over freedom. Living together is depicted in Nepali news (paper, radio, TV and social media such as Facebook, Twitter, YouTube) comes as a cause of crime, such as killing,7, 8, 9 domestic violence,10 depression and suicide11, honey trapping, birth of unwanted child and violence12, leaving a child in garbage, forest and infanticide. Sometimes it is depicted as a bargaining tool to exploit another partner. And from the side of human right activist, ‘living together’ relationship is also suppressive for women and girls.

Social perception also depends on the level of knowledge, freedom, awareness and social connectivity on how people enjoy with living together. Empirically we can see a prism of live-in relationship and in these multicoloured relationship impact differently in individual’s life. Some people are enjoying it as liberation and freedom, while some are waiting for time and situation to get married, some individual are suffering from the burden of unwanted child, some people are suffering from domestic violence and some people lose their life through the killing too, some people are in psychological trauma, threat of life, some children are suffering economically, physically and emotionally through the parent’s choice of living together. With the lack of authentic data, we do not know how many people have better peaceful relationship and how many individuals are suffering from it.

However, there are various forms of living together in Nepal such as early age marriage which are socio-culturally recognized but legally not married, heterosexual couple and legally not recognized youth heterosexual couple, socio-culturally not recognized homosexual couple, extramarital relationship holder couple and so on. Living relationship is socially stigmatizing, controversial and sensitive relationship even in highly modern and educated Kathmandu valley.

Nepal police office, courts, media and NGO worker often see a dark side of living together.13 Rather than the claim of the Marxism, Feminism and Modernism, the public face of living together as the alternative of marriage relation ‘as oppressive institution’ is vulgar for those people. Along with the killing, depression, suicide, there are various impact on children whose parents are not legally recognized as husband and wife. High rate of illegal abortion, leaving a child in garbage, domestic violence, facing difficulties to get birth certificate, getting government’s identity such as citizenship is hard without the marriage registration of parents. Socially they are unable to fully enjoy with their parent’s culture and social surrounding, especially with their grandparent, uncle, aunts and cousins and they are also not recognized by these social groups.

Academically we have few research on living together in Nepal. One among this few research is of Bishnu Prasad Dahal’s ‘Live-in relationship’ towards a new pattern of adult life in Kathmandu (2020). His study is based on case studies on Kathmandu District Court after separation of individual from the living together. He wrote that:

The field study was carried out amongst respondents who go away from living together and those respondents were key respondents who filed the cases in Kathmandu District Court after separation. Most of the cases are filed under the clauses that the female partner was raped by her male partner while they were in live-in relationship.

This research is unable to give the cause of living together rather it gives the glimpses of violence within the live-in relationship.

As authentic data depicts, Government of Nepal also does not have legal background to recognize the live-in relationship and formulation of the separate law on living together. But it is clear from Article 74 of the Civil Code of 2074 that ‘if a woman is found to have conceived and given birth to a child through physical contact with a man, such marriage shall be deemed to have occurred automatically’. It’s somehow the ground of legal recognition of child and father relationship. But not a solution of forced abortion, negligence, domestic violence against partner (not only a women) and child. This legal recognition is problematic too. Advocate Alpana Bhandari writes on her blog:

This means that if a child is born while a man and a woman are in physical contact, the law seeks to encompass both the child and the relationship that has been established in this manner. However, it should not be assumed that every child born in this manner is the result of a cohabitation connection. A baby can be born when a woman is sexually abused, when a man and a woman engage in sexual activity to satisfy their sexual desires, or when they engage in romantic activity.

Bhandari and senior advocate Shashi Adhikari have similar thoughts toward the provision of Civil Code of 2074. It’s not the legal recognition of living together, and it’s unable to solve the problem relating to the living together.

To recognize the live-in relationship, in 2078 two different committee of House of Representatives, i.e. Women and Social Committee and Law, Justice and Human Rights Committee discussed the issue of living together and criminalization of relationship. These two different meetings focused on registration of the relationship as couple under the government’s rules and regulation. Similarly, senior advocate Shashi Adhikari, Mohan Acharya have been advocating for legal recognition of live-in relationship. Last time, in first Chaitra 2080, advocate Annu Bhattarai filed a writ petition to Supreme Court urging for the legal status for live-in relationship.14

But from the corner of LGBTIQ community, they are focused on same sex marriage, and demanding the marital right as heterosexual people. After the rise of Gay liberation movement particularly and LGBTIQ movement generally demanding the marital status indicate that marriage is also a privilege to enjoy the sexual, reproductive and socio-cultural life. And there is a prominent question, which form of marriage is oppressive? Or there is a question ‘Marriage is oppressive for whom?’

Clare Chambers, a British political philosopher at the Faculty of Philosophy at the University of Cambridge, writes on her article The Marriage Free State which was published on 2013, that there are two types of critics specially from the feminist corner such as marriage as oppressive institution and it is privileges only for those who has hetero sexuality. In Nepal too for some individual, live-in relationship is preferred because they do not have the legal privilege to register their relationship and socio-culturally family and society are unable to accept their relationship.

As Chambers states, also in Nepal marriage is a privilege to those people who are unable to register their relationship and they also suffered from emotional breakdown, multiple relationship and violence. According to Durga Thapa, whose interview is collected in the book named Samanata Ko Rile Daud (2021), where she is one among twelve interviewees. She said that there are various cases of emotional torture, multiple relationship, battering and economic exploitation in the same sex relationship too. Within the women right movement in Nepal, they are together with other women in some part of movement. But same-sex (lesbian) women are now in that position to fight for already established right for women. These already established right is right to get marriage and to establish own family. Thapa (2021) said,

State and society do not acknowledge and has not allowed the same sex relationship of women. Through the same sex relationship, we are fighting for social and legal recognition for to enjoy the marital life and established the family.

So, various factors play important role to push an individual to live in live-in relationship. These factors include rising literacy, information and fictitious thought and search of romantic love relationship, not clear for lifelong commitment but searching a sex partner, urbanization which provide the sense of privacy, freedom and hidden space for relationship, education system which always teach the sense of freedom and sometimes separate from the traditional family setting, mass migration and high division of labour isolate the individual, limitation of legal provision; on a same sex relationship, double partnership, early age marriage. Some of these causes are interconnected to each other. We are unable to declare which factors are prominent and which factors are less important without conducting detail research.

CONCLUSION

Marriage, a pivotal social institution, has evolved from a normative union between a man and a woman for procreation and life-sharing to a more complex concept influenced by socio-cultural rights, responsibilities and philosophical ideologies. Traditionally, marriage was seen as a permanent, monogamous bond, often criticized from Marxist and feminist perspectives as oppressive, particularly towards women. Restrictions on sexual relationships outside of marriage, especially for women, were symbolized by practices like the Sati system and the wearing of white in Christian weddings, reflecting societal notions of purity.

The rise of science and technology, alongside the capitalist mode of production, has led to a re-evaluation of these norms, with increasing acceptance of living together, same-sex relationships, single parenthood, divorce, and remarriage. This shift reflects a broader societal transformation from well-known communal living to a more individualistic existence.

Sociological theories offer diverse lenses to analyse marriage. Marxism views it as an oppressive institution that should be abolished; feminism aligns with this view, highlighting the patriarchal control over women; structural functionalism emphasizes marriage’s role in social cohesion and order; and modernism focuses on rationality and the evolution of societal functions.

Marx and Engels, in their Communist Manifesto, argued that the bourgeois family structure, based on capital and private gain, would disappear with capitalism. Later developments in Europe, particularly after the fall of communist governments, saw a decline in marriage rates and a rise in cohabitation and births outside of marriage, suggesting that communism did not mandate the abolition of marriage.

Modernism posits that the weakening of religious control over individuals, particularly regarding sexuality, has led to a more liberal approach to relationships. Pope Francis’s recognition of cohabiting couples and the liberal political thought advocating for personal relationships beyond traditional marriage further illustrate this trend.

In summary, marriage as an institution is undergoing significant changes, reflecting the dynamic interplay between socio-cultural norms, legal frameworks, and philosophical thought. This article aims to provide a theoretical understanding of marriage and analyse the global and situation in Nepal, considering various sociological theories and critiques.
