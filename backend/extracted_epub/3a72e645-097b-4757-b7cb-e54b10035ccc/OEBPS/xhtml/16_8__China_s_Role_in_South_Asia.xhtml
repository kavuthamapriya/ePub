INTRODUCTION

The rising power China has implications for the international system as it is challenging the global structure, but in terms of security its implications are more visible at the regional level. China is the biggest neighbour of all the South Asian countries. The country has age-old, multidimensional, deep-rooted and good relations with all the member states of the regional organization SAARC. Chinese soft power has reached the grassroots in South Asian region. China’s role in this region is multidimensional and increasing day by day. For China, getting the global power status, South Asian region plays a vital role and its escalating engagement in political, economic, security and cultural areas with this region is noteworthy. On the other hand, South Asian countries are also very eager to build up smooth relationship with the second largest economy of the present world, China. In China’s foreign policy, this region is in priority list and on the other side South Asian countries could always play the China card to evade India’s influence. Second, India’s new South Asia policy with the focus on trade and connectivity has improved regional cooperation since 1991. But China remains an economically more attractive and politically more reliable partner for India’s neighbours. Current strategic and economic trends indicate that South Asia’s importance in China’s national security calculus is likely to increase in the twenty-first century. A Sino-Indian rivalry in southern Asia and the northern Indian Ocean may well be a dominant feature of future Asian geopolitics.

THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

One of the major changing pattern of Chinese foreign policy is Beijing’s changing political, strategic and economic linkages with the developed and the developing world, especially with South Asia. As the father of neorealism Kennth Waltz explained, ‘Unipolarity appears as the least stable of international confugurations. Unlikely though it is, a dominat power may behave modernnization, restraint and forbearance.’ Even if it does, however weaker state will worry about the future behaviour faced by unbalaced power, states try to increase their own strength or they ally with others to bring the international distribution of power into balance.1 Tom & Gray (1995) argue that power balancing in Asia is the key to stability, with regionl security regimes not yet likely to be effective.2 On the other side of the debate liberal and constructivist authors mainly focus on multilateral institutions. Constructivist Alexandar Wendt calls two increasingly accepted basic tanets of constructivism.3

In Realism Theory we find that, States therefore prioritize self-interest over collective interest and have to accumulate power in order to survive. Such thinking drives states to attain a favourable balance of power and compete for influence. Balancing can consist of internal balancing building up one’s own power, or external balancing while accumulating power through external relations.

In Liberalism Theory we find the friendship and cooperation between states and here in China-South Asia relations these are obvious. China’s national role in South Asian region is mostly based on cooperation and understanding. Another important element of Liberalism is freedom and in this relationship, we can see this. Both China and South Asian countries are enjoying their economic, political, cultural, military relations by their own choices not by any external pressure. So, in China-South Asia context realism and liberalism are found most.

HISTORICAL OVERVIEW OF CHINA-SOUTH ASIA

The interactions between China and South Asia could date back to third century but before the mid of twentieth century. During that time there were only cultural and religion interactions between them. Political connection started only after British subcontinent and China recovered independence in late of 1940s. China and South Asia failed to establish comprehensive and mutual benefits relationships before twenty-first century because their economic ties were very weak. China involved in a border conflict with India, the strongest country of South Asian region and on the other hand its friendly relation with Pakistan helped to keep the regional stability. Before twenty-first century, China’s foreign policy towards South Asia could be regarded as single dimensional and limited strategy. Only in twenty-first century, the relationship of China and South Asia has been significantly changed and China very positively participated the regional issues for the first time in history. In this consequence, China’s South Asia policy was redefined. The relationships between two sides were enlarged, deepened and reorganized. Then many important and positive phenomena have appeared among their relations, also some negative factors emerged and some traditional obstacles remain.4 During the cold war the intension of China’s policy towards South Asia was to secure its western part of territory and avoid the clash with the regional power in South Asia. Furthermore, given India’s hegemonic ambitions in South Asia and China’s principled opposition to hegemony, the other countries of South Asia have developed good relations with China as a means of restraint on India. However, China is a crucial external partner of South Asia and would play more and more important role on the stage of South Asia. China’s diplomatic relations started with the South Asian region since 1950s, when India and Pakistan made this relation with China after very short time of the country’s independence. In the twenty-first century China fundamentally has changed its South Asian policy like it has been conducting multidimensional cooperation with all the South Asian countries. Now, the country’s aim doesn’t limit it in the security issue any longer, China desires to upgrade its relationship with South Asia in all fields. In the beginning of this century, China was greatly involved in this region’s issues and clearly showed its intention and capability. At present China’s dominant role in South Asia is an important issue in international politics and gradually this national role of China in the areas of politics, economic development, security issues as well as cultural factors is increasing. China is playing a vital role in South Asian region as a very close neighbour and the members of this region are also enjoying a good relationship with this fastest growing economic giant of the world.

CHINA’S ROLE IN SOUTH ASIA

Traditionally, China preferred from the bilateral diplomacy to multi-lateral diplomacy. But, in the recent time, multilateral diplomacy is getting more and more important in Chinese foreign policy. After the great achievement gained by Chinese multilateral diplomacy in South East Asia, China began its attempt to participate in the integration progress of South Asia. Despite being geographically isolated by the Himalayan Mountain range from South Asia, China’s role in South Asia is very significant.5 China shares a common border with five South Asian nations (Afghanistan, India, Pakistan, Nepal and Bhutan) while the other three (Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Maldives) do not share borders with China. Nepal and Bhutan are situated along the Himalayan range and act as the buffer states between China and India. Though China had a long history of border tensions with India, the cross-border investment and trade continue to take precedence over past security conflicts. China is taking bolder steps to strengthen its position vis-à-vis the United States across Asia in general and South Asia in particular. Chinese involvement in South Asia is mostly focused on the two largest countries of the subcontinent, India and Pakistan. But China’s role in other countries of this region is also very remarkable mostly in economic, political, security and cultural areas.

However, with reference to the changing Chinese strategies, Lawrence Saez writes that Chinese policy toward South Asia has shifted subtly and may be experimenting with and pursuing a variety of strategic options like balancing multilateralism with peripheral stability and the pursuance of other security objectives (such as energy security). He further writes that with respect to South Asia, China is pursuing a policy of reluctant competition with India; contingent cooperation with Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka; and secretive cooptation with Nepal and Maldives.6 Pursuing this strategy at regional level, China has joined the South Asian Association of Regional Cooperation (SAARC) in 2005 and has been participating in all SAARC Summits regularly after it got observer’s status.

CHINA’S ECONOMIC ROLE IN SOUTH ASIA

China has world’s largest population and it is the second largest economy of the world.7 The country’s rise has been treated as one of the greatest events of the last century. It is one of the enigmas of the twenty-first century. Since the adoption of economic reforms and opening-up policy by Deng Xiaoping after 1978, China’s economy has been growing at a tremendous rate.8 China’s rise as an economic and trading power has been impressive on many levels.

However, China’s economic expansion in South Asian countries is increasing day by day and at present several mega projects connect these countries with China greatly. Though China’s economic engagement in this region is later then its political and cultural connection but its economic role in South Asia will be beneficial for the progress and consistency of this region. China’s economic growth will give much space to South Asian goods to Chinese markets. China’s policy towards this region can be understood through its periphery strategy which focuses on different changing atmosphere and on progress in bordering areas.9 Due to two reasons, South Asia is important for China. Firstly, China wants stability within state along with peace in bordering areas. Secondly, China’s interaction with neighbouring countries is necessary to enhance trade. South Asia has sufficient space for Chinese goods and its economic role was actively observed in this region in 2008 after international economic recession as compared to the past.10

From Beijing’s perspective, the most important nation in the South Asian region is India. India and China are the two most populous nations on earth. Indeed, India is expected to surpass China sometime in the 2020s. Sino-Indian relations have often been rocky. While the two states enjoyed relatively peaceful relations for the few years after the founding of the People’s Republic in 1949, the situation rapidly deteriorated and relations between the two powers have often been frosty. Several factors have played into this, but perhaps the most important factor was and is the border issue between the two countries. The precise demarcation of the border between China and India remains a contentious issue for both sides.11 Despite this India and China resumed trade in 1978 and in this consequence both countries signed the Most Favoured Nation (MFN) in 1984. In 2008, both countries bilateral trade reached US$ 51.8 billion and has made China as India’s largest trading partner in goods replacing the United States of America. But in 2009, because of the world economic downturn, bilateral trade dropped into US$ 43.27 billion and again in 2010 is reached at US$ 61.74 billion. These ups and downs happen as same as in their bilateral trade for last few years also. Commodity-wise, bilateral trade was dominated by reactors, boilers, machinery, electric machinery, sound equipment’s, organic chemicals, ores, cotton, etc. China’s investment in India is also noteworthy. Economically, China is also a more attractive partner for South Asian countries than India. Though, there are lots of problems in Sino-India bilateral relations but Sino-Indian trade is increasing day by day. In 2017 India export to China $12,495,230,459 and India import from China $71,922,747,817).12

The massive Chinese investment in India’s neighbourhood in the context of its ‘One Belt One Road’ (OBOR) Initiative will increase Beijing’s influence in South Asia. China has also expanded its trade relations and has surpassed India in some cases. Even in India, China has emerged as a significant economic factor. In the regional context, both countries are cooperating on initiatives like the Bangladesh-China-India-Myanmar (BCIM) economic corridor and China has also promised to make large scale infrastructure investment in India. Despite their bilateral problems and tensions from respective engagement in South Asia, India and China have also increased their collaboration on the global level as well as multilateral perspective like Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO), New Development Bank (NDA), Group 20 (G20) and the BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa).

Even in terms of China’s economic engagement, Pakistan remains China’s ‘biggest market’ for its capital investment in infrastructure projects; most of which remain of strategic importance if not directly all part of their defence collaborations. China-Pakistan economic cooperation began in 1979. China has become Pakistan’s largest arms supplier and second-largest trading partner. China invests in Pakistan’s infrastructural expansion through building of power plants, roads and communication nodes including the Pakistani deepwater port at Gwadar.13 Both countries are keen on strengthening the economic ties and promised to ‘propel’ cross-border trade. Pakistan has been granted free trade zone in China. Both countries signed a free trade agreement in 2008 and agreed to build a railway through the Karakoram.14 At present China’s Mega project with Pakistan is China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), which will develop Pakistan’s economy in a great range.

Chinese Economic role in Bangladesh is increasing in a remarkable way. At present China is the largest trading partner of Bangladesh and both countries are working together to promote the bilateral economic relations and trade cooperation. The People’s Republic of China and the People’s Republic of Bangladesh officially established diplomatic relations on 4 October 1975. Since then, the friendly relations and cooperation between the two countries have been growing steadily which is termed as ‘trusted friendship’ or ‘all weather friendship’. China is the assistance provider of Bangladesh in the areas of communications, power and energy, infrastructure development, etc. On the other hand, China is the biggest market for Bangladeshi products such as jute, textiles, leather, footwear, tobacco, etc. Chinese investment in Bangladesh is very important for Bangladesh and its rising. Chinese FDI comes to Bangladesh more in textile and China has supported Bangladesh with investments in projects and infrastructure. China’s ‘One Belt One Road’ initiative will increase opportunities for Bangladesh to connect in the economic prosperity. China also considers Bangladesh as an important part and BCIM (Bangladesh-China-India-Myanmar) is very significant for both countries. China’s role in the economic development of Bangladesh is noteworthy BCIM can become a way for Bangladesh and India to relate to a pan-Asian market.

In Nepal’s development endeavour, China has always been a steady and reliable partner more specifically in the areas of infrastructure and human resources development, education, health and food assistance among others. The first Agreement between China and Nepal on ‘Economic Aid’ was signed in 1956.15 In the year 1982 both countries established Inter-Governmental Economic and Trade Committee which focuses on bilateral economic and technical cooperation. China’s economic role in Nepal is increasing in the twenty-first century as their trade volume is growing. China is the topmost investor in Nepal and Chinese investment is growing as compared with the past. Regarding development in Nepal, the regular meetings of Inter-Governmental Economic and Trade Committee would be helpful for coordinated overall interactions. The present status of Nepal’s trade with China is good under Xi Jinping, as in 2017 the total trade volume between two states was approximately US$ 1.267 billion. Nepal is also connected with China’s mega project ‘OBOR’ initiative, which will create more opportunities for Nepal economic development and will improve the economic tie.

Sri Lanka and China have had historical links that can be traced back from several centuries. China’s economic role in Sri Lanka is very significant as China supports in various development projects such as Hambantota Port, Colombo South Harbour, Mattala International Airport16, etc., and these large-scale investments have the potential to transform economic opportunities, including export development and promote reconciliation through better economic integration within the domestic economy. China also supports in the Norochcholai Power Plant and several Highway, Expressways projects. China is the largest trading partner of Sri Lanka within Asian region and Sri Lanka offered oil exploration block to China and China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC) started working on it.17 Both countries are enjoying a very friendly economic environment from both sides. Sri Lanka signed China’s ‘OBOR’ initiative. China’s economic rising in the world arena is benefiting Sri Lanka in various aspects.

China’s economic role in another South Asian country Bhutan is not a very remarkable one because Bhutan has been a strong ally of India and refrained from establishing relations with China for a long time. Another reason for this gap is the border disputes.18 But recently Bhutan opened its economy and Chinese companies have been given contract to construct the world’s tallest Buddha Statue in Thimpu. Now China is an attractive source of investment and exporting farming and telecommunication equipment, as well as investing in the projects related to health and education services.

The diplomatic relations between China and Maldives were first established in 1972 and since then relations have gradually developed. Bilateral trade between both countries is increasing. China has built Maldives’ Ministry of Foreign Affairs building and national Museum. However, China’s economic role in this country is increasing and China also provided loans to build the 1,000 housing projects which have served to further expand goodwill and cooperations.19

China’s economic role in Afghanistan is increasing because US presence in Afghanistan slowly diminishe and China has taken several steps to signal its interest to Kabul as an economic partner. In 2014, for example, China committed 2 billion yuan (327 million) in aid to Afghanistan through 2017, larger than a total of 1.5 billion yuan ($240 million) it had provided in over a decade between 2001 and 2013.20 China was one of the first countries to invest in mineral extraction in Afghanistan. Afghanistan is poised to benefit from and partner with China through the ‘OBOR’ initiative. With considerable human talent and financial resources attached to it, the ‘OBOR’ initiative has the potential to serve as an integral part of the Afghan Government’s Infrastructure and Connectivity Development and Private Sector Development National Priority Programs. China is also investing in different development projects in Afghanistan which will improve the county’s economic conditions.

CHINA’S GEOPOLITICAL IMPLICATIONS
IN SOUTH ASIA

Emphasis on Peace and Stability, China’s present political and diplomatic efforts in the region remain focused on easing tensions and maintaining peaceful periphery to ensure environment conducive to her sustained economic development. China remains an economically more attractive and politically more reliable partner for South Asian states then India. Given China’s current preoccupations with internal stability, security, modernization and consolidation of territorial limits and strategic space in the Hong Kong-Taiwan-South China Sea sector, it is keen to maintain stable and good relations with the South Asian states.

China and India straddle a common geopolitical space across the Himalayas and South, Southwest and Southeast Asia. This makes for strategic and geopolitical competition. The growth of these two major power centres adjacent to one another would increasingly be a major factor shaping Asia’s politico-security environment. Stable relations between these two powers is therefore not only in the interest of their peoples, but also in Asian and global interest. Building the basis for a stable and cooperative relationship is a challenge.

But there are several geopolitical tensions that remain between the two countries such as border disputes, Indian Ocean, etc. The increasing Chinese presence in the Indian Ocean area, therefore, is viewed with much suspicion by India. As Jawaharlal Nehru is reported to have said, ‘To be secure on land, we must be supreme at sea’.21 With a 5,000-mile coastline in the Indian Ocean, Indian strategists have often considered the Indian Ocean as its ‘private lake’.22

On the other hand, China’s relation with Pakistan developed over a long period since 1951, when both countries established diplomatic relations. Pakistan’s geopolitical importance to China or any other major powers is very significant as Pakistan located between China, South Asia, on the one hand and Central Asia and West Asia on the other and is at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. Pakistani port of Gwadar is very important for China because of its strategic involvement in the northern areas of Pakistan, Gilgit and Baltistan bordering China. Gwardar provides China the shortest route for its trade with Africa, Middle East and Europe. So, China is working in this port and is matter of concern for India.

Bangladesh is a nation of strategic importance not only to the South Asian region but to the larger geopolitical dynamics of Asia as a whole. Bangladesh enjoys a strategically advantageous location and its geostrategic location is increasingly embedding its relations with China in the international strategic context where the interest of foreign powers set the parameters within which it has to manoeuvre. In terms of its political spin-off, China’s growing posture of neutrality in South Asian Affairs has been cited as the most visible outcome that clearly favours New Delhi.23 Frequent high-level visits of China’s officials to South Asian countries and also from those countries to China are making the political tie between China and South Asia stronger and creating many opportunities for both sides.

CHINA’S SECURITY ROLE IN SOUTH ASIA

Chinese objectives in South Asia have been already beyond the traditional security issue. Its linkage with South Asian countries expanded to all fields including economic, communication, energy and the untraditional security cooperation. China’s rising military capabilities, especially its nuclear and missile build up, has direct security implications on its neighbours in Asia with whom it has sovereignty disputes, ethnic-related insecurities and tensions and geopolitical rivalries and some of whom Beijing sees as potential threats.24

Following its takeover of Tibet, China became an integral part of the Southern Asian geopolitical and strategic environment. It shares borders with Afghanistan, Pakistan (given its occupation of a portion of Jammu & Kashmir), India, Nepal, Bhutan and Myanmar. As neighbouring states, each is affected—some positively and some negatively—by China’s military power, posture, strategic thinking and evolving role. China thus shapes in a major way the regional balance of power and security environment. Nearly 90 per cent of China’s total arms transfers are targeted at Pakistan, Iran, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Thailand and Sri Lanka, all neighbours of India.25 China has also developed naval and defense ties with all the Indian Ocean littoral states, dotting the area between the Gulf and Southeast Asia.

On the other hand, land-locked Nepal and Bhutan which have close security relations with India but which would be directly affected by a dominant or a hostile China, by migrations from China as a result of political turmoil in Tibet and by a deterioration in Sino-Indian relations. China’s Security role in Afghanistan is also very important. China was a major actor in the Afghan civil war and a key supplier of small arms to the insurgents in the combined effort of United States and Pakistan to force a Soviet withdrawal from the country.

However, non-traditional security issues such as extremism, piracy, managing global commons, etc., are increasingly occupying larger space in strategic considerations. Three powers India, Pakistan and China determine the strategic profile of South Asian security, constitute a security triangle.

CULTURAL EXCHANGES BETWEEN
CHINA AND SOUTH ASIA

Cultural exchange or cultural cooperation is a large field of activities and requires analytical attention independently. China-South Asia cultural relations have tremendous potential to bloom and can redefine cultural cooperation to suit the imagination of a new world order. China has adopted a policy of scientific expansion and it will become the most innovative country.

In this consequence, every year Chinese government is offering fellowships and South Asian countries are getting a remarkable portion. In most of the South Asian countries there are Chinese cultural and language centres, Chinese food restaurants, etc. On the other hand, some South Asian states signed agreements on cultural cooperation with China such as Nepal signed it to enhance cultural exchanges. Nepal has been receiving volunteer teachers for Chinese language training and agriculture sector too. Like Nepal, some other countries like Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Maldives, Pakistan and even with India, China has cultural cooperations. Cultural cooperation between Sri Lanka and China is blossoming in sports, interactions, etc.

PROBLEMS AND FUTURE CHALLENGES OF
CHINA-SOUTH ASIA

The relationship between the South Asian countries and China raises several issues related to the old order and the emerging order. In South Asian region, China’s role is increasing very significantly but still there are some remarkable challenges which China need to work on for making itself as a regional power compete with India.

TRADITIONAL GEO-POLITICAL DISPUTES

Judging from the integration achievements gained, the process of integration of South Asia is quite slow and insufficient, sometimes even moves forward two steps but backward one step. It is not economic factor but the long-term rivalry between India and Pakistan hijacked not only the development and expansion of the SAARC, but also the integration process of that region and the other issues including the relations between external players with South Asian countries. The core issue of these two countries traditional geopolitics dispute is Kashmir and since 1998, the nuclear issue has intruded. Even though both sides repeated their good-willing for many times and launched many diplomatic steps to mitigate the situation and promote the bilateral relation, the tension remains and no one could expect it could be relaxed in near future. For any external partners, India and Pakistan dispute is the priority concern when making policy or taking actions towards this region.26 So, for China this issue is a big challenge in South Asian region.

EXTERNAL FACTORS IMPACT

In strategic terms, South Asia was never an important region for the United States of America during the last many years. But now things have changed. Since 1990s, the American South Asia policy transited from Balance Strategy to Beyond Balance Strategy. Alliance between USA and Pakistan, which is against partnership between Russia and India, was renounced by Washington step by step and India became the keystone of USA’s South Asia policy. India and USA never addressed any statement or officially admitted their cooperation is confronting China, but in fact, it’s very obviously that ‘the strongest democracy country’ is seeking to ally with ‘the most populated democracy country’ to balance ‘the fastest growing economic entity in the world’. Of course, the cooperation between Washington and New Delhi also covers many other fields, including power security, communication, nonproliferation, etc. But managing China is a very important part.

TRADE DISPUTES

The increasing trade disputes, such as main complains from the local manufactures and anti-dumping investigations came after Chinese increasing commerce links with South Asia in recent years. China enjoyed huge favourable imbalance in all the bilateral trade relations between China and the rest of South Asian countries like Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal and Maldives. Bhutan is the only exception because there is no direct trade links with China. Although currently the trade conflict remains relative slight and could be controlled, it has already brought a certain negative effect to the relations between China and South Asia. Considering China would preserve the competitive edge in economic arena with South Asia smaller countries even in certain fields with India, it is not expected that the trade disputes would be resolved in short time.

FUTURE OPPORTUNITIES FOR CHINA

Rise of China, decline of America and shift of global power balance from west to the east, or from Europe to Asia or from Atlantic to the Asia-Pacific and Indian Ocean region are common references in most recent discussion on global power balance. China and South Asia between themselves can evolve into a power base for common good. There is only need for connectivity between the two people and civilizations. Greater interaction among the peoples of the two regions will have tremendous impact on exchange of ideas and intellect, of goods and services bringing the fruits of economic prosperity to each other’s doorstep. There are several opportunities for China to make its position stronger in South Asian region. China can Balance and Stabilize the Regional Security Environment in South Asia. It is in China’s interest to continue pursuing the objective of peace and stability in the region through balance of power and conflict resolution rather than strengthening the hegemonic position of the biggest regional country India. Balance of power in South Asia, does contribute towards China’s interests in South East Asia by curtailing India’s liberty of action.

On the other hand, China can promote regionalism in the South Asian region. China’s entry into the SAARC is likely to reinvigorate the forum and enhance regionalism in South Asia. China, due to its political clout may play an important role in fostering mutual trust, conflict resolution and promoting good neighbourly relations among regional states through SAARC plus one mechanism. Furthermore, China can integrate smaller regional states in economic development: To enhance long-term economic interdependence, South Asian states may be integrated in China’s economic development. Moreover, regional anti-terrorism cooperation will be more effective work for the whole South Asian region. United State of America (USA) and North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) seems to be failing stabilizing Afghanistan and curtailing spill-over effects of terrorism to other countries. Here, China can play an important role to stabilize this situation.

Energy is the highest demand for South Asian states. China may be an option for South Asian states to meet their energy needs. China also can help by sharing renewable energies technology like wind and solar power, etc., besides assisting them to explore their untapped mineral and hydropower resources. On the other sides, China can promote soft power image to South Asia for future development. Present level of youth exchange programmes, cultural, sporting and people-to-people interactions may be expanded. Enhanced interaction between universities, think-tanks, academic institutions, mass media and film and television industries are also likely to help China to occupy cognitive space in the region, mitigating the effects of negative propaganda of western media.

CONCLUSION

China’s increasing engagement at bilateral and multilateral forums in South Asia has surely increased its leveraging capacity and influence with most of South Asian countries. Especially, given the increasing global interest in South Asian affairs following the nuclear tests by India and Pakistan, China’s steady movement towards South Asian multilateralism also seems to be powered by its ambitions to emerge as the next global power. A powerful and prosperous India will also serve as the engine of change for the largest population concentration in the world in South Asia. What South Asia needs is not a containment of China but a realistic engagement strategy that takes the interests of all countries into account. That would involve enmeshing China in trade, investment and multilateral security ties. But it would also entail creating a new South Asian balance and supporting India and other states to secure their interests. Since the end of Cold War, China’s policy towards South Asia is undergoing changes both in theory and practice in line with changing global geopolitical environments. Despite China’s earnest desire to work in harmony with South Asian nations for common prosperity, mutual benefit and regional peace and stability, the policy faces many future challenges. Considering all things in conclusion it can be said that China’s national role in South Asia is increasing remarkably and in the international arena its importance is also increasing.
