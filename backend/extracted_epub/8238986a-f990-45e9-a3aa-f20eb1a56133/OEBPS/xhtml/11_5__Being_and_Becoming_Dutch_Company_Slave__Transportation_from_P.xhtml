CHAPTER 5

Being and Becoming Dutch
Company Slave: Transportation
from Pazhaverkadu to
Jakarta, 1621-1665

Slave trade and traffic to overseas countries developed on the Tamil coast after the advent of the Europeans. It is necessary and important to examine why the slave trade developed overseas, what circumstances prompted the presence and growth, how it was conducted, where the slaves were transported, who were involved in these activities and what was the role and policy of the state and the rulers. In historical research, data driven methodologies have gained ground. Hence integrating it would be valuable, and allow for new types of research questions and analyses.

Pazhaverkadu (called Pulicat) an entrepôt located on the Coromandel Coast developed long distance trade under the rulers of Vijayanagar. It had a direct international trade route with the ports of Melaka, Pasai and Pedir of Malay-Indonesian archipelago. Since several regional trade routes converged, it had the added advantage of commanding an entire sector of maritime commerce under the Portuguese where private merchants of Mylapore settled at Santhome and were very active in the nearby region.1

The Dutch East India Company (formed in 1602) and its traders first sailed and reached South East Asia. As the textiles of Coromandel were exchanged for pepper and spices, they came from the port of Banten to Pazhaverkadu in 1606. They began their negotiations with the local ruler seeking permission to trade but it broke off. They succeeded the next time, obtaining permission from Venkatapatidevaraya II (1586-1612) the ruler of Vijayanagar in May 1610. He allowed them for two years and it was stipulated that a tax of 2 per cent had to be paid on all imports and exports. Hans Marcelis, the Dutch chief, resided at Pazhaverkadu with four of his employees.2 He purchased and exported a variety of textile goods to Jakarta in Indonesia which served as the Dutch commercial headquarters in Asia.3 The Dutch Company gradually incorporated the purchase of slaves into its maritime trading network. It is mentioned in the documents that slave trade from Pazhaverkadu was more profitable than the cloth trade.4 Capital for investment in slave purchases had been found inadequate as was reported.5 This article therefore looks into the slave trading activities of the Dutch Company in Pazhaverkadu and its links with Jakarta. It is hoped that such an exercise in statistics would enable one to revisit to the old and fascinating history from a different perspective shedding new light on the topic. The study is based chiefly on the statistics culled from the primary sources, both manuscripts and printed documents in Dutch language.

Tapan Raychaudhuri was the first to mention the Dutch slave trade in Coromandel in his work published in 1962.6 Sanjay Subrahmanyam in his work in 1990 had failed to assign importance to Dutch trade in slaves at Pulicat.7 Scholars who have studied in general about the Dutch slave trade have not laid emphasis on geographical origin of slaves. Such pointers are important within the vast trading ocean to note the specificities of individual coastal zones in slave trade which contrasted with each other.8

It is well reported in the Dutch records that the first and the earliest shipment of slaves of 124 boys and girls from Masulipatnam had arrived on the ship Gouden Leeuw at Jakarta in 1622.9 This clearly points to the fact of the geographical origins of slaves in addition to the Telugu-speaking linguistic group. Hence in this study, one has carefully excluded the Telugu, Oriya and Bengali-speaking slaves purchased and brought from Maulipatnam, Pipili and Arakan to Pazhaverkadu for shipping to Jakarta that had been recorded in the documents. It is highly necessary to treat this subject separately. Only the purchase of slaves at Pazhaverkadu port and its immediate hinterland including places like Ponneri, Gummidipoondi, Aarampakkam, etc., has been included. The Dutch East India Company was directly involved in the slave trade at Pazhaverkadu. The records mention only the company slaves and not the slaves owned by various individuals. The company slaves after purchase were branded on the right thigh or arm with the logo (VOC) of the Dutch Company. This was done to make clear distinction from those owned by privateers.

The Dutch Company purchased slaves and it is reported that both the sale deed and the purchase deeds were executed. The sale must have been conducted through public auction which was the practice in Tamil country as the native records mention. Price was fixed at that time and payment was immediately made. The names of the writer of the deed and the witnesses present were also recorded. The native agents and brokers were doing the business and they assisted the Dutch. These slaves who were bought, were lodged in the Dutch trading factory. They were fed and waited for transport in Dutch ships to Jakarta. They were obtained mainly to serve the Dutch Company needs owing to labour scarcity that prevailed in Jakarta.

It had been ordered from Jakarta in September 1628 that slaves as old as some of those sent in the previous years should not be purchased and transported. According to Dutch records of Pazhaverkadu slave trade was not very regular. The Dutch had not exported slaves during the years 1618-20, 1626, 1632-3, 1635, 1638-9, 1649-50 and 1652-9.

It had also been said in December 1630 that the Dutch in Pazhaverkadu purchased slaves lest they starve to death. Therefore the ship Hoorn sailed to Jakarta with 100 slaves. In 1634 it was stated that there were only few slaves who could be purchased in Pazhaverkadu due to the exceedingly high mortality rate among the native population. The seven slaves purchased at that time were far more expensive than those bought in the previous years.13

The Dutch Company servants in Pazhaverkadu were instructed in June 1635 not to buy many women and girls in the near future. Further, in November 1635 they were also instructed to purchase in the ratio of one female slave for every two male slaves.

Dutch records report that the slave voyages from Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta normally took the duration of one month. In 1636 it had been stated that owing to adverse weather conditions, the voyage of Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta with slaves took over three months (3 July–9 October 1636). Four Tamil slaves died en route in addition to 26 Dutchmen. Arent Gardenijs in his letter of 14 February 1643 wrote that a storm in Pulicat had delayed the departure of the ship carrying slaves for some days.

The Dutch in Pazhaverkadu fought naval battles against the Portuguese who were rivals in trade. When ships were seized, those on board were made captives and enslaved. These slaves captured by the Dutch Company were also transported in Dutch ships from Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta.

It is mentioned that many slaves died during the voyages from Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta. Those who died on the voyage were thrown into the sea. In 1624 the vessels for making kanji (rice-gruel) were in poor shape due to heavy use and so the slaves were fed with half-cooked rice. As a result 45 slaves died during the voyage.23 One finds 572 slaves did not survive in the voyage from Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta during May-December 1646 because of a pestilential disease of bubonic plague.24 Again 137 slaves, who had been transported in ships from Pazhaverkadu to Jakarta in 1647 died. It is sad to note that 293 purchased slaves died in Pazhaverkadu before transportation to Jakarta.25 Thus in total 430 slaves had died in that year incurring a heavy financial loss to the Dutch Company.

The Dutch records just furnish the classification of company slaves such as men, women, boys, girls and infants. In some cases the total number of slaves alone had been stated. The price paid for the slave had been sometimes recorded. According to records the price of one slave ranged during famine period between 18 reals and 20 reals (1 real = ten panams). However during other times it was high and the price varied between 27 Dutch guilders to 40 Dutch guilders. During the famine, the price of one slave fell as low as 4 Dutch guilders.26 The records do not speak of the age of the slave, the caste and the name of the agents or brokers who sold them.

In short, the Dutch in Pazhaverkadu had purchased 10,470 slaves in and around the port for export to Jakarta between 1621 and 1665. The death of slaves transported was 1,086. Hence 9,384 slaves had reached Jakarta and they were employed in pepper and nutmeg gardens, animal husbandry and fortification works.27 Slaves from Pazhaverkadu were also re-exported to Amboina and Banda and did various jobs assigned. The Hindu rulers of Tamil country had banned the export and transport of slaves to countries overseas. The Dutch did not respect the orders issued by the native rulers. No one seems to have bothered about the condition of the slaves and this is the historical truth. The Dutch purchased slaves secretly with the connivance of many native intermediaries and indulged in unlawful human trade and traffic. The slave trade in Pazhaverkadu declined during 1658-69 because the Dutch had brought plenty of labourers from China to Jakarta. Further, slaves from Makassar, Bali, Timor and Kalimantan were brought to Jakarta to meet the labour demand.
